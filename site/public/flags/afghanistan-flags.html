Flags of Afghanistan

Afghanistan sits at one of the great crossroads of Asia — a corridor fought over and ruled through by Persian, Greek, and Islamic empires for two thousand years before anything resembling a unified Afghan state existed. That state is usually dated to a single afternoon in 1747, when Ahmad Shah Durrani was elected ruler at a loya jirga (grand council) in Kandahar and founded the Durrani Empire. From there the record runs through the nineteenth-century "Great Game" between British India and Russia, three Anglo-Afghan wars, a long twentieth-century monarchy, a 1973 republican coup, a 1978 communist revolution, a decade of Soviet military intervention, factional civil war, two periods of Taliban rule separated by two decades of internationally backed republic — and, unusually for the region, a country that was never formally colonized.

Flags here track political authority, not culture or ethnicity: nine entries span roughly two and a half millennia, with the first three deliberately compressed because no single Afghan flag can honestly be attributed to most of the pre-1747 era.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Afghanistan, c. 550 BCE–present. Because Afghanistan was ruled successively by outside empires for most of its history before 1747, with no continuous local polity to track, the ancient and medieval span is compressed into broad entries rather than given false year-by-year precision. Deeper coverage of the Achaemenid Persian, Hellenistic/Greco-Bactrian, and later regional empires that passed through this territory lives on their own pages in this atlas — see Persian Empire, Mongol Empire, and Ottoman Empire (the latter two never ruled Afghan territory directly but are referenced for regional context). This page is scoped to flags and polities specific to the Afghan lands themselves. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Afghanistan has never had a well-documented internal partition of the kind this atlas tracks elsewhere. Regional strongmen and provincial warlords have repeatedly contested control from the center, but no two rival governments each held a fixed, mapped half of the country the way Hungary or Poland once did — so this page shows one border and one ruling government at a time.

Occupied/client stateHamid KarzaiIslam (constitutional state religion)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: US & NATO forces (2001–2021)Immigration: refugee repatriation from Pakistan & Iran

Who & why: following the September 2001 attacks, a US-led coalition invades in October 2001 to remove the Taliban from power for sheltering al-Qaeda. An interim administration under Hamid Karzai is installed via the December 2001 Bonn Agreement; a new constitution is ratified in January 2004 establishing the Islamic Republic of Afghanistan, with Karzai (to 2014) and then Ashraf Ghani (to 2021) as president, backed throughout by an international military and civilian presence.

Who & why: the Taliban, having emerged from the chaos of the post-Soviet civil war, capture Kabul on 27 September 1996 and declare the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan under Mullah Mohammed Omar. Who & why: the communist government under President Najibullah, deprived of Soviet support after the 1989 withdrawal, falls on 28 April 1992 as mujahideen factions enter Kabul. A coalition government declares the Islamic State of Afghanistan, but rival mujahideen factions (led by figures including Burhanuddin Rabbani, Gulbuddin Hekmatyar, and Ahmad Shah Massoud) fight one another for control of Kabul and the country through the mid-1990s, a period of severe factional civil war that ends only with the Taliban's 1996 capture of the capital.

Democratic Republic of Afghanistan (PDPA / Soviet-backed)

Communist / Soviet-alignedNur Muhammad TarakiSecular (Marxist-Leninist, no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Soviet Union (1979–1989)Emigration: mass exodus to Pakistan & Iran

Who & why: the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan (PDPA) seizes power in the 27 April 1978 Saur Revolution, killing President Daoud Khan and ending the five-year republic. Internal PDPA factional violence and rural resistance prompt the Soviet Union to intervene militarily in December 1979, installing Babrak Karmal and beginning the Soviet-Afghan War, fought against US- and Pakistan-backed mujahideen through the 1980s. Soviet forces withdraw in February 1989; the communist government, now under Mohammad Najibullah, survives without direct Soviet troops until it falls in April 1992 as post-Soviet Russian support ends.

What it represents: a plain red flag (from 1980) carrying a gold state emblem in the upper hoist, replacing an earlier, briefer 1978 tricolor; the all-red field aligned Afghanistan visually with other Soviet-bloc states during this period.

Republic of Afghanistan (Daoud Khan)

RepublicMohammed Daoud KhanIslam (state religion)

Who & why: Mohammed Daoud Khan, the king's cousin and former prime minister, overthrows King Zahir Shah in a bloodless palace coup on 17 July 1973 while the king is abroad, ending the roughly two-century Durrani/Barakzai monarchy and declaring the Republic of Afghanistan with himself as president. Daoud's increasingly authoritarian rule and break with his former leftist allies contribute to his overthrow and death in the 1978 Saur Revolution.

What it represents: the black-red-green vertical tricolor is retained from the late monarchy but recentered on a new republican eagle-form emblem, marking continuity of the national colors across the change in government.

Kingdom of Afghanistan (Barakzai dynasty, 20th-century monarchy)

Kingdom, independentAmanullah KhanSunni Islam (Hanafi, state religion)
Ruling house:Barakzai dynasty

Who & why: King Amanullah Khan takes the throne in February 1919 and immediately fights the Third Anglo-Afghan War (May–August 1919); the resulting August 1919 Treaty of Rawalpindi secures Afghanistan full control of its own foreign affairs, formally completing its independence from British influence over external relations. Amanullah pursues sweeping modernizing reforms through the 1920s before abdicating amid rebellion in 1929. After a brief interregnum, the Barakzai line continues under Nadir Shah (1929–1933) and then his son Zahir Shah, whose long, largely stable reign from 1933 to 1973 ends when Daoud Khan's coup abolishes the monarchy.

What it represents: the black-red-green vertical tricolor, adopted in various emblem forms across this period (colors reportedly linked to the country's Islamic, Durrani royal, and reform-era history), centers a changing royal emblem — typically a mosque flanked by sheaves of wheat — that is redrawn several times as reigns and constitutions change but the tricolor itself endures.

Durrani Empire and successor Emirate of Afghanistan

Kingdom, independentAhmad Shah DurraniSunni Islam (Hanafi, state religion)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: British forces (Anglo-Afghan Wars, temporary)
Ruling house:Durrani dynasty (Sadozai)Barakzai dynasty

Who & why: Ahmad Shah Durrani, an officer in the service of the Persian conqueror Nader Shah, is elected ruler at a loya jirga in Kandahar in 1747 following Nader Shah's assassination — the event conventionally taken as the founding of the modern Afghan state. His Durrani Empire at its height stretches from eastern Persia to northern India. Through the 19th century the empire fragments and reconsolidates as the Emirate of Afghanistan, becoming the central battleground of the British-Russian "Great Game": Britain fights the First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–1842, ending in a disastrous British retreat from Kabul) and the Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–1880, after which Britain controls Afghan foreign policy while the emirate keeps internal self-rule). Afghanistan is never formally colonized, functioning instead as a buffer state between British India and Russian Central Asia, until full control of foreign affairs is regained in 1919.

What it represents: a plain black field was long used as the Durrani dynastic and later emirate standard, at various points bearing a central white emblem; documentation of exact emblem designs is inconsistent across this long span, so the field color (attested more reliably) is emphasized here over precise emblem detail.

Ancient and medieval crossroads: successive outside empires

Various empires, no continuous local flagNo single ruler documentedVaried by era (Zoroastrianism, Buddhism, then Islam from 7th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Arab Caliphate (7th–8th c.)Conquered by: Mongol Empire (1220s)

Who & why: for roughly two thousand years, Afghan territory is ruled successively rather than continuously by outside powers, with no single local polity to track. The Achaemenid Persian Empire holds the region from around 550 BCE; Alexander the Great conquers it in the 330s BCE, and Hellenistic successor states — the Seleucids and then the Greco-Bactrian kingdom — rule parts of it for roughly two centuries after. Later centuries bring Kushan, Sasanian Persian, and Hephthalite (Hunnic) rule, then a long Islamic era beginning with 7th–8th century Arab conquests and continuing through the Ghaznavid (10th–12th century, based at Ghazni), Ghurid (12th–13th century), Mongol/Ilkhanid, and Timurid (14th–16th century, based at Herat) dynasties, with the region later contested between the Safavid and Mughal empires before Nader Shah's Afsharid Persian conquest in the 1730s. This entry closes in 1747 with Nader Shah's assassination and Ahmad Shah Durrani's election at Kandahar.

What it represents: no flag can be honestly attributed to a continuous "Afghanistan" across this span, since no such continuous polity existed; standards and banners of the Achaemenid, Hellenistic, and various Islamic dynasties that passed through this territory are covered on their own pages in this atlas where documented (see the Persian Empire page for the Achaemenid and Sasanian/Afsharid periods). Shown here honestly blank rather than assigning a single invented banner to two thousand years of turnover.
