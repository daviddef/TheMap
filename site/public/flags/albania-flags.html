Flags of the Land of the Eagle

Every Albanian flag since 1912 traces back to one man's personal banner: Gjergj Kastrioti, "Skanderbeg," flew a red flag with a black double-headed eagle while holding the mountains against the Ottomans for twenty-five years in the 1400s. It disappeared for four and a half centuries of Ottoman rule, then came back exactly as it had been the moment independence was declared — the only change in over a century has been what, if anything, sits above the eagle's heads: nothing, a king's helmet, a fascist bundle of rods, a Communist star, and finally nothing again.

Albania spent its interwar decade in near-constant flux — a republic, a monarchy, an Italian personal union, a German puppet state — and then, uniquely in this atlas, forty-five years sealed off from nearly the entire world under Enver Hoxha, the last and longest-lasting Stalinist government in Europe.

Long before any Albanian polity existed, independent Illyrian tribal kingdoms — Queen Teuta's among them — held this Adriatic coast until Rome's Illyrian Wars folded it into the empire in 168 BCE; thirteen centuries of Roman and Byzantine provincial rule followed before the Principality of Arbanon emerges around 1190 as the first attested medieval Albanian state.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Albania, c. 400 BCE–present, thirteen political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

The 1939–44 occupation is not an internal split. Italy annexed the whole country in 1939, and Germany occupied the whole country in 1943 — one power replaced another across all of Albania at once, not two powers ruling different halves simultaneously the way Habsburg and Ottoman Hungary did. The chaotic 1914–25 decade came closest to a real division, with different zones briefly under different foreign garrisons, but no line held long enough or cleanly enough to trace honestly as a lasting internal border.

1991 – present

Republic of Albania

Sovereign stateRamiz AliaSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: mass exodus to Greece & Italy (1990s–)Immigration: Kosovar refugees (1999)

Who & why: the Party of Labour's reformers, under pressure from student protests and the collapse of Communism elsewhere in Europe, permit multi-party elections in March 1991; parliament formally renames the state the Republic of Albania on 29 April 1991, though the old guard's Socialist Party (the renamed Communists) briefly wins that first vote before losing decisively to the Democratic Party in 1992.

What it represents: the Communist-era star is dropped, leaving the plain red-and-black eagle flag — the same design flown at the 1912 Vlorë proclamation, restored now for the fourth time in the century. Albania joined NATO in 2009 and has been an EU candidate since 2014.

1946 – 1991

People's Socialist Republic of Albania

Communist stateEnver HoxhaState atheism

Who & why: Enver Hoxha's Communist-led Democratic Front wins a single-list election and proclaims the People's Republic on 11 January 1946; renamed the People's Socialist Republic under the 1976 constitution. Hoxha broke with the Soviet Union in 1961 and China in 1978, leaving Albania the most isolated state in Europe until his death in 1985 and the regime's collapse by 1991.

What it represents: the historic eagle is kept, but a red, gold-bordered five-pointed star is added above the two heads — the standard Communist addition to a national emblem, removed the moment the single-party state ended.

1944 – 1946

Democratic Government of Albania

Provisional / transitional governmentEnver HoxhaNo official state religion

Who & why: Hoxha's Communist-led National Liberation Movement drives the last German forces out and enters Tirana on 29 November 1944, forming a provisional government under the wartime Anti-Fascist Council. It rules for just over a year — jailing or executing rival wartime resistance leaders and royalist claimants — before the rigged December 1945 election clears the way for the formal republic proclaimed the following month.

What it represents: the traditional eagle flag with a plain red star, outlined in black rather than gold, added by the Partisans during the war — the gold border that would mark the settled Communist state's own flag was only fixed once the People's Republic was formally proclaimed in 1946.

1943 – 1944

German-occupied Albania

Fascist / puppet stateMehdi FrashëriNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Nazi Germany (1943–44)

Who & why: after Italy's armistice with the Allies on 8 September 1943, German forces move in within days and install a nominally independent government under a Regency Council, formally dissolving Zog's kingdom and the union with Italy. Communist and nationalist Partisan resistance intensifies through 1944, ending German control when Tirana falls on 29 November 1944.

What it represents: the occupiers restore the plain pre-1939 eagle flag, without Zog's royal helmet or Italy's fasces, as a legitimizing gesture toward an Albania nominally independent of both former patrons — a bare continuity flag for a state with no real sovereignty.

1939 – 1943

Albania under the Italian Crown

Occupied territory (personal union)Victor Emmanuel IIINo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Fascist Italy (1939–43)

Who & why: Italy invades on 7 April 1939; King Zog flees into exile within days, and the Albanian parliament offers the crown to Victor Emmanuel III of Italy, who accepts it on 16 April — a genuine personal union, distinct from most WWII occupations, with Rome appointing a viceroy to govern in the king-emperor's name. The union ends with Italy's armistice in September 1943.

What it represents: the historic eagle flag is kept, but a small fasces — the bundle of rods and axe that was Fascist Italy's own state emblem — is added beneath it, a visible mark of subordination to Rome even as the traditional Albanian symbol stays formally in place.

1928 – 1939

Kingdom of Albania (Zog I)

KingdomZog I (Ahmet Zogu)No official state religion
Ruling house:House of Zogu

Who & why: President Ahmet Zogu has himself proclaimed King Zog I on 1 September 1928, keeping most republican institutions in form while ruling as an increasingly conservative, land-owning autocrat closely tied to Italian loans and military advisers — a dependency that leaves the kingdom exposed when Mussolini decides invasion is cheaper than continued subsidy.

What it represents: the eagle is crowned with a version of Skanderbeg's own goat-horned helmet — a deliberate visual claim of continuity between the new monarchy and Albania's 15th-century national hero, the same device later revived again on the republic's coat of arms.

1925 – 1928

Albanian Republic

RepublicAhmet ZoguNo official state religion

Who & why: Ahmet Zogu, who had seized power at the head of a Yugoslav-backed force in December 1924 after ousting Bishop Fan Noli's brief reformist government, has the constituent assembly declare a republic on 21 January 1925 and installs himself as its first president, ruling with near-dictatorial authority even under a nominally democratic form.

What it represents: the plain red-and-black eagle flag, without any monarchical device — a republican assertion that lasts barely three years before Zogu's own ambitions convert the office into a crown.

1914 – 1925

Contested Sovereignty (Competing Governments & Occupation)

Contested / provisionalNo single ruler documentedNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: rotating WWI powers (1914–18)

Who & why: Prince Wied's departure in September 1914 leaves no settled government as World War I engulfs the region — Austro-Hungarian, Italian, Serbian, Montenegrin, French, and Greek forces all occupy different parts of the country at different points through 1918, and a short-lived autonomous Republic of Korçë (1916–20, under French protection) and a separate Vlorë-based government briefly govern fragments of it. The December 1920 Congress of Lushnjë re-establishes a national government with Tirana as capital, but coups and countercoups — including Fan Noli's brief 1924 reformist government — keep central authority unsettled until Zogu's return that December.

What it represents: no new flag emerges across this decade of upheaval — every faction and occupying force that claimed to speak for Albania continued using or tolerating the same 1912 eagle flag, shown here with a broken border to mark a sovereignty real in symbol only.

1912 – 1914

Principality of Albania

PrincipalityIsmail QemaliNo official state religion

Who & why: Ismail Qemali proclaims independence from the crumbling Ottoman Empire at Vlorë on 28 November 1912, during the First Balkan War, and the Great Powers recognize the new state at the 1913 Conference of London. Prince Wilhelm of Wied, a German aristocrat chosen as a neutral compromise candidate, arrives as the internationally recognized monarch in March 1914 but flees just six months later as a peasant revolt and the outbreak of World War I make the country ungovernable.

What it represents: Qemali raises the same red flag with a black double-headed eagle that Skanderbeg had flown four and a half centuries earlier — a deliberate revival, not an invention, making this the flag every subsequent Albanian government has kept some version of ever since.

1479 – 1912

Ottoman Rule (Scutari, Janina & Kosovo Vilayets)

Occupied / annexed territoryMehmed IISunni Islam (Ottoman)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Ottoman Empire (1479)Emigration: Arbëresh flight to southern Italy

Who & why: organized resistance collapses with the fall of Shkodër in 1479, eleven years after Skanderbeg's death; Ottoman administration eventually splits the Albanian-inhabited lands mainly across the Scutari (Shkodër), Janina, and Kosovo vilayets, with significant local autonomy under semi-independent pashaliks, most famously Ali Pasha of Janina's effectively self-ruled domain from the 1780s to 1822. Ottoman rule ends with the First Balkan War in 1912.

What it represents: the standard Ottoman red flag with white crescent and star — provincial and pashalik identity was expressed through local seals and, in Ali Pasha's case, his own personal banners, not a distinct Albanian regional flag.

1444 – 1479

League of Lezhë (Skanderbeg's Resistance)

Resistance leagueSkanderbeg (Gjergj Kastrioti)Roman Catholic (League's coalition)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: early Arbëresh flight to Italy (from 1468)
Ruling house:House of Kastrioti

Who & why: Gjergj Kastrioti, an Albanian nobleman who had served the Ottomans before defecting, unites the region's rival lords at the League of Lezhë in March 1444 and holds the mountainous interior against repeated Ottoman invasions for twenty-four years, becoming known to Europe as Skanderbeg. His death in January 1468 removes the coalition's only unifying figure, and organized resistance finally collapses with Shkodër's fall in 1479.

What it represents: contemporary and near-contemporary sources, including the 16th-century chronicler Marin Barleti, attribute to Skanderbeg a red flag bearing a black double-headed eagle, topped by his own distinctive goat-horned helmet crest — a real, attested banner, unlike the placeholder eras before it, and the direct ancestor of the flag Albania still flies today.

c. 1190 – 1444

Medieval Albanian Principalities

Fragmented principalitiesProgon (founder of Arbanon)No single established religion
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Venetian coastal enclaves
Ruling house:House of Kastrioti

Who & why: the Principality of Arbanon, the first attested medieval Albanian polity, emerges around 1190 under the Progoni family as Byzantine central authority weakens; the Angevin Kingdom of Albania, a Naples-ruled coastal client state, follows from 1272 to 1368. Across the 14th and early 15th centuries, power fragments among competing noble houses — the Balshaj, Topia, Muzaka, Dukagjini, and Kastrioti families among them — ruling separate, often rival domains under nominal Byzantine, Serbian, Angevin, or Venetian overlordship until Skanderbeg unites several of them at Lezhë in 1444.

What it represents: no unified flag covered this two-and-a-half-century span of overlapping and rival lordships — each noble family used its own dynastic seal or coat of arms, not a shared national banner, and no single device can honestly stand in for the whole fragmented era. Shown blank rather than invented, in the same spirit as the equivalent placeholder eras elsewhere in this atlas.

c. 400 BCE – c. 1190 CE

Illyrian Kingdoms & Roman/Byzantine Illyricum

Fragmented tribal kingdoms, then Roman/Byzantine provinceTeutaIllyrian paganism, then Christianity (4th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Rome (168 BCE)Immigration: Slavic peoples (6th–7th c.)

Who & why: before any Albanian polity existed, the territory is home to independent Illyrian tribal kingdoms — the Ardiaei under kings like Bardyllis, Agron, and his widow Teuta contest control of the Adriatic coast until Rome, alarmed by Illyrian piracy, fights three Illyrian Wars between 229 and 168 BCE, capturing the last king, Gentius, and annexing the coast. Roman rule brings the province of Illyricum, later split into Dalmatia and Epirus Nova; Diocletian, a Roman emperor born in the neighboring Illyrian provinces, later retires to a palace at Split. Slavic migrations from the 6th–7th centuries and centuries of Byzantine rule follow, with Byzantine central authority only weakening enough for local lords to assert themselves around 1190, when the Principality of Arbanon emerges.

What it represents: no unified flag survives for the independent Illyrian kingdoms or for centuries of Roman and Byzantine provincial rule that followed; Illyrian tribal standards are, at best, mentioned in passing by Greek and Roman writers, never described in enough detail to reconstruct. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the Illyrian Wars run alongside Rome's climactic struggle with Hannibal and Carthage in the Second Punic War (218–201 BCE); centuries later, this same Illyrian coastline produces the Emperor Diocletian, whose retirement palace at Split (built c. 305 CE) still stands, and the region's Christianization keeps pace with Constantine's 313 CE Edict of Milan. The Slavic migrations of the 6th–7th centuries coincide with the final fall of the Western Roman Empire's institutions and, further east, the rise of Islam under Muhammad in the early 7th century.
