Flags of Algeria

Algeria's modern borders were drawn by the Ottoman Regency of Algiers, but its modern nationhood was forged in one of the 20th century's most significant and violent wars of independence. From the Berber kingdom of Numidia through Roman, Vandal, and Byzantine provincial rule, the Arab conquest, and centuries of Berber and Arab dynasties, through three centuries as an Ottoman regency, 132 years folded directly into metropolitan France itself, and an eight-year war that cost hundreds of thousands of lives, this page traces five eras and the green-white flag with its red star and crescent that has represented Algerian nationhood since the struggle for independence.

Unlike most French colonies, Algeria was legally treated as an integral part of France itself from 1830 until 1962 — not a protectorate or a colony administered separately, but constitutionally French soil, which is part of why its war of independence was fought so bitterly and for so long.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Algeria, c. 202 BCE–present, five political entities. No interactive map on this page — that layer is deferred project-wide to a future phase. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

One border, no internal split

Algeria's own real border, traced whole — Africa's largest country by area, but with no genuinely separate internal region to carve out honestly for this atlas. Press play, or drag the slider, to watch who held it, era to era.

Algeria has no well-documented internal partition of the kind this atlas tracks elsewhere. Regional Berber and Arab dynasties held different corners of the Maghreb across the centuries before Ottoman and then French rule, but no two rival governments each held a fixed, mapped half of the specifically Algerian territory the way Hungary or Poland once did — so this page shows one border and one ruling government at a time.

Who & why: the Algerian War of Independence (1954–1962), fought by the National Liberation Front (FLN) against French rule, is one of the 20th century's most significant and costly decolonization conflicts — marked by guerrilla warfare, the Battle of Algiers, and widespread civilian suffering on both sides before the March 1962 Évian Accords and a nearly unanimous July 1962 independence referendum end 132 years of French control.

What it represents: the green-and-white flag with a red crescent and five-pointed star, used by the FLN and Algerian nationalists since the 1930s–40s, becomes the flag of the new republic at independence and remains unchanged since.

French Algeria

Colonial ruleLouis de BourmontNo state religion imposed (Islam retained locally)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: France (from 1830)Immigration: European settler colonization (1830s–)

Who & why: France invades and captures Algiers in July 1830, beginning a conquest that takes decades to complete against sustained resistance, notably Emir Abdelkader's campaigns through the 1830s–40s. Unlike most French colonial holdings, Algeria is organized from 1848 into departments administered as an extension of metropolitan France itself, with large-scale European settlement. That status holds until the Algerian War of Independence (1954–1962), a real and violent conflict fought by the FLN against French rule, ends in independence.

What it represents: the French Tricolor, flown as the sole official flag throughout this entire period, reflecting Algeria's unusual legal status as French soil rather than an overseas colony or protectorate.

Who & why: the corsair brothers Oruç and Hayreddin Barbarossa establish Ottoman authority over Algiers in the 1510s–20s, and the regency becomes a largely autonomous military state under a Ottoman-appointed dey, its economy long built around Mediterranean corsair activity, before falling to French invasion in 1830.

What it represents: the Ottoman red field with white crescent and star, the standard naval and state ensign flown across Ottoman North Africa, including over Algiers throughout this regency period.

c. 202 BCE – 776 CE

Numidia, Rome & the Arab Conquest

Berber kingdom, then Roman/Vandal/Byzantine provinceMassinissaBerber/Punic paganism, then Christianity, then Islam (from 7th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Rome (46 BCE)Conquered by: Umayyad Caliphate (7th–8th c.)

Who & why: Massinissa unifies the Berber kingdom of Numidia in 202 BCE after siding with Rome against Carthage at the Battle of Zama; his descendants rule as Roman allies and occasional rivals (most famously Jugurtha, who fights Rome to a standstill for years before his defeat) until Julius Caesar annexes Numidia as a province after Thapsus in 46 BCE. Roman rule gives way to the Vandal Kingdom after Genseric's invasion in 429 CE, then to a Byzantine reconquest under Justinian's general Belisarius in 534; Umayyad Arab armies finally complete the conquest of the region by the early 8th century over sustained Berber resistance, and by 776 the Rustamid Ibadi imamate is founded at Tahert, opening the sequence of Berber and Arab dynasties covered in the next entry.

What it represents: no flag survives from Numidian, Roman, Vandal, or Byzantine rule over this territory, and the Berber resistance under Kahina is described only in later Arab chronicles without enough detail to reconstruct a banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Massinissa's Numidia takes shape in the immediate aftermath of Rome's climactic defeat of Hannibal at Zama; three and a half centuries later, Roman Numidia produces Augustine of Hippo, whose Confessions and City of God (written after Rome itself was sacked by the Visigoths in 410 CE) become foundational texts of Western Christian thought. The Arab conquest of the 7th–8th centuries runs alongside the broader Umayyad expansion that also reaches Spain by 711 and the height of Tang-dynasty China.
