Flags of the Arabian Peninsula

Seven countries — Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait — each with its own flag lineage, and below, one map showing who actually ruled this coastline-heavy peninsula across thirteen centuries, one slider-turn at a time.

Each country's own deep-dive page is still being painted; the cards below are placeholders until they're ready. Drag the map's slider first if you want the whole region's story at a glance — watch for how often it stays honestly fragmented rather than uniting.

The seven lineages

c. 630–present Saudi Arabia The Hejaz's caliphal centuries, Najd's long fragmentation, three Saudi states, the kingdom of 1932. c. 1200 BCE–present Yemen The Sabaean and Himyarite kingdoms, the Zaidi Imamate, a 72-year north/south split, reunification in 1990. c. 700–present Oman The Ibadi Imamate, Portuguese-held ports, a maritime empire reaching Zanzibar, the sultanate today. c. 700–present United Arab Emirates The Qawasim confederation, the Trucial States system, a 1971 federation of seven emirates. c. 700–present Qatar Long inside Bahrain's orbit, an 1868 separation, the Al Thani emirate, independence in 1971. c. 700–present Bahrain A medieval Arab emirate, an island seized by Portugal, the Al Khalifa dynasty since 1783. c. 700–present Kuwait The Al Sabah dynasty since 1752, an 1899 British treaty, the Gulf's first 1961 independence.

Thirteen centuries, one map

A real, traced map — actual coastlines along the Red Sea, the Arabian Sea, and the Gulf, not schematic shapes. Drag the slider or press play; each snapshot simplifies a messier reality into the dominant power of the moment, and Bahrain's tiny island footprint is drawn at an exaggerated scale (as this atlas does elsewhere for very small countries) so it stays visible.

Borders here are data, not decoration. A line between two regions only draws solid when they were actually governed separately. When they weren't — the whole peninsula under one Caliphate in 700, say, or North and South Yemen reunited in 1990 — the line dissolves and the names merge into whoever actually ruled them both. Where no one ruled at all — much of the interior for most of this map's history — the color reads plainly as "no single ruler," not a false unity.

Islamic Arabia

Real traced coastlines (simplified). Jordan, Iraq, and Iran are shown faintly for context — the Peninsula's major non-Peninsula neighbors — but are never colored or merged; they are not part of this atlas's tracked regions.

Try: start at 700 and watch every border across all seven countries dissolve at once — one Caliphate, one color, the peninsula's first real unification. Then jump to 1200 and watch Najd, the future UAE, Qatar, and Kuwait all merge into a single dusty "no single ruler" block, while Yemen and Oman and Bahrain each hold their own separate color — a real fragmented interior, not a forced one. Finally watch 1971: six separate colors resolve into the UAE's single new color while Kuwait, Qatar, and Bahrain each stay their own; then at 1990, watch North and South Yemen's internal line dissolve one last time as they reunite.

The same story, as a timeline

Maps are bad at showing when; timelines are bad at showing where. This bar chart uses the same real turning-point years as the map above, stretched out so you can compare how long each region actually spent under each power — watch how much of Najd's and the Gulf's timeline reads "no single ruler" compared to Yemen's or Oman's more continuous local dynasties.

The same story, nine frames — only the change lights up

Every region keeps its true color, but anything unchanged from the previous frame fades to low opacity; only the region(s) that actually changed hands stay bold with a solid outline. A caption under each frame spells out exactly what flipped. No slider to operate — scan left to right, top to bottom, and watch the map redraw itself like a flipbook.

About this atlas

This started as a family history project: tracing the flags that flew over the lands our ancestors came from, close enough to paint accurately — correct colors, correct proportions, correct dates. The Arabian Peninsula is Phase 7, moving the project into the Middle East for the first time.

Every country page (once painted) will carry its own timeline of flags with paint-ready color specs and a per-era location map. Colors here are close historical approximations; pre-modern swatches especially are informed reconstructions, not official specifications, and are labeled as such throughout. The regional map above simplifies thirteen centuries of messy, overlapping, and often genuinely unruled territory into nine snapshots — useful for orientation, not a substitute for the detail each country's own page will eventually carry.
