Flags of Armenia

From the ancient Artaxiad and Arsacid kingdoms — the first state in the world to adopt Christianity as its official religion, in 301 CE — through centuries without a crown of its own, a medieval Bagratid revival centered on the "city of a thousand and one churches," six more centuries split between empires, the Armenian Genocide, two years of hard-won independence, seven decades of Soviet rule, and finally a return to sovereignty in 1991 — ten flags for a people whose statehood has been interrupted more often than almost any other in this atlas, yet whose modern tricolor is the very same flag its short-lived first republic raised in 1918.

Armenia's history is unusually discontinuous: long stretches without any sovereign Armenian state at all sit between two genuinely independent kingdoms and two genuinely independent republics, a rhythm this page tries to render honestly rather than smoothing over.

Before the Artaxiads, the same highland was ruled first by the Iron Age kingdom of Urartu, a rival power to Assyria whose king Argishti I founded the fortress that became Yerevan in 782 BCE, and then, after Urartu's fall, by Orontid satraps under Achaemenid Persia until the region breaks free following Alexander's conquests.

Scope of this page: the lands of historic and present-day Armenia, c. 860 BCE–present, eleven political entities. This page is a flag timeline only — no interactive map of internal regional splits is included here; that layer is deferred to a future phase of the atlas, project-wide. See the Caucasus hub for a regional map covering Armenia alongside Georgia and Azerbaijan.

The eastern lineage of a once-divided homeland

This traces modern Armenia's own real border \u2014 which descends specifically from the EASTERN half of historic Armenia, the half Russia (not the Ottomans) ended up controlling. Press play, or drag the slider, to move through the page's own ten established eras.

Historic Armenia really was split for centuries \u2014 but not along modern Armenia\u2019s own borders. From 1375 (Cilicia\u2019s fall) through 1828, the Armenian highlands were partitioned between the Ottoman Empire (west) and Safavid/Qajar Persia (east); when Russia annexed the eastern half in 1828, that specific eastern territory became the lineage modern Armenia descends from. Western Armenia stayed Ottoman, then Turkish, and was never part of the modern republic\u2019s own land \u2014 so a map scoped to today\u2019s Armenia can\u2019t honestly show a two-sided partition on its own soil; the split is real, but it happened on ground this page doesn\u2019t cover.

1991 – present

Republic of Armenia (modern)

Republic, independentLevon Ter-PetrosyanArmenian Apostolic Christianity (constitutionally recognized national church)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: post-Soviet economic exodus (mainly to Russia)Immigration: Nagorno-Karabakh Armenian refugees (2023)

Who & why: Armenia holds an independence referendum on 21 September 1991 as the Soviet Union dissolves. The new republic almost immediately fights a war with Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh, a mostly Armenian-populated region inside Soviet Azerbaijan's former borders, reaching a 1994 ceasefire that leaves it a de facto independent, Armenian-backed enclave for nearly three decades — until Azerbaijan retakes the territory in a 2020 war and a further 2023 offensive.

What it represents: the red-blue-orange tricolor is readopted essentially unchanged from the First Republic's own 1918 flag, a deliberate statement of continuity with that earlier, short-lived independence.

1920 – 1991

Soviet Armenia

Soviet republicSargis KasyanState atheism
Migration & conquest:Immigration: diaspora repatriation (1946–48)

Who & why: weakened by war with Turkey, the First Republic is sovietized in November–December 1920 after the Treaty of Alexandropol. Armenia is folded into the Transcaucasian SFSR alongside Georgia and Azerbaijan from December 1922 until the 1936 "Stalin Constitution" splits it out as its own Armenian SSR. Soviet rule brings industrialization and, from the 1920s onward, organized resettlement of Armenians from the diaspora, alongside the repressions common to Stalin-era rule.

What it represents: the standard Soviet red banner with gold hammer and sickle, distinguished by a horizontal blue stripe near the bottom edge, a version adopted in 1952 and flown until the USSR's collapse.

1918 – 1920

First Republic of Armenia

Republic, independentHovhannes KachaznuniArmenian Apostolic Christianity (national church, no formal establishment)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: genocide survivors & refugees

Who & why: after a short-lived Transcaucasian federation collapses, Armenia declares independence on 28 May 1918 — the first sovereign Armenian state in nearly nine centuries, and the first anywhere since the fall of Cilician Armenia. The republic absorbs huge numbers of genocide survivors and refugees while fighting wars with Ottoman and then Turkish nationalist forces and border conflicts with Georgia and Azerbaijan, before falling to Soviet Russia's Red Army in late 1920.

What it represents: a red-blue-orange tricolor adopted in 1918, later interpreted as red for blood shed in the struggle for freedom, blue for the Armenian sky and hope, and orange (or apricot) for the courage and industriousness of the people and the land.

1915 – 1918

The Armenian Genocide and the end of Russian Armenia

OccupiedNo single ruler documentedNo single state religion (Ottoman / Russian partition)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: forced deportation & flight during the Genocide

Who & why: this bridging entry covers a genuinely messy, two-track period. In the Ottoman Empire's Armenian-populated eastern provinces, the government carries out the systematic deportation and mass killing of the Armenian population beginning in 1915 and continuing into the early 1920s — the Armenian Genocide, a well-documented historical atrocity in which an estimated 1 to 1.5 million Armenians died. At the same time, the much smaller "Russian Armenia" — the eastern Armenian lands annexed by Russia since 1828 — remains under the Russian Empire until the 1917 revolutions dissolve central authority there too, opening the way to the 1918 declaration of independence.

What it represents: no Armenian state flag exists in either territory during this period; Ottoman rule flew Ottoman colors in the west, and Russian imperial authority collapsed without a replacement flag in the east. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

1828 – 1915

Russian Armenia

AnnexedNicholas I of RussiaRussian Orthodoxy (state church); Armenian Apostolic Church tolerated
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Russian Empire (1828)Immigration: Armenian resettlement from Persia & Ottoman lands

Who & why: the February 1828 Treaty of Turkmenchay ends the second Russo-Persian War and forces Qajar Persia to cede the Yerevan and Nakhchivan khanates outright, bringing eastern Armenia under Russian rule for the first time. The treaty also encourages resettlement of ethnic Armenians from Persia and the Ottoman Empire into these newly Russian provinces, reshaping the region's demographics for the century that follows — even as the much larger population of western Armenians remains under Ottoman rule throughout this same period, a division that will end in catastrophe.

What it represents: the black-gold-white tricolor used as an unofficial "imperial" flag across the Russian Empire's territories from the mid-19th century, including its Armenian provinces.

1375 – 1828

Centuries split between empires

OccupiedLevon V of CiliciaNo single state religion (Ottoman / Safavid-Qajar partition)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: forced deportation to Persia (1604–05)

Who & why: with no Armenian kingdom of its own since 1045 and the Cilician kingdom gone by the 1370s, historic Armenia is parceled between rival powers for the better part of five centuries: the Kara Koyunlu and Ak Koyunlu Turkic confederations through the 1400s, then the Ottoman-Safavid rivalry that comes to define the whole Caucasus. Shah Ismail I founds the Safavid dynasty in 1501; the 1555 Treaty of Amasya and later treaties repeatedly formalize western Armenia as Ottoman territory and eastern Armenia as Persian (Safavid, later Qajar) territory, a partition that holds, with adjustments, until Russia's 1828 conquest of the eastern half.

What it represents: no single flag can honestly represent a genuinely divided territory; this entry shows the Ottoman crescent-and-star tradition on the west and a Safavid-style gold roundel on the east side by side, a deliberate split design rather than a single documented Armenian banner.

1045 – 1375

Loss of Ani, and the separate Kingdom of Cilicia

OccupiedGagik IIArmenian Apostolic Christianity (national church, no state backing)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Byzantine Empire, then Seljuk Turks (1045, 1071)Emigration: refugee resettlement in Cilicia
Ruling house:Rubenid – Hethumid dynasty

Who & why: the Byzantine Empire annexes the Bagratid kingdom in 1045, and the Seljuk Turks overrun the Armenian highlands after their victory at Manzikert in 1071, followed by Mongol overlordship in the 13th century. Historic Armenia has no sovereign state of its own for the whole of this period. Far to the south, however, Armenian refugees and nobility fleeing Seljuk conquest found a genuinely separate and long-lived state, the Kingdom of Cilicia (Cilician Armenia), on the Mediterranean coast from the late 11th century; crowned as a kingdom in 1198 under the Rubenid dynasty and later ruled by the Hethumids and Lusignans, it survives as an important Crusader-era ally until the Mamluks conquer it in 1375.

What it represents: no distinct Armenian state flag is attested for the homeland during this period of Byzantine, Seljuk, and Mongol overlordship; the field shown is a generic, informed representation of that succession of foreign rule, not a documented Armenian banner. (Cilicia flew its own royal heraldry, not shown here, as this page tracks the historic-Armenian-homeland lineage.)

885 – 1045

Bagratid Kingdom of Armenia

Kingdom, independentAshot IArmenian Apostolic Christianity (state church)
Ruling house:Bagratuni dynastyArtsruni dynasty

Who & why: Ashot I, of the Bagratuni (Bagratid) noble house, is recognized as "Prince of Princes" by the Byzantines and Abbasid Caliphate and crowned king in 885, reestablishing Armenian statehood after roughly four and a half centuries without a sovereign crown. The kingdom's capital, Ani, grows into a major medieval city famed as the "city of a thousand and one churches," reaching its height under Gagik I around the year 1000. Internal succession disputes weaken the kingdom, and the Byzantine Empire annexes it outright in 1045, ending Bagratid rule in the Armenian homeland.

What it represents: a blue field with a gold cross and central disc, drawn from Bagratid royal and ecclesiastical iconography associated with Ani's churches and coinage — a separate dynasty from Georgia's own Bagrationi house despite the shared name.

428 – 885

Byzantine and Arab overlordship

OccupiedArtaxias IVArmenian Apostolic Christianity (national church, no state backing)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Sasanian Persia, then the Arab Caliphate

Who & why: the Sasanian Persian Empire abolishes the Arsacid Armenian monarchy in 428, dividing the territory between Byzantine and Persian administration. Armenian noble (nakharar) houses retain substantial local autonomy throughout, and Armenian religious and cultural life flourishes regardless — Mesrop Mashtots invents the Armenian alphabet around 405, just before this period begins — but no single Armenian king rules the whole territory again until the Muslim Arab conquerors, who absorb the region from the mid-7th century, allow the Bagratuni family to rise to formal kingship in 885.

What it represents: no securely documented Armenian state flag survives from this long stretch under Byzantine and Arab overlordship. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 321 BCE – 428 CE

Ancient Kingdom of Armenia (Artaxiad & Arsacid)

Ancient kingdomArtaxias IChristianity (state religion from 301 CE)

Who & why: Armenian statehood begins with the Orontid dynasty as a Persian satrapy from the 6th century BCE, but the fully sovereign kingdom is usually dated from Artaxias I founding the Artaxiad dynasty around 189 BCE; under Tigranes the Great (r. 95–55 BCE) it briefly becomes one of the most powerful states in the region, stretching from the Caspian to the Mediterranean. The Arsacid (Arshakuni) dynasty rules from 52 CE. In 301 CE, King Tiridates III makes Christianity the kingdom's official religion — the first state in world history to do so, more than a decade before Rome's Edict of Milan. The Sasanian Persian Empire finally abolishes the Arsacid monarchy in 428, partitioning the kingdom.

What it represents: no flag or fixed heraldic banner survives from this pre-heraldic antiquity; the ancient kingdom is known through Greek, Roman, and Armenian written sources, coinage, and archaeology rather than surviving vexillology. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 860 – 321 BCE

Kingdom of Urartu & the Orontid Satrapy

Ancient kingdom, then Persian satrapyArgishti IUrartian polytheism, later Zoroastrian-influenced
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Medes & Scythians (c. 590 BCE)Ruled by: Achaemenid Persian Empire (from c. 550 BCE)

Who & why: the Kingdom of Urartu, centered on Lake Van with its capital at Tushpa, rises as a rival to Assyria from the 9th century BCE; King Argishti I founds the fortress of Erebuni in 782 BCE, the direct predecessor of modern Yerevan. Weakened by generations of Assyrian warfare, Urartu collapses under Median and Scythian pressure around 590 BCE. The Achaemenid Persian Empire absorbs the highland afterward, governing it as the satrapy of Armina under the Orontid (Yervanduni) dynasty from the mid-6th century BCE. Persian control ends with Alexander the Great's conquest of the Achaemenid Empire in the 330s BCE; the Orontids continue as a Hellenistic-era dynasty until the satrap Orontes III effectively breaks from the collapsing Achaemenid successor states following Alexander's death, conventionally dated to 321 BCE and treated as the start of a fully sovereign Armenian kingdom.

What it represents: no flag or heraldic banner survives from Urartu or the Orontid satrapy; Urartian kings are known instead through their own royal cuneiform inscriptions and archaeology at sites like Tushpa and Erebuni, not through vexillology. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Urartu's wars with Assyria run alongside the height of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, including King Sargon II's own recorded campaign against Urartu in 714 BCE; the kingdom's fall around 590 BCE is roughly contemporary with the Babylonian Captivity of the Jews and the rise of the Achaemenid Persian Empire under Cyrus the Great. Alexander's conquest of Persia in the 330s BCE, which ends direct Achaemenid rule over Armenia, coincides with the height of Classical Greek philosophy and the library culture that flowers soon after at Alexandria.
