Flags of Australia

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived on this continent for at least 65,000 years — home to the world's oldest continuous living cultures, organized into hundreds of distinct language groups and nations long before any European sail appeared on the horizon. This page's flag lineage proper begins where the documentary record of flags itself begins: British colonization from 1788. No flag is invented to stand in for the pre-colonial era; it is shown here honestly, without one.

From a single penal colony at Sydney Cove, six separate British colonies grew up across the continent over the following century, each self-governing by the 1850s–90s but never politically united until they federated themselves, peacefully and by referendum, into the Commonwealth of Australia on 1 January 1901 — the flag chosen that year, only lightly formalized since, is the one still flown today.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Australia, pre-1788 to present, three entries. No interactive map on this page — see the Pacific hub for the region-wide overview. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Hundreds of nations, six colonies, one Federation

Australia's own real state borders, starting from the same undivided continent Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander nations governed for at least 65,000 years, through each colony founded separately — New South Wales first in 1788, the last (Queensland) splitting off in 1859 — and never governed as one until Federation on 1 January 1901. Australia spent 113 years as up to six separate, self-governing British colonies before they were ever one country. New South Wales originally claimed the whole eastern mainland; Tasmania (1803), Western Australia (1829), South Australia (1836) were founded as separate colonies from the start, while Victoria (1851) and Queensland (1859) later split off from New South Wales itself. The border lines below solidify wherever two neighboring colonies were genuinely governed apart, and dissolve the moment Federation unites them on 1 January 1901.

Commonwealth of Australia

Real traced state/colony borders — the six original colonies' modern-state boundaries, used as the best available approximation of their 19th-century colonial borders (the Northern Territory, part of South Australia until 1911, is folded into SA here). The pre-1788 era additionally names eight major documented Aboriginal nations at their approximate real locations (Eora, Wiradjuri, Kulin, Noongar, Kaurna, Arrernte, Yuggera, Palawa) — a representative selection, not an exhaustive or precisely-bounded one. No boundary lines are drawn for them: with several hundred distinct nations recorded across the continent (see Tindale's foundational 1940 map and the modern AIATSIS Map of Indigenous Australia it informs), any small set of borders this atlas could draw at this scale would misrepresent a reality far more complex than six colonial rectangles, so the region beneath these names stays in the same single honest “no boundaries invented” color as the rest of the pre-contact era.

Who & why: the six British colonies — New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, South Australia, Western Australia, and Tasmania — vote by referendum to federate, becoming the Commonwealth of Australia on 1 January 1901. A public design competition that same year produces the winning flag; the Commonwealth government formally fixes it in law as the Australian National Flag under the Flags Act 1953, effective 1954, with no change to the design itself.

What it represents: the Union Jack in the canton marks the federation's origin within the British Empire and Commonwealth; the large seven-pointed Commonwealth Star below it originally stood for the six founding states plus the territories; five white stars on the fly form the Southern Cross, the constellation visible only from the southern hemisphere.

Who & why: Britain establishes a penal colony at Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788, transporting convicts to what it names New South Wales. Five further colonies split off or are separately founded across the century — Tasmania (1825), Western Australia (1829), South Australia (1836), Victoria (1851), and Queensland (1859) — each gaining its own responsible self-government by the second half of the 19th century while remaining separate British possessions with no shared government until federation.

What it represents: the Union Jack, Britain's own flag, flew as the formal colonial ensign throughout; each colony later flew its own Blue Ensign variant with a distinct badge in the fly, but the shared Union Jack canton is the constant across the whole pre-federation era.

Cost to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander nations: British settlement is founded on the legal fiction of terra nullius — land belonging to no one — not formally overturned until the High Court's 1992 Mabo decision. Smallpox devastates the Sydney region's Aboriginal population within a year of the First Fleet's arrival; frontier violence follows colonial expansion across the continent for well over a century, with the University of Newcastle's Colonial Frontier Massacres project alone documenting more than 400 confirmed mass-killing events between 1788 and the 1930s. Aboriginal nations are progressively dispossessed of their own country, their governance and law broken up, and surviving communities forced onto missions and reserves far from traditional land — the process the map above traces as hundreds of named nations give way to six colonial administrations with no Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander voice in their government.

c. 65,000 years ago – 1788

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australia

Indigenous nations, pre-contactNo single ruler documentedNo single state religion (distinct nations, distinct traditions)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: earliest human arrival from Southeast Asia (c. 65,000 ya)

Who & why: archaeological evidence places Aboriginal peoples on the continent for at least 65,000 years, organized into several hundred distinct nations and language groups with their own law, land custodianship, and oral tradition — the oldest continuous living culture documented anywhere in the world. Territorial and legal knowledge across this diversity was carried and transmitted through songlines — oral and performative pathways encoding real, checkable navigational and geographic information across vast distances; a 2014 study of Wardaman songline navigation by astronomer Ray Norris and Wardaman elder Bill Yidumduma Harney confirmed such traditions functioned as genuine, working wayfinding systems, not merely narrative custom. The anthropologist Norman Tindale's 1940 map cataloguing the approximate territories of these nations remains a foundational reference informing the modern AIATSIS Map of Indigenous Australia still in use today — though both carry their own explicit caution that any drawn boundary is necessarily approximate, which is why the map above names a representative few of these nations without drawing borders around any of them. Torres Strait Islander peoples, culturally and linguistically distinct, inhabit the islands between Australia and New Guinea. No single continent-wide political entity ever existed in this era, and none is invented here to stand in for one.

What it represents: flags as a European heraldic form were never part of pre-contact Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander culture; this entry is left honestly blank rather than retrofitted with a symbol. The Aboriginal Flag and Torres Strait Islander Flag now in wide use were both designed in the 1970s–90s, well within the modern era, and are outside this page's pre-1788 scope.
