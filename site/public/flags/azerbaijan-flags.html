Flags of Azerbaijan

From ancient Caucasian Albania, a Christian kingdom under Sassanid Persian and then Arab overlordship, through the long-lived Shirvanshahs of Shamakhi, who ruled some form of their own state from the 9th century to 1538 — one of the longest-running dynasties in this atlas — through absorption into Safavid and then Qajar Persia, a two-stage Russian imperial conquest, the first secular, democratic, parliamentary republic ever established in the Muslim world in 1918 (which extended the vote to women that same year), seven decades of Soviet rule, and independence again in 1991 — eight flags for a country whose modern blue-red-green tricolor with its crescent and star is, like Armenia's, essentially the same flag its short-lived first republic raised.

Azerbaijan's story runs on nearly the same clock as its neighbors Georgia and Armenia — Persian-and-then-Russian domination, a brief 1918 independence, Soviet incorporation, and a 1991 return — but its deepest roots are Persian rather than Byzantine or Ottoman, a distinction this page's flags trace directly.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Azerbaijan, c. 4th century BCE–present, eight political entities. This page is a flag timeline only — no interactive map of internal regional splits is included here; that layer is deferred to a future phase of the atlas, project-wide. See the Caucasus hub for a regional map covering Azerbaijan alongside Georgia and Armenia.

One border, and one lasting internal split: Nagorno-Karabakh

Azerbaijan's own real border, traced whole, with one schematic internal region carved out: Nagorno-Karabakh, administered separately from Baku for roughly three decades. The 18th-century fragmentation into some twenty local khanates under weakening Persian suzerainty was real too, but never a clean two- or three-way partition this atlas can responsibly map, so that period is still shown as a single region. Press play, or drag the slider, to watch who held it, era to era.

The 18th-century khanate period was a real fragmentation, but into roughly twenty small polities, not the kind of clean two- or three-way split this atlas maps elsewhere. Baku, Ganja, Shirvan, Karabakh, Quba, and over a dozen other khanates each ruled their own small territory under nominal Persian suzerainty — too many, and too irregularly bounded, to responsibly reconstruct as a small handful of honest regions, so this page shows one border and one dominant power at a time for that era. The 1988–2023 Nagorno-Karabakh period is different: for most of that span, a self-declared, Armenian-backed authority administered the territory separately from the government in Baku, a real and mappable administrative split shown here as its own traced region. This map describes who controlled the territory when, not who was right to.

1991 – present

Republic of Azerbaijan (modern)

Republic, independentAyaz MutalibovSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Azerbaijani refugees from Armenia & Karabakh (1988–94)Emigration: ethnic Armenians (1988–early 1990s)Emigration: ethnic Armenians from Karabakh (2023)

Who & why: Azerbaijan declares independence on 18 October 1991 as the Soviet Union dissolves. The republic fights a war with Armenia over Nagorno-Karabakh, a mostly Armenian-populated enclave inside its Soviet-era borders, losing effective control of it after a 1994 ceasefire; Azerbaijan retakes the territory in a 2020 war and a further 2023 offensive, ending nearly three decades of de facto Armenian-backed separation.

What it represents: the blue-red-green tricolor with a white crescent and eight-pointed star is readopted from the 1918 Democratic Republic's own flag, officially explained as blue for Turkic heritage, red for progress and modernity, and green for Islam, with the crescent and star further symbolizing Islam and the eight branches of the Turkic peoples.

1920 – 1991

Soviet Azerbaijan

Soviet republicNariman NarimanovState atheism
Migration & conquest:Immigration: laborers into the Baku oil industryEmigration: ethnic Armenians amid Sumgait & Baku violence (1988–90)
Ruling house:Qajar dynasty

Who & why: the 11th Red Army marches into Baku in April 1920, ending the Democratic Republic with barely a fight. Azerbaijan is folded into the Transcaucasian SFSR alongside Georgia and Armenia from December 1922 until the 1936 "Stalin Constitution" splits it out as its own Azerbaijan SSR. Baku's oil fields make the republic an industrial priority for Moscow throughout the Soviet period, and Nagorno-Karabakh is established as an autonomous oblast inside the republic's borders in 1923, a decision whose consequences outlast the Soviet Union itself.

What it represents: the standard Soviet red banner with gold hammer and sickle, distinguished by a horizontal light-blue stripe near the bottom edge, a version adopted in 1952 and flown until the USSR's collapse.

1918 – 1920

Azerbaijan Democratic Republic

Republic, independentMammad Amin RasulzadeSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: displacement amid Baku's 1918 intercommunal violence
Ruling house:Qajar dynasty

Who & why: declared on 28 May 1918 after a short-lived Transcaucasian federation collapses, the Azerbaijan Democratic Republic is the first secular, democratic, parliamentary republic ever established anywhere in the Muslim world. Its parliament extends the vote to women that same year, ahead of many European states including, notably, the United Kingdom and the United States. The republic is poor and embattled, fighting border conflicts with Armenia, before the Red Army takes Baku in April 1920 with little resistance.

What it represents: a blue-red-green tricolor with a white crescent moon and eight-pointed star, adopted in November 1918, blending pan-Turkic blue, modernizing red, and Islamic green with traditional Islamic crescent-and-star imagery — the same flag the modern republic readopts in 1991.

1813 – 1918

Russian imperial rule

AnnexedAlexander I of RussiaRussian Orthodoxy (state church); Islam tolerated, not established
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Armenian resettlement after 1828Immigration: Baku oil-boom laborers (1870s–)
Ruling house:Qajar dynasty

Who & why: Russia's conquest of Azerbaijani territory happens in two stages. The First Russo-Persian War (1804–13) ends with the Treaty of Gulistan, which forces Qajar Persia to cede Baku, Ganja, Shirvan, Karabakh, and most of the other khanates north of the Aras River outright. The Nakhchivan khanate, further south, remains Persian until the Second Russo-Persian War ends with the February 1828 Treaty of Turkmenchay, completing Russian control of the whole territory that becomes modern Azerbaijan. Baku's oil boom from the 1870s onward makes it one of the Russian Empire's most important industrial cities.

What it represents: the black-gold-white tricolor used as an unofficial "imperial" flag across the Russian Empire's territories from the mid-19th century, including its Azerbaijani provinces.

1747 – 1813

Vassal khanates under Persian suzerainty

Vassal khanatesNo single ruler documentedTwelver Shi'a Islam (state religion, inherited from Safavid rule)
Ruling house:Qajar dynasty

Who & why: the 1747 assassination of Nader Shah, who had briefly reunited Persia by force, fragments Persian authority and allows a patchwork of semi-autonomous local khanates — Baku, Ganja, Shirvan, Karabakh, Quba, Sheki, Nakhchivan, and others — to emerge across Azerbaijani territory, each ruled by its own khan but paying nominal tribute to whichever Persian dynasty holds the throne. The Qajar dynasty consolidates power in Persia from 1789, tightening (without fully restoring) control over these khanates, until Russian conquest begins in 1804.

What it represents: no single flag unites these many rival khanates; the field shown is a generic, informed representation of Persian suzerain authority during this fragmented era, not a documented banner of any one khanate.

1538 – 1747

Safavid Persia

Vassal khanatesShah Tahmasp ITwelver Shi'a Islam (state religion)
Ruling house:Safavid dynasty

Who & why: Shah Ismail I founds the Safavid dynasty in 1501, converting Persia to Twelver Shia Islam, and within a generation the Safavids extinguish the long-ruling Shirvanshahs outright in 1538, absorbing their lands directly into the empire. Most of what becomes Azerbaijan is governed as Safavid provinces for the following two centuries, contested at the margins with the rival Ottoman Empire, until the dynasty collapses amid Afghan invasion in the 1720s and, after an interregnum, Nader Shah's 1747 assassination opens the way to local khanates.

What it represents: a teal field with a gold roundel, representative of Safavid dynastic and religious imagery associated with the period's coinage and court banners across the empire's provinces, including Shirvan.

c. 861 – 1538

Shirvanshahs and local emirates

Kingdom, independentHaytham ibn KhalidSunni Islam (state religion of the Shirvanshahs)
Ruling house:ShirvanshahsSafavid dynasty

Who & why: the Shirvanshahs, ruling from Shamakhi (later Baku) in what is now northeastern Azerbaijan, are attested from the 9th century and go on ruling, remarkably, in some form for nearly seven centuries — through Mongol invasion, Timur's campaigns, and constant pressure from larger neighbors — making them one of the longest-lived dynasties anywhere in this atlas. Other parts of Azerbaijani territory in this long span pass through various local Muslim dynasties and, briefly, Mongol Ilkhanate overlordship in the 13th–14th centuries. The line finally ends in 1538, when the Safavids depose the last Shirvanshah and absorb his lands directly.

What it represents: no single Shirvanshah state flag is documented with certainty; the green field and white crescent shown here are an informed representation drawing on general Islamic dynastic heraldic conventions of the era, not a specific surviving banner.

Caucasian Albania and the Sassanid/Arab conquest

Kingdom, then Sassanid/Arab provinceUrnayr of AlbaniaZoroastrian/pagan, then Christian (4th–8th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Umayyad Caliphate (705 CE)
Ruling house:Arranshahik dynasty

Who & why: Caucasian Albania, a kingdom in the eastern Caucasus roughly overlapping modern Azerbaijan, is attested from the 4th century BCE and survives for the better part of a millennium as a shifting client of Rome, Parthia, and above all Sassanid Persia. King Urnayr's conversion to Christianity around 370 CE, under Armenian ecclesiastical influence, opens centuries of semi-autonomous Christian rule by the Arranshahik dynasty even as Sassanid Persia dominates the kingdom's foreign policy. The Umayyad Caliphate's conquest of the Caucasus, completed by 705 CE, ends this autonomy for good; the fractured, gradually Islamizing local dynasties that follow — including the Shirvanshahs, attested from 861 — inherit post-Albanian Shirvan directly.

What it represents: no flag survives from Caucasian Albania; its kings are known through Armenian and Georgian chronicles, Sassanid-era inscriptions, and archaeology, not through any vexillological record. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the Parthian and Sassanid empires' long wars with Rome, the construction of the Sassanid palace at Ctesiphon, and the rapid 7th-century Arab conquests that end the Sassanid Empire itself in 651 CE — the same conquest wave that reaches Caucasian Albania barely a generation later, in 705.
