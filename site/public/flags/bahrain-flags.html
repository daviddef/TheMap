Flags of Bahrain

One of the Persian Gulf's earliest and most continuously significant trading hubs, home to the ancient Bronze Age civilization of Dilmun and its Hellenistic and Sassanid successors Tylos and Mishmahig, through a medieval Arab emirate, Portuguese occupation of the island in 1521, over a century and a half of Persian Safavid rule, the Al Khalifa dynasty's 1783 conquest that still rules today, British protection through the 19th and 20th centuries, and the first Gulf state to strike oil, in 1932, giving it a head start on diversifying beyond it.

Bahrain's red-and-white serrated flag traces the same plain-red maritime tradition shared across this whole coast; the white serrated border, required by 19th-century British treaty to distinguish it from neighboring red flags, is the flag's oldest distinguishing mark, though the exact number of serrations shifted for decades before being fixed at five points only in 2002.

Scope of this page: the island of Bahrain, c. 3000 BCE–present, eight political entries. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Kingdom of Bahrain

Kingdom, independentHamad bin Isa Al KhalifaIslam (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: South Asian & Filipino migrant labor
Ruling house:House of Al Khalifa

Who & why: Sheikh Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa upgrades Bahrain from an emirate to a kingdom in February 2002, styling himself king as part of a wider constitutional reform package following a 2001 referendum. Bahrain becomes an early adopter of Gulf financial diversification, building a regional banking hub that lessens its dependence on its now-modest oil reserves, first tapped back in 1932.

What it represents: the same red-and-white serrated flag continues, its number of white serrations formally fixed at five in 2002 to symbolize the Five Pillars of Islam — ending decades of inconsistent serration counts on earlier prints.

State of Bahrain (independent, pre-kingdom)

Emirate, independentIsa bin Salman Al KhalifaIslam (state religion, per 1973 constitution)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: South Asian labor migration (oil-boom era)
Ruling house:House of Al Khalifa

Who & why: Bahrain declares independence on 15 August 1971, choosing to go it alone rather than join the federation that becomes the UAE, after a United Nations survey the same year confirms Bahraini preference for independence over a rival Iranian claim to the island dating back centuries. Bahrain uses its early oil-era head start to diversify into finance and services well ahead of most of its neighbors.

What it represents: the red-and-white serrated flag continues from the protectorate era, its number of white points still varying somewhat from print to print — commonly eight, sometimes twenty-eight — until the 2002 fix at five.

1783 – 1971

Al Khalifa dynasty (British-protected sheikhdom from the 19th c.)

Sheikhdom / protectorateAhmed bin Muhammad Al KhalifaSunni Islam (ruling dynasty; Shia majority population)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Bani Utbah Arabs (1783)
Ruling house:House of Al Khalifa

Who & why: the Bani Utbah tribal confederation's Al Khalifa branch conquers Bahrain from its Persian-aligned rulers in 1783, founding the dynasty that still rules the kingdom today. Britain and Bahrain sign a series of treaties through the 19th century (1861, 1880, 1892) formalizing British protection over the sheikhdom's foreign affairs, and Bahrain becomes the first place in the Gulf to strike oil, at Jabal Dukhan in 1932, prompting the region's earliest moves toward economic diversification beyond it.

What it represents: the plain red flag, this coast's shared maritime color, gains a white band at the hoist under British treaty requirements meant to distinguish Bahrain's ships from other red-flagged neighbors — the serration itself develops gradually over the following century.

Persian (Safavid, later Afsharid) rule

Persian suzeraintyShah Abbas ITwelver Shia Islam (Safavid state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Safavid Persia (1602)

Who & why: Safavid Persia expels the Portuguese from Bahrain in 1602, ruling the island directly or through local governors for most of the following 180 years, with a brief interlude of Omani Ya'ariba control in the 1720s before Nader Shah's Afsharid Persia reasserts authority. Persian rule ends for good when the Bani Utbah's Al Khalifa branch conquers the island in 1783.

What it represents: no distinctly Bahraini flag is attested under direct Persian administration in this period; various Persian provincial and dynastic banners would have applied instead. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

1521 – 1602

Portuguese occupation

OccupationAntónio CorreiaRoman Catholic (Portuguese Crown); population remained Muslim
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Portugal (1521–1602)

Who & why: Portuguese forces seize Bahrain in 1521 as part of their wider push to control Gulf and Indian Ocean trade routes, building Bahrain Fort (Qal'at al-Bahrain, on the site of the ancient Dilmun capital) as their stronghold. Portuguese control ends in 1602 when the island's own governor, backed by Safavid Persia, expels the garrison.

What it represents: the Portuguese fly their own maritime ensigns over the fort rather than any distinctly Bahraini banner; no local flag is attested for this occupation period. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

899 – 1521

Medieval Arab emirate (Qarmatian, Uyunid, Usfurid, Jarwanid)

Fragmented / successive dynastiesAbu Sa'id al-JannabiIsmaili Islam (Qarmatian, at start); later dynasties mixed Sunni/Shia

Who & why: the Qarmatians, a radical Ismaili sect, seize control of the wider Bahrain region (then a name covering the whole eastern Arabian coast, not just the island) in 899, ruling as a distinctive and controversial state until the Uyunid dynasty overthrows them in 1076. The Uyunids, then the Usfurids, then the Jarwanids govern the region in succession through the following centuries — real, datable medieval Arab dynasties, but no single flag can be attributed with confidence across four different ruling houses over six centuries, so this is one honest bridging entry.

What it represents: these medieval Arab dynasties used seals, banners, and religious symbols rather than fixed territorial flags in the modern sense; none is well enough attested to reconstruct with confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Early Islamic Bahrain, on Dilmun's ancient trading foundations

Caliphal suzeraintyAbd al-Malik (Umayyad Caliph)Islam (Umayyad, then Abbasid caliphal authority)

Who & why: Bahrain converts to Islam during Muhammad's own lifetime in the 7th century, one of the faith's earliest conversions outside the Hejaz, and comes under successive caliphal authority. The island sits on the legacy of the ancient Dilmun civilization, a major Bronze Age trading hub linking Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley millennia earlier, a continuity of trade significance this small island has held for roughly four thousand years running.

What it represents: no flag in the modern territorial sense survives from this era; the region's rulers used caliphal and provincial insignia rather than a fixed local banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 3000 BCE – 700 CE

Dilmun, Tylos & Sassanid Mishmahig

Kingdom, later Persian/Hellenistic provinceNo single ruler documentedPolytheistic (cult of Inzak), later Zoroastrian
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Achaemenid Persia (6th c. BCE)

Who & why: Bahrain's known history opens with Dilmun, a major Bronze Age trading civilization first attested in Sumerian cuneiform tablets by the late 3rd millennium BCE, linking Mesopotamia to the Indus Valley civilization through Gulf maritime trade; Sargon of Akkad and later Assyrian kings record tribute from Dilmun's own rulers. Achaemenid Persian control from the 6th century BCE draws the island — known to Greek geographers as Tylos — into successive Achaemenid, Seleucid, and Parthian orbits, and Sassanid Persia renames and garrisons it as Mishmahig by the 3rd century CE. Small Christian communities take root under Sassanid tolerance in late antiquity, shortly before the island's conversion to Islam during Muhammad's own lifetime opens the era covered in the following entry.

What it represents: no flag in the modern territorial sense survives from Dilmun, Tylos, or Sassanid Mishmahig; this deep history is known through Sumerian and Akkadian cuneiform tablets, Greek geographic texts, and archaeology, not through any vexillological record. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the building of Egypt's pyramids at Giza (Dilmun's earliest cuneiform attestations are roughly contemporary with Egypt's Old Kingdom), Babylon's Hanging Gardens under Nebuchadnezzar II, Alexander the Great's own admiral Nearchus surveying the Gulf coast after the Indian campaign, and the Sassanid Empire's own collapse in the same 7th-century Arab conquests that bring Islam to Bahrain itself.
