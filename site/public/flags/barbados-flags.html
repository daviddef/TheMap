Flags of Barbados

Of every territory traced in this Caribbean hub, Barbados is the only one that never changed colonial hands at all: claimed and settled by the English in 1627, it stayed continuously under the same crown — English, then British — for well over three centuries, without a single interruption by another European power, until independence in 1966. No other island in this hub can say that.

The story doesn't end at independence, either. On 30 November 2021, Barbados's 55th anniversary of independence, the country became a republic — removing the British monarch as head of state and swearing in a Barbadian president in her place. The flag itself did not change that day; this page treats 2021 as a change in the form of government, not a fourth flag.

Independent Barbados

Independent state / republicErrol BarrowNo established church
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to Britain, the US & Canada (1950s–70s)Immigration: CARICOM migrants (1990s–)
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: Barbados becomes independent from Britain on 30 November 1966, ending 339 unbroken years of colonial rule. On the 55th anniversary of that independence, 30 November 2021, Barbados becomes a republic — a Barbadian president, Dame Sandra Mason, replaces Queen Elizabeth II as head of state. It is a real and recent constitutional change, but not a change of flag: the same banner adopted in 1966 still flies over the republic today.

What it represents: two ultramarine bands (sea and sky) flank a central gold band (the island's sand), with a black “broken trident” centered on the gold — the trident head kept, its shaft deliberately shortened, symbolizing Barbados's break from its colonial past. The trident itself references the Poseidon-adjacent badge carried on the old colonial ensign, reclaimed here as an independence symbol rather than a colonial one.

British Barbados (continuous, unbroken)

British colonySir William CourtenAnglican (established)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: enslaved Africans (17th–18th c.)Emigration: Panama Canal & regional labor migration (early 1900s)
Ruling house:House of StuartHouse of Hanover

Who & why: English settlers first land and claim Barbados for England in 1625, with the first permanent settlement founded at Holetown in 1627. Remarkably, Barbados is the only major territory in this hub that never changed hands between colonial powers even once — it stays continuously English, then British, for well over three centuries. It becomes one of the wealthiest sugar-plantation slave societies in the Caribbean; the harsh 1661 Barbados Slave Code was later copied as a model by other English colonies. Emancipation comes in 1834, with a four-year “apprenticeship” period ending full freedom in 1838, the same timeline as Jamaica's.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign — a dark blue field with the Union Jack in the canton — defaced with a Barbados colonial badge, formalized in the later 19th century. The badge's trident/shield imagery is the direct origin of the black trident carried on the later independence flag.

Pre-colonial and uninhabited-at-contact Barbados

Indigenous / unsettledNo single ruler documentedNo state religion documented

Who & why: Barbados was inhabited by indigenous Kalinago and other Arawakan-speaking peoples at various points in the centuries before European contact. But for reasons still debated among historians — possibly Spanish slave raiding in the early 1500s, possibly voluntary withdrawal, possibly disease — the island appears to have been largely uninhabited by the time English settlers arrived in the 1620s. Unlike almost every other island in this hub, there was no ongoing indigenous society for the English to displace by force at the moment of settlement.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from Barbados's earlier indigenous period. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented, in keeping with how this atlas treats every pre-heraldic era.

c. 350 – 1200s CE

Saladoid and early Arawakan settlement

Indigenous / unsettledNo single ruler documentedNo state religion documented

Who & why: archaeological evidence places the earliest settlement of Barbados by Saladoid horticulturalists, migrating up the island chain from South America's Orinoco basin, at around the 4th century CE — part of the same broad Arawakan-speaking migration that settled much of the Lesser Antilles. Successive Arawakan communities continue occupying the island for roughly eight centuries, developing pottery traditions and small farming settlements, before the pattern of continuous occupation ends by around the 1200s, opening the more sparsely documented pre-contact centuries covered in the following entry.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this era; the island's earliest inhabitants are known only through pottery, middens, and burial sites, not through any vexillological record. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the late Roman Empire's western collapse in the 5th century, the rise of the Maya civilization's Classic-period city-states in Mesoamerica, the founding of Islam in the 7th century, and the construction of Cambodia's Angkor Wat in the early 12th century — all unfolding while Barbados, isolated far out in the Atlantic beyond the main Lesser Antilles chain, remained untouched by any of it.
