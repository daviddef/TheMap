Flags of Belgium

Long before Belgium existed as an idea, Julius Caesar's legions carved the province of Gallia Belgica — the source of the modern name — out of the fierce Belgae tribes, and Rome's fall left a patchwork of feudal counties, a duchy, and a prince-bishopric with no shared government for half a millennium. Belgium's own borders were then fixed by other people's decisions for four and a half centuries before its own 1830 revolution — a Burgundian purchase, a Habsburg succession, a French annexation, a peace conference in Vienna — and then, twice in the 20th century, briefly erased again by German occupation. Eleven political entities, and a black-yellow-red tricolor that has now outlasted every regime that ever flew a different flag over these same provinces.

Belgium's flag colors come from the coat of arms of the old Duchy of Brabant — a black lion with red claws and tongue on a gold field — abstracted into three vertical bands by the revolutionaries of 1830, deliberately styled after republican France's tricolor rather than the horizontal Dutch one they had just rebelled against.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Belgium, c. 57 BCE–present, eleven political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the Low Countries hub map and linked pages at the bottom.

One country, on a real map, 57 BCE–present

Belgium doesn't get this atlas's usual split-border treatment. Its historic north/south linguistic divide — Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia, formalized through federal reforms between 1970 and 1993 — is genuine, but it's an internal, negotiated devolution of a single existing state, not two territories separately conquered or ruled by rival foreign powers at the same time the way Hungary's Royal/Ottoman split was. Inventing a military-style partition line here would misrepresent what actually happened, so this map instead traces a single Belgian outline — a real shape, lifted from the same boundary data as the hub map above — and simply recolors it as control changes hands, including the two 20th-century interruptions when it briefly wasn't self-governed at all.

The Belgian tricolor is younger than it looks, and the country under it was occupied twice. The flag itself dates only to 1830. But it has now flown continuously since 1944 — longer than any single flag flew over these provinces before it — broken only by two German occupations, four years in the First World War and four in the Second, both of which get their own frame below rather than being folded silently into the Kingdom's story.

Who & why: Brussels is liberated on 3 September 1944; the government returns from exile in London and the monarchy is restored, though King Leopold III's controversial 1940 surrender triggers a bitter postwar "Royal Question," resolved only by his 1951 abdication in favor of his son Baudouin. Belgium becomes a founding member of NATO (1949) and the European project that follows, with Brussels emerging as its de facto capital.

What it represents: the same 1830 tricolor, unchanged, now the longest-continuous flag these provinces have flown — the 1970–1993 constitutional reforms that turn Belgium into a federal state of Flemish, Walloon, and Brussels regions happen entirely underneath this one national flag, which none of that devolution ever touches.

Occupied territoryAlexander von FalkenhausenRoman Catholic (majority, unchanged); no state religion imposed
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Nazi Germany (1940–44)
Ruling house:House of Belgium

Who & why: Germany invades on 10 May 1940 without a declaration of war; King Leopold III, remaining with the army rather than following his government into exile, surrenders on 28 May 1940, while his ministers set up a government-in-exile in London. Belgium is placed under a German military governor (Militärverwaltung) rather than a puppet Belgian regime, a distinction from occupied countries that got collaborationist governments. Liberation begins with Brussels falling on 3 September 1944.

What it represents: the Belgian tricolor is suppressed and Nazi Germany's own flag flies in its place — no Belgian symbol of any kind is issued or tolerated by the occupying authority during these four years.

Kingdom of Belgium (interwar)

Sovereign stateAlbert ISecular (no state religion; Catholic majority)
Ruling house:House of Belgium

Who & why: King Albert I, hailed as a wartime hero for staying with his troops on the Yser front through the whole occupation, leads the country's reconstruction; Belgium receives war reparations and the small former German territories of Eupen and Malmedy under the 1919 Treaty of Versailles. Albert dies in a 1934 mountaineering accident; his son Leopold III inherits the throne five years before the next invasion.

What it represents: the same 1830 tricolor resumes the moment the German occupation ends, with no redesign — a deliberate statement of unbroken continuity after four years when the flag itself could not legally be flown.

German-occupied Belgium (Generalgouvernement)

Occupied territoryMoritz von BissingRoman Catholic (majority, unchanged); no state religion imposed
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: German Empire (1914–18)Emigration: wartime refugees (1914–18)
Ruling house:House of Belgium

Who & why: Germany invades neutral Belgium on 4 August 1914 as part of the Schlieffen Plan's route into France, triggering Britain's entry into the war over its own 1839 treaty guarantee of Belgian neutrality. Nearly the whole country falls under a German General Government within weeks; King Albert I and a sliver of unoccupied territory hold out behind the Yser river for the rest of the war, while the elected government relocates to Le Havre, France.

What it represents: the Imperial German tricolor administered from Brussels by a German governor-general, not a puppet Belgian regime — the legitimate Belgian government continues to exist throughout, just not on Belgian soil, a pattern the country repeats a generation later.

1830 – 1914

Kingdom of Belgium (founding era)

Sovereign stateLeopold ISecular (no state religion, 1831 constitution); Catholic majority
Migration & conquest:Emigration: rural Flemish emigration (late 19th c.)
Ruling house:House of Belgium

Who & why: independence is declared on 4 October 1830 after the August riot that grew into full revolution against King William I's rule; the National Congress adopts a constitutional monarchy and, unable to find a Belgian candidate the great powers would accept, offers the crown to Leopold of Saxe-Coburg, inaugurated as Leopold I on 21 July 1831 — still celebrated as Belgian National Day. Rapid industrialization follows, and Leopold II's brutal personal rule over the Congo Free State (1885–1908) becomes the era's darkest legacy before the state itself annexes it as the Belgian Congo in 1908.

What it represents: vertical black-yellow-red bands, adopted by the provisional government within days of the uprising — colors drawn from the old Duchy of Brabant's coat of arms, arranged vertically in deliberate homage to revolutionary France rather than the horizontal Dutch flag the rebels had just overthrown.

1815 – 1830

United Kingdom of the Netherlands

Personal unionWilliam I of Orange-NassauDutch Reformed Protestant (royal court); Catholic majority in the south
Ruling house:House of Orange-Nassau

Who & why: the Congress of Vienna, seeking a stronger buffer state against any future French expansion, merges the former Austrian Netherlands (Belgium) with the former Dutch Republic under King William I of Orange-Nassau, finalized through 1814–15. The union strains almost immediately: the largely Catholic, French- and Flemish-speaking south resents a Dutch Protestant king, an official Dutch-language policy, and chronic underrepresentation in government despite having the larger population.

What it represents: the same red-white-blue Dutch tricolor flown across both halves of the union — no distinct Belgian flag exists in this period, part of exactly the erasure of separate identity that fuels the 1830 revolution.

Who & why: Revolutionary France defeats Austrian forces at Fleurus in June 1794 and formally annexes the former Austrian Netherlands on 1 October 1795, dividing it into French administrative départements. 1714 – 1795

Austrian Netherlands

Habsburg ruleCharles VIRoman Catholic (Habsburg)

Who & why: the Spanish Habsburg line dies out with Charles II in 1700, and the resulting War of the Spanish Succession is settled by the treaties of Utrecht (1713) and Rastatt (1714): the Spanish throne goes to a Bourbon, but these provinces pass instead to the Austrian branch of the Habsburgs under Emperor Charles VI. Empress Maria Theresa's mid-century reforms modernize administration, and a brief 1789–90 revolt (the Brabant Revolution) briefly declares an independent United Belgian States before Austrian troops restore control — the first time the name "Belgian" is used for a state of its own, five years before it becomes permanent.

What it represents: still Habsburg, still flying the Cross of Burgundy on many occasions, but now under the Austrian rather than Spanish crown — shown here with the Habsburg imperial eagle to mark the dynastic shift, even though daily governance changes less than the ruling address does.

Spanish Netherlands

Habsburg rulePhilip IIRoman Catholic (Spanish crown, enforced)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Protestant refugees to the Dutch Republic (1580s–)
Ruling house:House of Habsburg

Who & why: when the northern provinces formally renounce Philip II in the 26 July 1581 Act of Abjuration, the Belgian provinces — Flanders, Brabant, Hainaut, and the rest — do not follow. Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, spends the early 1580s reconquering the south city by city for Spain, and it stays Habsburg and Catholic for the rest of the Eighty Years' War, formally confirmed at the 1648 Peace of Westphalia. Antwerp, cut off from the sea by a Dutch blockade of the Scheldt, declines sharply while Amsterdam rises in its place.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy — two red diagonal branches on white, the personal badge of the Dukes of Burgundy inherited by their Habsburg successors — used across Spain's entire empire, flown here specifically to mark the provinces that stayed loyal to Madrid rather than joining the Dutch Revolt.

Burgundian / Habsburg Netherlands

Habsburg rulePhilip the BoldRoman Catholic
Ruling house:House of Valois-BurgundyHouse of Habsburg

Who & why: Philip the Bold, Duke of Burgundy, becomes Count of Flanders in 1384 through his marriage to Margaret of Male — the founding acquisition of what becomes the core Belgian territory. His successors add the rest piece by piece through the early 15th century: Namur by purchase in 1421, Brabant and Limburg by inheritance in 1430, Hainaut in the early 1430s, so that by the reign of Philip the Good nearly every Belgian province answers to one Burgundian court at Brussels. The line passes by marriage to the Habsburgs in 1482, and Charles V's 1549 Pragmatic Sanction formally locks the whole bloc — Belgian and Dutch provinces alike — together as one indivisible inheritance, intact until the Dutch Revolt splits it in 1581.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy, the dukes' own personal badge, used as the common banner of their whole domain well before "Belgium" or "the Netherlands" existed as separate ideas — each province still kept its own local arms and Estates underneath this shared symbol.

Roman Gallia Belgica and the Frankish/feudal principalities

Roman province, then feudal principalitiesJulius Caesar (conqueror of the Belgae)Celtic/Roman paganism, then Christianity
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Rome (Julius Caesar, 57–51 BCE)

Who & why: Julius Caesar's Gallic Wars (58–51 BCE) conquer the Belgae, a coalition of Celtic tribes Caesar himself singled out as the fiercest fighters in all of Gaul, incorporating the region into the Roman province of Gallia Belgica — the direct source of the modern country's name. Roman rule lasts nearly five centuries until the Western Empire's collapse, after which Frankish Merovingian and then Carolingian kings absorb the region; Charlemagne's grandsons' 843 partition at Verdun splits the Low Countries between competing heirs, and out of that fragmented Carolingian inheritance a patchwork of feudal principalities emerges from the 9th century onward — the County of Flanders (Baldwin I, from 862), the Duchy of Brabant, the Prince-Bishopric of Liège, and the County of Hainaut foremost among them — each under its own count, duke, or bishop, with no shared government, until Philip the Bold's 1384 marriage begins consolidating them under one Burgundian court.

What it represents: no flag survives from Roman Gallia Belgica or the early feudal principalities in the modern territorial sense; Rome used legionary and provincial insignia, and the medieval counts, dukes, and bishops bore their own personal heraldic arms — Flanders' black lion on gold is attested only from the 12th century onward — rather than any single banner shared across the future Belgian territory. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the height of the Roman Empire (the Colosseum, built 70–80 CE, while Gallia Belgica was a quiet Roman province), the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476, Charlemagne's own 800 coronation as Holy Roman Emperor and his empire's 843 partition at Verdun, and the First Crusade's 1099 capture of Jerusalem — a campaign in which Flemish and Hainaut nobles were themselves prominent participants.
