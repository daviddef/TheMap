Flags of Benin

From the coastal Kingdom of Allada, built by Aja migrants from Tado and grown wealthy on early Atlantic trade, through the militarized inland offshoot kingdom it spun off at Abomey — a state that armed one of history's only regular corps of female soldiers and grew wealthier still shipping captives through the port of Ouidah — through half a century of French colonial rule, an unstable first independence, sixteen years as a self-declared Marxist-Leninist republic under a new name and a new flag, and finally a peaceful, negotiated return to the pan-African tricolor and multiparty rule — seven political eras across roughly five centuries, on a strip of West African coast that changed its own name once, in 1975, and never changed it back.

Benin's green-yellow-red tricolor, first raised in 1959 on the eve of independence, has flown over this territory for all but sixteen of the last sixty-six years — interrupted only by the plain green, red-starred banner of the People's Republic of Benin between 1975 and 1990.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Benin, c. 1500s–present, seven political entities. The kingdom of Allada, from which Dahomey's own founders broke away around 1625, opens the page; other contemporaneous Aja and Yoruba polities such as Porto-Novo remain outside this page's scope, which follows the single lineage that becomes independent Benin. 1990 – present

Republic of Benin (multiparty)

Republic, independentMathieu KérékouSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: labor migration to Nigeria, Gabon & France

Who & why: facing economic collapse and mounting unrest, President Mathieu Kérékou convenes the National Conference of the Living Forces of the Nation in February 1990, which strips the ruling party of its constitutional monopoly and charts a peaceful transition to multiparty democracy; a new constitution is adopted by referendum in December 1990. Kérékou himself loses the 1991 election and is later re-elected democratically in 1996, becoming one of Africa's rare peaceful reformed strongmen. The country keeps the name Benin, adopted in 1975.

What it represents: the original green-yellow-red tricolor, unused since 1975, is restored on 1 August 1990, the thirtieth anniversary of independence — green for hope and the palm groves of the south, yellow for the savanna wealth of the north, red for the courage of the ancestors.

People's Republic of Benin

Marxist-Leninist stateMathieu KérékouState atheism

Who & why: Major Mathieu Kérékou, who seized power in a 1972 military coup, formally commits the country to Marxist-Leninism and, on 30 November 1975, renames it the People's Republic of Benin — after the historic Bight of Benin, not the unrelated Benin City in neighboring Nigeria — distancing the state from the colonial-era name "Dahomey," which Kerekou's government associated with the old kingdom and its narrower ethnic base. The sole legal party, the People's Revolutionary Party of Benin, rules until 1990.

What it represents: the pan-African tricolor is set aside for a plain green field with a single red five-pointed star in the canton, a deliberate break from the old flag and a visual alignment with the socialist iconography of the era's other Marxist-Leninist states.

Republic of Dahomey (independent)

Republic, independentHubert MagaSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: full independence from France is proclaimed on 1 August 1960 under President Hubert Maga. The following decade is one of the era's most unstable in West Africa — six successful coups or changes of head of state between 1963 and 1972, driven by regional and ethnic rivalries among the country's three main political blocs. The cycle ends when Major Mathieu Kérékou seizes power in October 1972, setting the stage for the Marxist turn three years later.

What it represents: the green-yellow-red tricolor adopted the year before independence carries through the entire republic without change, even as the government above it changes hands repeatedly.

Autonomous Republic of Dahomey

Autonomous republicHubert MagaSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: under the new French Fifth Republic's constitution, Dahomey votes to join the French Community and becomes an autonomous republic on 4 December 1958, self-governing in domestic affairs while France retains defense and foreign policy. Full independence follows within two years, on 1 August 1960.

What it represents: the territorial assembly adopts a new national flag on 16 November 1959, choosing the pan-African green, yellow, and red — a design that, apart from one sixteen-year interruption, remains the country's flag to the present day.

French Dahomey (protectorate, then colony)

ColonyVictor BallotRoman Catholic (French colonial favor)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: France (1892–94)Emigration: civil servants across French West Africa
Ruling house:Kingdom of Dahomey

Who & why: the Second Franco-Dahomean War (1892–94) ends the kingdom's independence; King Béhanzin fights on but is defeated and exiled to Martinique in 1894. The territory is administered first as a French protectorate, then formalized as the colony of French Dahomey in 1904 and folded into the federation of French West Africa (Afrique Occidentale Française), governed from Dakar. It remains French colonial territory through both World Wars until constitutional reform in 1958.

What it represents: the French tricolor is the sole flag flown over the territory throughout this period; no distinct colonial flag for Dahomey existed.

c. 1625 – 1894

Kingdom of Dahomey

Kingdom, independentHouegbadjaVodun (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: enslaved people exported via Ouidah
Ruling house:Kingdom of Dahomey

Who & why: Fon-speaking Aja migrants under King Houegbadja found the kingdom around 1625 on the Abomey plateau, north of the coastal Bight of Benin (sources vary, some giving founding dates as late as the 1640s). Over the following two centuries Dahomey becomes one of West Africa's most centralized and militarized states, maintaining a regular corps of female soldiers, the Agojie — called the "Dahomey Amazons" by nineteenth-century Europeans — documented repeatedly by European visitors and unique among the era's standing armies. The kingdom's economy is heavily built on the transatlantic slave trade, exporting captives in large numbers through the port of Ouidah, one of the busiest slaving ports on the West African coast well into the nineteenth century. Dahomey resists French encroachment under King Béhanzin but is defeated in the Second Franco-Dahomean War of 1892–94.

What it represents: no single national flag in the European sense is documented; Dahomean kings instead flew appliqué-cloth banners bearing each ruler's own praise-symbol (a ship for King Agaja, a buffalo for King Ghezo, a shark for King Béhanzin), a living tradition still practiced by Abomey's appliqué artists today. The flag shown here is an informed reconstruction evoking that royal banner tradition's red field and emblem, not a transcription of any single attested flag.

Kingdom of Allada and the Aja migration from Tado

Kingdom, independent (Aja)No single ruler securely documentedVodun (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Migration: Aja peoples from Tado into the Bight of Benin

Who & why: oral tradition and early Portuguese and Dutch trade records place the Kingdom of Allada (also called Ardra), on the coastal plain of the Bight of Benin, as the dominant Aja-speaking power in the region from at least the early 16th century, descended from Aja peoples who had migrated south from Tado, on the Togolese plateau. Allada grows wealthy as a coastal middleman in the expanding Atlantic trade, hosting European factors from the early 1600s. Dynastic rivalry within the royal family produces breakaway offshoots in the early 17th century: one founds Porto-Novo on the coast, and around 1625 another, led by Houegbadja, founds the inland kingdom of Dahomey at Abomey covered in the following entry.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from Allada; the kingdom is known through Portuguese, Dutch, and later French trade records rather than any vexillological source, and its own founding ruler is not attested with certainty across the surviving accounts. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the Portuguese and Dutch exploration and trading-post era along the West African coast, the height of the Songhai Empire in the western Sahel before its 1591 collapse to Moroccan invasion, and the earliest English and French colonial ventures in the Americas — the same Atlantic trading world that would soon draw Allada's own offshoot kingdom of Dahomey into the transatlantic slave trade.
