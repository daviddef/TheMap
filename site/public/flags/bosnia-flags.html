Flags of the Bosna Valley

A medieval kingdom's golden lilies, four centuries under the Ottomans, an Austro-Hungarian bicolor invented to avoid favoring Vienna or Budapest, a wartime revival of the old lily arms, and finally a flag no Bosnian parliament actually voted for — imposed by a Spanish diplomat in 1998 because the country's own institutions couldn't agree.

Nowhere else in this atlas is the flag itself so openly a negotiation between rival claims — blue and gold chosen specifically because they belonged to no one.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Bosnia and Herzegovina, c. 9 CE–present, ten political entities. Part of a larger atlas covering the former Yugoslav lands — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

1998 – present

Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina

Sovereign state
Migration & conquest:Emigration: postwar diaspora to Western Europe & beyond

Who & why: the Dayton Peace Agreement (1995) left Bosnia's parliament unable to agree on a flag — a February 1998 vote produced only a plurality. High Representative Carlos Westendorp, the internationally mandated overseer of Dayton's civilian implementation, used his "Bonn Powers" to impose this flag by decree on 4 February 1998, refining the commission's design with vexillologist Jos Poels.

What it represents: blue and yellow were chosen specifically because neither side claimed them — avoiding red, white, and the specific devices (fleur-de-lis, šahovnica, Serbian tricolor) fought over by the country's three constituent peoples. The triangle's three sides represent the three peoples and the wedge shape of the country itself; the stars, cut off top and bottom to suggest infinity, echo the European flag as a gesture toward EU integration.

1992 – 1998

Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina (war-era flag)

Sovereign stateAlija IzetbegovićSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Republika Srpska / Herzeg-Bosna forcesEmigration: wartime refugees & displaced (1992–95)

Who & why: adopted 4 May 1992, weeks after the independence referendum, by President Alija Izetbegović's government as war broke out between the Bosnian government, the breakaway Republika Srpska, and Croat-held Herzeg-Bosnia. Actual wartime control was fragmented among all three through 1995.

What it represents: a direct, deliberate revival of the medieval Kingdom of Bosnia's fleur-de-lis arms on white — asserting a specifically Bosnian statehood rooted in the Kotromanić line, distinct from Serb or Croat national claims. Bosnian Serbs and Croats saw it as insufficiently inclusive of their own communities, a grievance that fed directly into its 1998 replacement.

1945 – 1992

Socialist Republic of Bosnia and Herzegovina

Socialist republicĐuro PucarState atheism
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Gastarbeiter to Western Europe (1960s–80s)

Who & why: one of six constituent republics of Tito's SFR Yugoslavia, its borders matching the modern state almost exactly — one of the few times in the 20th century Bosnia existed as a bounded administrative unit corresponding to today's country.

What it represents: unlike Croatia, Slovenia, or Serbia, Bosnia's socialist flag did not revive a historic national tricolor — there wasn't a settled one to revive across its mixed Bosniak, Serb, and Croat population. Instead, a plain red field (for the "brotherhood and unity" of socialist liberation) carried the small federal Yugoslav tricolor-and-star in the canton.

1941 – 1945

Bosnia under the Independent State of Croatia (NDH)

Occupation / puppet stateAnte PavelićRoman Catholicism (state-favored church)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: NDH / Nazi Germany & Fascist ItalyEmigration: genocide & deportation of Jews and Serbs

Who & why: Ante Pavelić's Ustaše regime, installed by Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy after the April 1941 invasion, annexed the whole of Bosnia and Herzegovina into the NDH — Ustaše ideology claimed Bosnian Muslims as "the purest Croats." No separate Bosnian flag existed; some autonomist proposals from local political factions never produced an official flag.

What it represents: the same red-white-blue tricolor with the Croatian checkerboard (first square white) and Ustaše "U" monogram flown over Croatia proper — documented in the Croatia atlas — imposed here as an assertion of "Greater Croatia" over a multi-confessional Bosnian population.

1918 – 1941

Bosnia within the Kingdom of SHS / Yugoslavia

Kingdom (in union)Alexander I KarađorđevićEastern Orthodoxy (state-privileged church)

Who & why: Bosnia joined the new South Slav kingdom in 1918 with no separate status; the 1929 royal-dictatorship reorganization split it across several banovinas (Vrbas, Drina, Zeta) that ignored its historic borders entirely, with a western slice later folded into the 1939 Banovina of Croatia.

What it represents: simply the kingdom's own blue-white-red tricolor, flown uniformly — Bosnia's centuries-old distinct territorial identity administratively erased in favor of a unitary "Yugoslav" ideal.

1878 – 1918

Austro-Hungarian Occupation

Occupied provinceFranz Joseph IRoman Catholicism (state church)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Austria-HungaryEmigration: Muslim muhacir exodus to Ottoman landsImmigration: Austro-Hungarian agricultural colonists
Ruling house:House of Habsburg

Who & why: the 1878 Congress of Berlin handed Bosnia to Austro-Hungarian administration; it was run as a corpus separatum — belonging to neither Austria nor Hungary — by the joint Imperial-Royal Ministry of Finance, then formally annexed in 1908, triggering the Bosnian Annexation Crisis.

What it represents: a deliberately neutral bicolor, red over gold for Bosnia (Herzegovina flew it reversed), with arms revived from the medieval Bosnian/Herzegovinian noble Stjepan Vukčić Hrvatinić — from whose title "Duke's Land" Herzegovina takes its name — used to lend the new administration historical legitimacy while avoiding identification with either half of the Dual Monarchy.

1463 – 1878

Ottoman Bosnia

Occupied provinceMehmed IISunni Islam (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Ottoman EmpireImmigration: Sephardic Jews to SarajevoEmigration: Christians fleeing to the Habsburg Military Frontier
Ruling house:House of Osman (Ottoman Sultanate)

Who & why: Ottoman conquest absorbed Bosnia as a sanjak, later elevated to an eyalet and, after the 1860s Tanzimat reforms, the Vilayet of Bosnia, administered from Travnik and later Sarajevo. For most of this period no distinct "Bosnia" flag existed — provinces flew the general Ottoman red flag with white crescent and star.

What it represents: the flag shown is the exception: during the 1831–32 uprising, Husein-kapetan Gradaščević ("the Dragon of Bosnia") led local Muslim notables against Sultan Mahmud II's centralizing reforms under a green flag with a gold crescent and star — asserting Bosnian autonomy within an Ottoman/Islamic visual idiom, the closest thing to a distinct Bosnian flag before the 20th century.

1377 – 1463

Kingdom of Bosnia

KingdomTvrtko I KotromanićRoman Catholicism (royal faith)
Ruling house:House of Kotromanić

Who & why: Tvrtko I Kotromanić was crowned "King of the Serbs, Bosnia, the Seaside and the Western Lands" on 26 October 1377 at the Mile monastery near Visoko, claiming continuity with the old Nemanjić line. At its height under Tvrtko the kingdom reached the Adriatic coast south of the Neretva and into western Serbia and Dalmatia — its largest extent ever.

What it represents: the Kotromanić lily arms, unchanged, now carrying the prestige of a full kingdom rather than a vassal banate. The state fragmented after Tvrtko's death in 1391; the Ottomans took the last royal seat, Jajce, in 1463 (Herzegovina held out under the Kosača family until 1482).

c. 1154 – 1377

Banate of Bosnia

BanateBan BorićBosnian Church (tolerated)
Ruling house:House of Kotromanić

Who & why: the House of Kotromanić, ruling as bans (semi-autonomous under Hungarian and Byzantine overlordship at various points), built a distinct Bosnian polity centered on the Bosna river valley. Ban Stjepan II Kotromanić (r. 1322–1353) expanded it into Hum (Herzegovina) and the Dalmatian hinterland.

What it represents: six gold fleurs-de-lis on a blue shield split by a white diagonal bend — adopted in homage to the Angevin (Anjou) kings of Hungary, whose backing helped the Kotromanići hold power. Later, popular tradition read the lily as the native "golden lily of Bosnia" wildflower, though the heraldic original is French in origin.

c. 9 CE – 1154

Illyrian Dalmatia, Slavic settlement, and early medieval Bosona

Roman province, later contested Slavic borderlandNo individually named local ruler documentedIllyrian/Roman paganism, then Christianization (4th–9th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Rome (completed 9 CE)Migration: South Slavic settlement (6th–7th c.)

Who & why: centuries before any Bosnian state existed, this territory is Illyrian tribal land, subdued by Rome in a series of wars beginning in 168 BCE and fully absorbed into the province of Dalmatia by 9 CE, after Tiberius crushed the Great Illyrian Revolt. South Slavic peoples migrate into the region from the 6th–7th centuries, gradually absorbing the Romanized Illyrian population; the name of the land itself first survives in writing only around 950 CE, when Byzantine Emperor Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus's De Administrando Imperio mentions a small inhabited district called "Bosona." For two more centuries it remains a contested borderland among Byzantium, Hungary, Croatia, and Serbia's regional lords, with no independently attested ruler by name until Ban Borić appears around 1154.

What it represents: no flag or comparable banner is documented for Illyrian, Roman-provincial, or early Slavic Bosona; Rome's provincial administration flew its own legionary and imperial standards rather than any symbol specific to this land, and no early Slavic banner survives. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs from the tail end of Rome's Republican-era Illyrian Wars and the height of the Roman Empire, through the fall of the Western Roman Empire (476), Emperor Justinian's 6th-century Byzantine reconquests, the founding and rapid expansion of Islam under the Umayyad and Abbasid Caliphates, Charlemagne's 800 coronation, and the 1054 Great Schism that formalized the Catholic–Orthodox split running straight through the Balkans.
