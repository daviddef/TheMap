Flags of Brazil

Pedro Álvares Cabral's landing on the Brazilian coast in April 1500 opens three centuries of Portuguese colonization — but Brazil's own story soon runs strange: when Napoleon's armies invaded Portugal in 1807, the entire Portuguese royal court fled across the Atlantic and set up the seat of empire in Rio de Janeiro, the only time a European colonial power actually governed from inside its own colony. In 1815 that arrangement was made formal, with colonial Brazil elevated to a co-equal kingdom alongside Portugal itself.

When independence came in 1822, Brazil took a path no neighbor followed: it stayed a monarchy, under Pedro I and then Pedro II, while Spanish South America fractured into republics around it. Not until 1889 did a republic finally arrive — and the flag adopted that year carries a genuinely documented detail: its 27 white stars reproduce the exact configuration of the night sky over Rio de Janeiro on 15 November 1889, the day the republic was proclaimed, each star standing for a state or federal district and the constellation updated as Brazil's internal map grew, most recently in 1992.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Brazil, c. 1500–present, five entries from pre-contact indigenous nations through colony, accidental co-equal kingdom, independent empire, and republic. Brazil's federal states have been added and adjusted over time (most recently in 1992), but that is a change of internal composition within one continuous republic, not a governing split, so — consistent with this atlas's practice of only mapping genuine internal partitions — this page carries no internal-partition map. 1889 – present

Federative Republic of Brazil

RepublicDeodoro da FonsecaSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Japanese, Italian & Levantine settlers (1889–1930s)

Who & why: a military coup led by Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca deposes Pedro II on 15 November 1889 and proclaims the republic, ending 67 years of monarchy. The 20th century that follows is turbulent — the Vargas era of the 1930s–40s, then a military dictatorship from 1964 to 1985 — but through all of it the republican flag itself never changes, so this single entry spans the whole span from proclamation to today.

What it represents: green field (echoing the old imperial green), gold rhombus, and a blue celestial globe bearing 27 white five-pointed stars in the exact arrangement of the sky over Rio de Janeiro on the night of the proclamation, one star per state and the Federal District, plus the motto "Ordem e Progresso" (Order and Progress), drawn from Auguste Comte's Positivist slogan.

1822 – 1889

Empire of Brazil

Empire, independentPedro IRoman Catholic (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Italian & German settlers; continued enslaved-African trade (to c. 1850)
Ruling house:House of Braganza

Who & why: Prince Pedro, regent of Brazil, declares independence from Portugal on 7 September 1822 on the banks of the Ipiranga (the "Grito do Ipiranga") and is crowned Pedro I, Emperor of Brazil. He abdicates in 1831 in favor of his five-year-old son, who reigns as Pedro II from 1840 until the 1889 republican coup. Uniquely among Brazil's South American neighbors — all of which broke from Spain as republics — independent Brazil kept its monarchy for 67 years.

What it represents: green field for the House of Braganza (Pedro's dynasty) and gold rhombus for the House of Habsburg (via his wife, Empress Leopoldina), with a blue armillary sphere — a navigational device tied to Portugal's maritime past — ringed by 19 stars for the empire's provinces and topped with the imperial crown.

1815 – 1822

United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves

Kingdom, in unionJohn VIRoman Catholic (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Portuguese royal court & nobility (1808)
Ruling house:House of Braganza

Who & why: when Napoleon's forces invaded Portugal in 1807, the Portuguese royal family and court fled across the Atlantic and, by 1808, were governing the empire from Rio de Janeiro — a genuinely unusual reversal, the only case of a European colonial power's own capital operating out of its colony. In 1815 King John VI formalized this by elevating Brazil from a colony to a kingdom co-equal with Portugal itself, joined under one crown as the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves.

What it represents: Brazil did not yet have a flag distinct from Portugal's own royal banner during this brief seven-year union; shown here is a simplified approximation of the Portuguese royal arms and colors of the period, standing in for the shared sovereign's flag rather than any dedicated Brazilian design.

1500 – 1815

Colonial Brazil (Portuguese America)

ColonyPedro Álvares CabralRoman Catholic (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: PortugalImmigration: enslaved Africans (transatlantic slave trade)
Ruling house:House of Aviz (Portugal)House of Habsburg (Iberian Union)House of Braganza

Who & why: Pedro Álvares Cabral lands on the Brazilian coast in April 1500 and claims the territory for the Portuguese crown. Over the following three centuries, Portugal builds a colonial economy first on sugar and later on gold and diamonds, run under royal charters and later direct crown administration — a single, continuous Portuguese-speaking colonial track, in contrast to the Spanish colonial administrations that produced every other country in this hub.

What it represents: no single fixed flag flew over colonial Brazil for this entire 315-year span; ships and colonial authorities generally flew variants of the Portuguese royal arms and ensigns of the period, which changed repeatedly in Portugal itself, rather than any dedicated Brazilian colonial banner. Shown here honestly as a variable, approximate emblem rather than a fabricated fixed flag.

to 1500

Pre-colonial indigenous peoples

No unified polityNo single ruler documentedDiverse indigenous belief systems

Who & why: before European contact, the territory now called Brazil was home to a wide diversity of indigenous nations — Tupi and Guaraní peoples along the coast and in the interior, along with many other distinct groups across the Amazon basin and beyond — organized into villages, kinship networks, and regional alliances rather than any single overarching state.

What it represents: no unified polity existed to fly a single flag, and no fixed heraldic banner is documented for any of these nations individually. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
