Flags of Bulgaria

From a pre-heraldic Bulgar khanate crossing the Danube in 681, through Byzantine absorption, a golden-lion medieval empire, nearly five Ottoman centuries, a treaty that split the newly-freed nation in two, and a Cold War star that never fully displaced the tricolor beneath it — eleven flags, and a white-green-red banner that has been Bulgaria's own since 1878.

The Congress of Berlin's 1878 settlement is the hinge of this whole story: it freed Bulgaria from Ottoman rule but split it into two separately governed pieces on the spot — the autonomous Principality in the north, and the separately administered province of Eastern Rumelia in the south — reunited only seven years later, by a local coup rather than by any treaty.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Bulgaria, c. 632–present, twelve political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Principality and Eastern Rumelia, 1878–1885

Bulgaria's own real border, split into the two pieces the Congress of Berlin created on the spot in July 1878: the fully autonomous Principality of Bulgaria north of the Balkan Mountains, and the separately administered autonomous Ottoman province of Eastern Rumelia to the south. Plovdiv answered to a different government than Sofia for barely seven years. Eastern Rumelia had its own governor-general, its own militia, and its own institutions, distinct from the Principality's — until a bloodless coup in Plovdiv on 6 September 1885 declared union anyway, no treaty required. Republic of Bulgaria

RepublicZhelyu ZhelevEastern Orthodox (traditional, not established)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: post-1989 economic emigration to Western Europe & the US

Who & why: Todor Zhivkov, in power since 1954, is forced to resign on 10 November 1989 as the wider Eastern Bloc collapses. Round-table talks lead to a new democratic constitution, adopted 12 July 1991, formally establishing the current Republic of Bulgaria. NATO membership follows in 2004, EU membership in 2007.

What it represents: the same white-green-red tricolor first adopted in 1878, simply stripped of the communist-era state emblem. The 1991 constitution fixes the plain tricolor as the national flag, keeping the coat of arms as a separate state symbol rather than part of the flag itself.

1946 – 1989

People's Republic of Bulgaria

Communist stateGeorgi DimitrovState atheism
Migration & conquest:Emigration: expulsion of ethnic Turks to Turkey (1989)

Who & why: a rigged September 1946 referendum, over 95% reported "yes," formally abolishes the monarchy under the Soviet-backed Fatherland Front government; nine-year-old King Simeon II is exiled with his family. Georgi Dimitrov's Communists tighten one-party rule through the following years, and Todor Zhivkov governs from 1954 almost unbroken until 1989.

What it represents: the historic tricolor keeps its familiar stripes but gains a state emblem — a wreath of wheat sheaves, a five-pointed red star, and a dam and factory representing industrialization — introduced in 1948 and refined again in 1967 (simplified here as a star). Unlike the Hungarian Soviet Republic's blank red flag decades earlier, Bulgaria's communist government never abandons its national colors.

Fatherland Front government (Soviet-occupied transition)

Occupied territoryKimon GeorgievEastern Orthodox (nominal, still established)
Ruling house:House of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha

Who & why: the USSR declares war on Bulgaria on 5 September 1944 and the Red Army crosses the border days later; on 9 September a leftist Fatherland Front coalition seizes power in a coup, immediately turning Bulgaria's army against Germany for the war's final months. King Simeon II — still a child after his father Boris III's sudden death in August 1943 — remains nominal monarch under a three-man regency, but real power rests with the Communist-dominated Front and the occupying Soviet military.

What it represents: the plain tricolor returns, the royal crown-and-lion state emblem stripped from the flag within days of the coup even though the monarchy itself isn't formally abolished for another two years — a flag caught between two systems.

1941 – 1944

Kingdom of Bulgaria (Axis ally)

Axis allyBoris IIIEastern Orthodox (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: deportation of Jews from occupied Macedonia & Thrace
Ruling house:House of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha

Who & why: Tsar Boris III, having resisted German pressure for over a year, allows Bulgaria to join the Tripartite Pact on 1 March 1941, chiefly to recover territory lost after WWI — Southern Dobruja is returned by Romania that same year, and Bulgaria occupies most of Vardar Macedonia and western Thrace. Bulgaria never declares war on the USSR, and Boris III's government notably resists German pressure to deport Bulgaria's own Jewish citizens (roughly 48,000 saved), even as it fails to prevent deportations from the occupied Macedonian and Thracian territories it administers. Boris III dies suddenly on 28 August 1943, weeks after a tense meeting with Hitler; his six-year-old son Simeon II becomes tsar under a regency.

What it represents: the same royal tricolor as the preceding era, unchanged by the alliance — unlike the NDH or Vichy, no new symbolism is imposed; the same government that had flown this flag for decades makes the Axis choice.

1908 – 1941

Kingdom of Bulgaria (Third Bulgarian Kingdom)

Sovereign kingdomFerdinand IEastern Orthodox (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Bulgarian-Greek population exchange (1919–)
Ruling house:House of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha

Who & why: on 22 September 1908, Prince Ferdinand I proclaims full independence at Veliko Tarnovo and takes the title Tsar, ending even nominal Ottoman suzerainty. The kingdom fights two Balkan Wars in 1912–13 — first winning large territory from a collapsing Ottoman Empire, then losing much of it to former allies Serbia, Greece, and Romania in the disastrous "Second Balkan War" — and then WWI on the losing Central Powers side, ending with further territorial loss at the 1919 Treaty of Neuilly.

What it represents: the tricolor gains a royal crown atop the state coat of arms — a golden lion rampant, itself dating to medieval heraldic tradition — marking the formal shift from an internationally-tributary "prince" to a fully sovereign "tsar."

Principality of Bulgaria / Eastern Rumelia (split era)

Autonomous principalityAlexander of BattenbergEastern Orthodox (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Muslim/Turkish exodus after the Russo-Turkish War
Ruling house:House of Battenberg

Who & why: the July 1878 Congress of Berlin, redrawing the Balkan settlement imposed weeks earlier at San Stefano, creates two separate Bulgarian-majority territories: a fully autonomous Principality of Bulgaria north of the Balkan Mountains (Sofia its capital from 1879), and Eastern Rumelia, a separately administered autonomous Ottoman province to the south, with its own governor-general and institutions based in Plovdiv. See the map above for how the two pieces were governed apart until 1885.

What it represents: the Principality adopts the plain white-green-red tricolor almost immediately — the same colors and order Bulgaria's flag keeps to this day. Eastern Rumelia, a province rather than a state of its own, flies a separate green flag in the meantime.

1396 – 1878

Ottoman rule

Occupied territoryBayezid ISunni Islam (Ottoman state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Ottoman EmpireImmigration: Turkish & Yörük settlersEmigration: refugees fleeing to Russia & Wallachia

Who & why: the medieval Bulgarian state ends with the fall of Vidin in 1396, the last independent Bulgarian stronghold, completing an Ottoman conquest that began with Tarnovo's fall in 1393. Bulgaria is absorbed into Ottoman provincial administration for nearly five centuries — organized into sanjaks, and after 1864 into the reformed Danube Vilayet — punctuated by periodic revolts, most famously the April Uprising of 1876, brutally suppressed, which shocked European opinion and helped trigger the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 that finally ends Ottoman rule here.

What it represents: the standard Ottoman red field with crescent and star — no distinct Bulgarian flag exists through this entire span, the longest single era in this timeline.

1185 – 1396

Second Bulgarian Empire

EmpireIvan Asen IEastern Orthodox (state religion)
Ruling house:House of AsenHouse of Shishman

Who & why: brothers Ivan Asen I and Peter IV lead an uprising against Byzantine rule starting in 1185, restoring an independent Bulgarian state within a few years. The empire peaks under Ivan Asen II (r. 1218–41), reaching from the Adriatic to the Black Sea, before fragmenting under pressure from Mongol raids, internal rivalries, and a resurgent Ottoman advance from the 1350s on; Tarnovo, the capital, falls in 1393, and the last holdout at Vidin falls in 1396.

What it represents: no contemporaneous flag survives, but a gold lion on a dark red field is the standard modern heraldic reconstruction tied to the Asen dynasty and later adopted as Bulgaria's own national emblem — shown here as an informed reconstruction, not an attested medieval banner.

1018 – 1185

Byzantine rule

Occupied territoryBasil IIEastern Orthodox (Byzantine state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Byzantine Empire

Who & why: Emperor Basil II, remembered as the "Bulgar-Slayer," completes his long conquest of the First Bulgarian Empire in 1018 after defeating Tsar Samuil's forces at Kleidion in 1014 — the mass blinding of Samuil's captured soldiers there is the episode that earned Basil his epithet. Byzantine provincial administration governs the territory for the next century and a half, until the Asen brothers' uprising of 1185.

What it represents: no distinct Bulgarian flag exists under direct Byzantine rule; shown here with a schematic imperial device rather than any Bulgarian symbol, since no Bulgarian state or banner exists at all through this span.

c. 681 – 1018

First Bulgarian Empire

Early empireAsparuhPagan (Tengrism/Slavic), later Eastern Orthodox from 864/865
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Bulgar migration across the Danube

Who & why: Khan Asparuh leads Bulgar forces across the Danube and, after defeating a Byzantine army in 680, secures a treaty in 681 recognizing an independent state south of the river — traditionally treated as the founding date of the First Bulgarian Empire. Khan Boris I converts to Christianity in 864/865, and under Tsar Simeon I (r. 893–927) the empire reaches its medieval territorial and cultural peak, becoming a major rival to Byzantium itself, before a long decline through the 10th century ends in Basil II's 1018 conquest.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic era; contemporary sources describe banners and totemic standards rather than a fixed territorial flag. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 632 – 681

Old Great Bulgaria (Kubrat's khanate)

Khanate (steppe confederation)KubratTengrism (Bulgar paganism); Kubrat personally baptized
Migration & conquest:Formation and fragmentation: Bulgar khanate north of the Black Sea (c. 632–668)Migration: Asparuh's Bulgars southwest to the Danube (668–681)

Who & why: before Asparuh's Bulgars cross the Danube, the Bulgar tribes are united north of the Black Sea and Sea of Azov under Khan Kubrat, who breaks free of Avar overlordship around 632 and rules "Old Great Bulgaria" as an ally of Byzantine Emperor Heraclius. On Kubrat's death, c. 668, the rising power of the Khazar khaganate to the east fractures his khanate among his sons; one, Asparuh, leads his branch west and eventually south across the Danube, founding the state recognized by treaty in 681. The land itself already carried a much longer documented past by this point — Thracian tribes had inhabited it since at least the early Iron Age, and it had been the Roman, then Byzantine, province of Moesia for roughly seven centuries before the Bulgars ever arrived.

What it represents: no flag or comparable banner survives, or is likely to have existed, from Old Great Bulgaria; Byzantine and other contemporary sources describe standards and tribal totems, not a fixed territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Kubrat's khanate rises in the same years as the Byzantine Empire's desperate wars against Sassanid Persia and, from the 630s, the explosive early Arab conquests that take Jerusalem (637) and Alexandria (641) within Kubrat's own lifetime; its fragmentation under Khazar pressure in the 660s comes as the Umayyad Caliphate consolidates its hold on the wider Middle East, a generation before Asparuh's Bulgars ever reach the Danube.
