Flags of Cambodia

From Funan and Chenla, the Indian-influenced pre-Angkor kingdoms known almost entirely through Chinese envoys' records, through the Khmer Empire, whose temple-city of Angkor was once the largest pre-industrial urban complex on Earth, centuries of decline, French colonization, independence, a brief republic, the Khmer Rouge genocide, and a long transition back to constitutional monarchy — eight entries across one of Southeast Asia's most dramatic national histories.

Angkor Wat itself has appeared on nearly every Cambodian flag since independence, through governments as different as the royalist kingdom, the Khmer Republic, the Khmer Rouge, and the Vietnamese-backed People's Republic — the one constant symbol across a turbulent century.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Cambodia, c. 1st century CE–present, eight political entries. The 1975–1979 Khmer Rouge period is stated plainly and factually, weighted appropriately to its documented scale, consistent with this atlas's established standard for this exact topic. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

One Khmer border, seven regimes

Cambodia's own real border, traced whole — the heartland of the Khmer Empire and, ever since, a single kingdom or state governed as one unit through every regime change this page records.

No internal partition exists here to show. Every government that has ruled Cambodia — kingdom, protectorate, republic, Khmer Rouge state, or restored monarchy — governed the whole of this same territory at once; there is no genuinely separate internal Cambodian region to carve out honestly. What changes here is only who held the whole border at once.

Kingdom of Cambodia (restored)

Kingdom, constitutionalNorodom SihanoukTheravada Buddhism (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: refugees repatriated from Thai camps (1992–93)
Ruling house:House of Norodom

Who & why: UN-supervised elections in 1993, following the 1991 Paris Peace Accords that ended over a decade of civil war, restore the monarchy under King Norodom Sihanouk as a constitutional figurehead within a multiparty parliamentary system, though real political power has since remained concentrated under long-serving Prime Minister Hun Sen and, from 2023, his son Hun Manet.

What it represents: the same essential design used under the earlier 1953–1970 kingdom — blue and red bands with a white three-towered silhouette of Angkor Wat centered on red — readopted as the restored kingdom's flag.

People's Republic of Kampuchea / State of Cambodia

Republic, socialistHeng SamrinState atheism, easing toward Buddhism
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Vietnam (1979–89)Emigration: to Thai border refugee camps

Who & why: Vietnam invades in December 1978 and topples the Khmer Rouge in January 1979, installing a Vietnamese-backed government under former Khmer Rouge officers who had defected; the ousted Khmer Rouge continue a guerrilla insurgency from border areas, still recognized by the UN as Cambodia's legitimate government for years afterward. The state renames itself Cambodia in 1989, and the Paris Peace Accords finally end the civil war in 1991, opening the way to the 1993 UN-supervised elections.

What it represents: a gold five-tower silhouette of Angkor Wat on plain red, a socialist-era design keeping the temple motif that outlasts every regime around it.

1975 – 1979

Democratic Kampuchea (Khmer Rouge)

Totalitarian regimePol PotState atheism (religion abolished)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: fleeing to Vietnam

Who & why: the Khmer Rouge, led by Pol Pot, take the capital Phnom Penh on 17 April 1975 and immediately begin a radical, brutal restructuring of society, forcibly evacuating cities and abolishing money, religion, and private property in pursuit of an agrarian utopia. Over the next three years and nine months, an estimated 1.5 to 2 million people — roughly a quarter of the population — die from execution, forced labor, and starvation, until Vietnam's December 1978 invasion topples the regime in January 1979.

What it represents: a plain red field with a yellow silhouette of Angkor Wat's three central towers, the temple imagery retained even by a regime that otherwise sought to erase Cambodia's existing institutions and culture.

Khmer Republic (Lon Nol)

RepublicLon NolTheravada Buddhism (state religion)

Who & why: General Lon Nol overthrows Prince Norodom Sihanouk in a March 1970 coup, abolishing the monarchy and proclaiming the Khmer Republic amid escalating spillover from the neighboring Vietnam War, including heavy US bombing of Cambodian territory used by North Vietnamese forces. The republic proves unable to contain the growing Khmer Rouge insurgency, and Phnom Penh falls to them on 17 April 1975.

What it represents: a blue field bordered in red with the white silhouette of Angkor Wat centered, the new republic's chosen break from the earlier kingdom's flag while still keeping the temple emblem.

Kingdom, independentNorodom SihanoukTheravada Buddhism (state religion)

Who & why: King Norodom Sihanouk secures full independence from France on 9 November 1953, through a mix of diplomacy and his own "royal crusade for independence" campaign, then abdicates in 1955 to lead the country as an elected, still-dominant political figure through the following decade and a half of Cold War neutrality.

French Protectorate of Cambodia

Colonial (France)Norodom ITheravada Buddhism (retained under protectorate)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Vietnamese civil servants & plantation workers
Ruling house:House of NorodomHouse of Sisowath

Who & why: King Norodom I accepts French protection in 1863, chiefly to check encroachment by neighboring Siam and Vietnam, and France formally folds Cambodia into French Indochina in 1887. Japan occupies the protectorate alongside the rest of Indochina from 1941, briefly installing a Japanese-backed independent government under King Norodom Sihanouk in March 1945 before French administration is restored after the war, holding on until full independence in 1953.

What it represents: the plain French tricolor, the colonial power's own national flag, with the Cambodian monarchy retained as a French-supervised figurehead throughout.

c. 802 – 1863

Khmer Empire (Angkor) and later decline

Empire, independentJayavarman IIHinduism, later Theravada Buddhism (from c. 13th century)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Ayutthaya (Siam), 1431
Ruling house:House of Norodom

Who & why: Jayavarman II's self-proclamation as a universal monarch around 802 traditionally marks the founding of the Khmer Empire, which by the 12th and 13th centuries, under rulers like Suryavarman II and Jayavarman VII, builds Angkor Wat and the wider Angkor complex — then the largest pre-industrial urban settlement on Earth. Repeated wars with neighboring Ayutthaya from the 1400s onward, and the abandonment of Angkor itself in 1431, mark a long decline during which Cambodia becomes a shrinking, embattled kingdom squeezed between more powerful Siamese and Vietnamese neighbors, eventually seeking French protection in 1863.

What it represents: no securely attested flag survives from the Khmer Empire or its long post-Angkor decline; the empire expressed authority through temple inscriptions and royal iconography rather than a fixed territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Funan and Chenla (pre-Angkor kingdoms)

Kingdom, independentKaundinya (legendary founder)Hinduism (Indian-influenced)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Indian traders & Brahmins (1st c. CE–)Absorbed by: Chenla, c. mid-6th century

Who & why: the earliest known state in what is now Cambodia is recorded almost entirely through Chinese envoys' accounts: Funan, centered on the lower Mekong delta and its port of Oc Eo, appears in Chinese chronicles from the 3rd century CE, with a founding legend crediting the Indian Brahmin Kaundinya and local ruler Liuye. From the mid-6th century Funan is gradually superseded by a successor polity Chinese sources call Chenla — later split by those same sources into competing "land" and "water" factions — whose major sites include the brick temple complex of Sambor Prei Kuk (Isanapura), built under King Isanavarman I in the early 7th century. Jayavarman II's unification of these fragmented Chenla territories around 802 traditionally marks the end of this six-century span and the start of the Khmer Empire proper.

What it represents: no flag or banner survives from either Funan or Chenla, and none is likely to have existed in any modern sense; both kingdoms projected royal authority through Sanskrit inscriptions, temple architecture, and Indian-derived titles rather than a woven or painted standard. Shown here honestly blank.

World context: archaeologists have recovered Roman gold medallions from the reigns of Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius at Funan's port of Oc Eo, physical evidence that Indian Ocean trade networks reached the Roman Mediterranean by the 2nd century CE; Funan's later centuries overlap the fall of the Han dynasty in China (220 CE), and Chenla's own span runs alongside the life of Muhammad and the founding of Islam in the early 7th century and the height of Tang dynasty China, Cambodia's own historical trading partner and the source of most surviving written accounts of this era.
