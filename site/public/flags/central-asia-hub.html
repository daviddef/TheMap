Flags of Central Asia

Five countries at the heart of the Eurasian landmass, bound together by geography and history more tightly than almost any other region in this atlas. For over a thousand years the region's oases and steppe corridors carried the Silk Road's trade between China, Persia, and Europe, and its cities — Samarkand, Bukhara, Khiva, Merv — grew wealthy as caravan hubs and centers of Islamic scholarship. Four of the five peoples here, the Kazakhs, Uzbeks, Turkmen, and Kyrgyz, are Turkic, descended from waves of nomadic and settled Turkic migration across the steppe; the Tajiks are the exception, a Persian-speaking, Iranian people whose language and culture trace instead to the old Persianate civilization of the region's river valleys.

Genghis Khan's Mongol armies swept through in the early 13th century, folding the region into the Mongol Empire and its successor khanates. Timur (Tamerlane), a Turco-Mongol conqueror who claimed descent from Genghis Khan's line, built a new empire from Samarkand in the late 14th century, and the city's Registan and Timurid architecture still stand as the era's monument. Centuries later, the region became the central battleground of the 19th-century "Great Game," the rivalry between the Russian and British empires for influence in Central and South Asia; Russia annexed the region's khanates and steppe in stages from the 1860s to the 1890s. Soviet planners then redrew the map entirely: the "national delimitation" of the 1920s–30s carved the five modern republics out of a region whose ethnic and linguistic groups had never mapped cleanly onto fixed borders, and those Soviet-drawn lines are the borders these five countries hold today. All five declared independence in 1991 as the USSR dissolved, then diverged sharply — from Kazakhstan's mineral-driven, multi-vector diplomacy to Tajikistan's brutal 1992–97 civil war.

The 5 countries

antiquity–present Kazakhstan The world's largest landlocked country, steppe homeland of the Kazakh Khanate, Russian and Soviet rule, and since 1991 the region's mineral-wealthiest state pursuing a multi-vector foreign policy between Russia, China, and the West. antiquity–present Uzbekistan The region's most populous nation, home to Timur's Samarkand and Bukhara's centuries-old Silk Road heritage, Russian Turkestan, and a Soviet republic whose 1991 independence carried the deepest Silk Road legacy of the five. antiquity–present Turkmenistan Turkmen tribal confederations and the Silk Road oasis of Merv, conquered by Russia last among the region's khanates in the 1880s, and since 1995 a constitutionally neutral, famously isolationist state. antiquity–present Kyrgyzstan A historically nomadic, mountainous Turkic homeland along the Tian Shan, absorbed into the Russian Empire and then Soviet Kirghizia, and since 1991 the region's most politically turbulent republic, with revolutions unseating presidents in 2005, 2010, and 2020. antiquity–present Tajikistan The region's lone Persian-speaking nation, heir to Samanid-era Persianate culture, carved out as a Soviet republic in the 1920s, and scarred after independence by a devastating 1992–97 civil war.

About this hub

The interactive map above traces nine points across roughly two and a half thousand years: Silk Road oasis-states, the Arab-Islamic conquest, the Mongol conquest, Timur's empire centered on Samarkand, the Khiva-Bukhara-Kokand khanate era, the Russian imperial conquest, Soviet national delimitation, the simultaneous 1991 independence of all five republics, and today. Each country below also has its own deep-dive flag-timeline page, linked from the cards above.

The shared thread across all five is striking: ancient Silk Road trade that made cities like Samarkand and Bukhara wealthy crossroads between China, Persia, and Europe; conquest by Genghis Khan's Mongols in the 13th century and, later, by Timur's Turco-Mongol empire centered on Samarkand; absorption into the Russian Empire through the 19th-century Great Game; Soviet rule and the 1920s–30s national delimitation that drew today's borders, often cutting across ethnic and linguistic lines rather than following them; and independence in 1991 with the Soviet Union's collapse. What distinguishes them is what came after — Kazakhstan's resource wealth, Uzbekistan's Silk Road heritage and population weight, Turkmenistan's unusual neutral and isolationist path, Kyrgyzstan's comparatively turbulent post-Soviet politics, and Tajikistan's civil war.
