Flags of Colombia

Centuries before the Muisca, the San Agustín culture of the southern Andes carved South America's largest collection of monumental stone statuary, then abandoned its ceremonial centers for reasons archaeology has not established. The high plateau around modern Bogotá later belonged to the Muisca, a confederation of chiefdoms famed for gold work so rich it fed the El Dorado legend — a real trading and political order, though never remotely the scale of the Inca state to the south. Spanish conquest in the 1530s folds this territory into the Viceroyalty of New Granada, seated at Bogotá, one of the major administrative units of Spain's American empire.

Independence came bundled with two neighbors, not alone: Simón Bolívar's campaigns of the late 1810s created Gran Colombia, a single republic spanning modern Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, and briefly Panama, before regional rivalries pulled it apart within a decade of its founding. Colombia inherited the "New Granada" core and, later, would lose its own Panamanian province to a US-backed 1903 secession tied to canal politics.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Colombia, c. 1st century BCE–present, six political entities. Panama is treated as Colombian territory through 1903 and as its own separate country after; this page does not attempt Panama's own flag lineage past that split. No internal governing partition exists within Colombia's own modern borders after independence, so the map below traces the whole country rather than an internal partition. Republic of Colombia (modern)

Republic, independentTomás Cipriano de MosqueraRoman Catholic (official until 1991)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: fleeing armed conflict (1980s–2000s)Immigration: Venezuelan migrants & refugees (2015–)

Who & why: after decades of shifting federal and unitary experiments under names like the Granadine Confederation and the United States of Colombia, the country settles on "Republic of Colombia" and a fixed flag under President Tomás Cipriano de Mosquera in 1861. The republic survives the 1899–1902 Thousand Days' War, the 1903 loss of Panama (which declares independence with US naval backing after Colombia's senate rejects a canal treaty), and, in recent decades, a long internal armed conflict that a 2016 peace accord with the FARC substantially wound down.

What it represents: the yellow-blue-red tricolor inherited from Gran Colombia, with the yellow band widened to double the width of blue and red beneath it, a distinction unique to Colombia among the old Gran Colombian states.

Republic of New Granada

Republic, independentFrancisco de Paula SantanderRoman Catholic (official)

Who & why: Gran Colombia, weakened by regional rivalries and Bolívar's own declining health, formally dissolves in 1830-31 as Venezuela and Ecuador secede; the remaining core, corresponding roughly to the old Viceroyalty of New Granada plus Panama, reorganizes as the Republic of New Granada. The new state cycles through several constitutional forms — unitary republic, then the Granadine Confederation from 1858 — while retaining the tricolor as a shared inheritance from Bolívar's project.

What it represents: the same yellow-blue-red tricolor as Gran Colombia, at this stage still in equal horizontal bands, before Colombia's own later distinguishing modification to the yellow band's width.

Gran Colombia

Union republicSimón BolívarRoman Catholic (official)

Who & why: Simón Bolívar's decisive 1819 victory at Boyacá secures New Granada's independence, and the Congress of Angostura that same year proclaims Gran Colombia, formally uniting New Granada (Colombia), Venezuela, and Ecuador into a single republic under Bolívar as president, with Panama joining as well. The union proves fragile — regional caudillos resist Bogotá's central authority, and Venezuela and Ecuador both secede by 1830, the year Bolívar dies, ending the project he had hoped would anchor a unified Spanish America.

What it represents: the yellow-blue-red tricolor devised by Francisco de Miranda for earlier Venezuelan independence efforts, adopted as Gran Colombia's own flag and the shared ancestor of Colombia's, Venezuela's, and Ecuador's later national flags alike.

Viceroyalty of New Granada (Spanish colonial rule)

Colonial territoryCharles I of Spain (Charles V)Roman Catholic (Spanish colonial)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: enslaved Africans via Cartagena (forced)
Ruling house:House of HabsburgHouse of Bourbon

Who & why: Spain organizes its conquered northern Andean territory first as the Royal Audiencia of Santa Fe de Bogotá under the Viceroyalty of Peru, then elevates it to its own Viceroyalty of New Granada, formally established in 1717/1739, governing what is now Colombia, Venezuela, Ecuador, and Panama from Bogotá. Spanish rule brings colonial administration, the Catholic Church, and forced labor systems over the Muisca and other indigenous populations, while Cartagena becomes a major fortified port on Spain's transatlantic treasure route. Independence movements gather through the 1800s–10s, culminating in Bolívar's 1819 victory at Boyacá.

What it represents: Spain flew no single fixed colonial flag across all its American territories; the red saltire (cross of Burgundy) on white was the common Spanish military and naval ensign of the era, shown here schematically alongside the viceregal seal's gold sun to represent Spanish crown authority over the colony, not a specific New Granada banner.

Muisca Confederation

Indigenous confederationNo single ruler documented (zipa and zaque chieftains)Muisca polytheistic traditions (no state church)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Spain (1537–38)
Ruling house:Zipa of Bacatá

Who & why: on the high plateau around modern Bogotá and Tunja, the Muisca people organize into a loose confederation of chiefdoms (zipa and zaque) by the 15th century, sustained by agriculture, salt mining, and renowned goldwork; the Muisca cacique's ritual gold-dusted offerings at Lake Guatavita feed the later Spanish El Dorado legend. Spanish forces under Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada conquer the Muisca heartland in 1537–38, founding Santa Fe de Bogotá on the site and beginning colonial rule that formalizes over the following decades.

What it represents: no flag in the European territorial sense is documented for the Muisca; the gold disc shown here represents their renowned metalwork and ceremonial gold objects, an informed symbolic stand-in rather than an attested banner.

San Agustín culture

Archaeological culture, no unified state documentedNo named rulers survive in the recordIndigenous religious & funerary tradition (no state church)
Migration & conquest:Ceremonial sites abandoned, c. 8th century (cause undocumented)

Who & why: in the highlands of what is now Huila department, in Colombia's southern Andes, a distinct culture known to archaeology as San Agustín carves hundreds of monumental stone statues — anthropomorphic and animal deities, shamanic figures, and tomb guardians — over roughly nine centuries, leaving South America's largest collection of megalithic religious sculpture and funerary architecture. The culture is geographically and chronologically distinct from the later Muisca Confederation to the north; its major ceremonial centers are abandoned by around the 8th century CE, well before Spanish contact, for reasons the archaeological record does not establish.

What it represents: no flag or comparable banner survives from San Agustín; the culture expressed itself through monumental stone carving and burial architecture rather than any woven or painted standard. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: San Agustín's main statuary-carving centuries run alongside the height of the Roman Empire, the rise of Teotihuacan in central Mexico, and the creation of the Nazca Lines on Peru's southern coast, and overlap much of the Classic Maya civilization's peak (c. 250–900 CE) further north in Mesoamerica.
