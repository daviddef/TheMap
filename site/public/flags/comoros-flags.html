Flags of Comoros

The islands' earliest known inhabitants, small farming and fishing communities documented only through archaeology at sites like Dembeni on Mayotte, arrived from the East African coast centuries before Islam or any sultanate reached the archipelago. Later settled further by Bantu, Arab, Swahili, and Shirazi Persian voyagers across the Indian Ocean trade routes and organized for centuries into competing island sultanates, then folded into the French colonial empire, the Comoro archipelago won independence in 1975 through a unilateral declaration — one that split the group when the island of Mayotte voted to remain French — and has since become notorious for a very high number of coup attempts against a small and financially strained state.

Five entries trace this lineage, from the earliest archaeologically documented settlement and the sultanates' own banners through French rule to a young republic whose own flag design has shifted more than once as its government did.

Scope of this page: the islands of the Comoro archipelago, c. 6th century CE–present, administered together as one French territory and then one independent state — Ngazidja (Grande Comore), Nzwani (Anjouan), and Mwali (Mohéli) — from precolonial sultanates to present. Mayotte, the archipelago's fourth main island, voted in a 1974 referendum to remain French rather than join independent Comoros and has never been part of the independent state; it is noted here factually but not covered as part of this lineage. The 1975–2001 span, covering a first independence flag, the 1978 Federal Islamic Republic, and the country's exceptionally frequent coups and changes of government, is treated as one honest bridging entry rather than a card for every brief regime, since the crescent-and-stars flag format stayed broadly consistent throughout even as exact star counts and arrangement shifted. Union of the Comoros

Republic, independentAzali AssoumaniSunni Islam (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: large diaspora in France, remittance-dependent

Who & why: after a 1997 secessionist crisis on Nzwani and Mwali nearly broke the young state apart entirely, a new 2001 constitution restructures the country as the Union of the Comoros, granting each island significant autonomy under a rotating union presidency — an attempt to defuse the separatism and coup-proneness that had defined the previous quarter-century. The new flag follows in 2001–02. Political instability continues into the 2000s (including a 2008 African Union-backed intervention to unseat a rebel governor of Anjouan) but the state has held together in this form since, with Azali Assoumani, first taking power in a 1999 coup, dominating union politics for most of the period since.

What it represents: four horizontal stripes — yellow, white, red, and blue — for the islands of Ngazidja, Mwali, Nzwani, and Mayotte (the last still claimed despite its 1974 vote to remain French), a green hoist triangle for Islam, and a white crescent with four stars for the same four islands.

Independence, the Federal Islamic Republic, and repeated coups

Republic, unstableAhmed AbdallahSunni Islam (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: kwassa-kwassa boat migration to Mayotte (1995–)

Who & why: Comoros unilaterally declares independence from France on 6 July 1975 under President Ahmed Abdallah; Mayotte alone, in a separate referendum, votes to remain French, splitting the archipelago — stated here factually and neutrally, as a decision of Mayotte's own voters rather than a contested annexation. Abdallah is quickly overthrown, and the mercenary-installed radical Ali Soilih pursues a brief, chaotic secularizing revolution before mercenaries (led by the French soldier of fortune Bob Denard) restore Abdallah in 1978, who then proclaims the Federal Islamic Republic of the Comoros with a new constitution. What follows is an extraordinary run of instability even by regional standards: by some counts more than twenty coups or attempted coups between 1975 and the early 2000s, including further Denard-led interventions, Abdallah's own 1989 assassination, and a 1997 secessionist revolt on Nzwani and Mwali against rule from the capital, Moroni. Through nearly all of this the flag keeps its basic form — a green field bearing a white crescent and stars — with only the star count and arrangement shifting as the state's own self-definition (and island count) changed.

What it represents: green and the crescent moon for Islam, the country's state religion, with white stars representing the constituent islands the government of the moment claimed — a design broadly continuous even as the republic's institutions were repeatedly overturned.

French colonial rule

Colonial territoryAndriantsolySunni Islam (retained under indirect rule)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: France (1841–90s)Emigration: laborers to Madagascar & Réunion
Ruling house:Abdallah dynasty (Anjouan)Sultanate of Bambao

Who & why: France annexes Mayotte in 1841 at the invitation of a local sultan seeking protection, then extends protectorate and later colonial control over the rest of the archipelago through the 1880s–90s. The islands are administered jointly with Madagascar from 1912 until becoming a separate French overseas territory in 1946. A gradual, uneven push for self-government follows after World War II, culminating in the 1975 declaration of independence, though a 1973 agreement with France had already set the terms for a staged transition that the abrupt 1975 declaration cut short.

What it represents: the plain French Tricolor, flown over the territory as over France's other colonial holdings; no distinct territorial flag existed for the Comoros during French rule.

Island sultanates

Fragmented sultanatesNo single ruler documentedSunni Islam (sultanate establishment)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Bantu, Arab & Shirazi Persian settlers
Ruling house:Abdallah dynasty (Anjouan)Sultanate of Bambao

Who & why: for centuries before French rule, the Comoro Islands are settled and shaped by successive waves of Bantu African, Arab, Swahili, and Shirazi Persian migration and trade, producing a distinctively Islamic, Swahili-inflected culture. Each main island typically hosts its own sultanate or set of rival sultanates — Ngazidja alone was often divided among several competing sultans at once — with no single ruler ever unifying the whole archipelago before colonization.

What it represents: individual sultans may have flown their own banners in the wider Islamic and Swahili-coast vexillological tradition, but no single flag unified the islands, and none is securely reconstructable today. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Earliest settlement (pre-Islamic)

Early farming & fishing settlements, no state yetNo named rulers survive in the recordPre-Islamic (specific beliefs undocumented)
Migration & conquest:Settled by: Bantu-speaking migrants from the East African coast

Who & why: centuries before any sultanate, the Comoro Islands' earliest known inhabitants are documented only through archaeology, not written record. Excavations at Dembeni on Mayotte — the type-site for this earliest identified settlement phase — show small farming and fishing communities, plausibly linked to Bantu-speaking migration from the nearby East African coast, established by roughly the 6th to 8th century CE and importing pottery that attests to early Indian Ocean trade contact. The widespread Arab, Swahili, and Shirazi Persian migration and Islamization that define the later sultanate era are a subsequent, separately documented development, arriving from around the 9th–10th century onward.

What it represents: no flag or comparable symbol survives from this period, and none plausibly existed in any modern sense; the settlement is known through excavated pottery, house foundations, and midden deposits rather than any political or written record. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this earliest settlement phase is roughly contemporary with the rise of the first Swahili coast trading towns along East Africa, the Austronesian settlement of nearby Madagascar from Southeast Asia, and the height of the Abbasid Caliphate's Indian Ocean trade network, which carried goods (and, indirectly, people) between the Middle East, East Africa, and as far as Tang dynasty China.
