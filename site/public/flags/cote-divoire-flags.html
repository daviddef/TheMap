Flags of Côte d'Ivoire

Before European contact, the lands that became Côte d'Ivoire held a patchwork of smaller Akan kingdoms — the Baoulé chiefdoms among them — and Mande-speaking states in the north and west, none dominant enough to unify the whole territory. France established coastal trading posts from the 1840s and formally declared the colony of French Ivory Coast in 1893, folding it into French West Africa. Independence arrived on 7 August 1960 under Félix Houphouët-Boigny, who led the country for the next 33 years and, for much of that period, presided over one of the region's stronger post-independence economies.

The vertical orange-white-green tricolor adopted in 1959, ahead of independence, encodes that same north-south geography: orange for the savanna of the north, green for the forest of the south, and white for the peace binding the two together — a unity the country's own history has tested, in the civil conflicts of 2002–07 and 2010–11, without ever changing the flag itself.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Côte d'Ivoire, from pre-colonial Akan and Mande polities through French colonization to the modern republic. No single flag is attested for the many smaller kingdoms that preceded French rule, so that era is shown as one honest, clearly labeled entry rather than an invented banner. Republic of Côte d'Ivoire (independent)

Migration & conquest:Immigration: Burkinabé & Malian laborers (1960s–)Immigration: Liberian war refugees (1990s–2000s)Emigration: war & crisis refugees (2002–11)

Who & why: independence comes on 7 August 1960 under Félix Houphouët-Boigny, who governs until his death in 1993, a period of relative political stability and export-led growth (cocoa and coffee) often called the "Ivorian miracle." The following decades bring real strain: a first civil war splits the country north-south from 2002 to 2007, and a disputed 2010 election triggers a second, sharper crisis in 2010–11 before a single government is restored; the flag itself never changes through any of this.

What it represents: the orange-white-green vertical tricolor, adopted 3 December 1959 ahead of independence: orange for the northern savanna, green for the southern forest, and white for peace holding the two together.

Autonomous Republic within the French Community

Autonomous republicFélix Houphouët-BoignySecular (no state religion)

Who & why: unlike neighboring Guinea, Côte d'Ivoire votes "yes" in the September 1958 referendum on de Gaulle's French Community, becoming a self-governing republic within it rather than breaking immediately. Félix Houphouët-Boigny, already a senior figure in French politics, leads the territory toward full independence two years later.

What it represents: the orange-white-green tricolor is adopted on 3 December 1959, ahead of full independence, and carries forward unchanged once sovereignty is declared in 1960.

French Ivory Coast (colony)

ColonyLouis-Gustave BingerRoman Catholic (French colonial favor)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Voltaic (Burkinabé) forced plantation labor

Who & why: France formally declares the colony of Ivory Coast in 1893 after decades of coastal trading-post presence, then folds it into French West Africa (AOF) in 1904. Colonial rule brings forced labor and export-crop agriculture (cocoa, coffee, timber) built largely on the backs of local and migrant labor; Houphouët-Boigny himself rises to prominence in this period as a planter and labor-rights advocate before entering French politics.

What it represents: the French Tricolore flew as the sovereign flag across French West Africa; no distinct territorial flag existed for Ivory Coast itself. Pre-colonial Akan and Mande polities

Fragmented kingdomsNo single ruler documentedNo single state religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Baoulé migration from Ashanti lands (18th c.)
Ruling house:House of Watara (Kong Empire)House of Amon Ndoufou (Sanwi Kingdom)

Who & why: before French rule, the territory holds no single unifying kingdom: Akan groups including the Baoulé migrate into the central forest region in the 18th century (fleeing Ashanti expansion further east), the Agni kingdoms sit in the southeast, and Mande-speaking states including the Kong Empire hold sway in the north. Portuguese and later French traders make coastal contact from the 15th–17th centuries, but no single power controls the whole territory before colonization.

What it represents: no securely documented flag unifies this era, since no single polity ever governed the whole territory; each kingdom used its own regalia and standards rather than a shared national symbol. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
