Flags of Cyprus

Cyprus's position at the eastern edge of the Mediterranean, within reach of Europe, Anatolia, and the Levant alike, made it a prize passed between an unusually long chain of outside powers, going back further than almost any other page in this atlas: a millennium of independent Greek city-kingdoms under shifting Assyrian, Egyptian, and Persian overlordship, a Byzantine province for the better part of a further millennium, a crusader kingdom ruled by a French noble dynasty for three centuries, a Venetian trading outpost, an Ottoman province for three hundred years, and a British possession for most of the twentieth century, before independence in 1960 produced a flag deliberately designed to belong to neither of the island's two largest communities alone.

That 1960 flag — a copper-colored silhouette of the island itself, flanked by two olive branches on plain white — has remained the flag of the internationally recognized Republic of Cyprus ever since, even as the island's political reality grew more complicated after 1974, a division this page notes as a documented fact without taking a position on it.

Scope of this page: the island of Cyprus, c. 1200 BCE–present, eight political phases. Cyprus's Mycenaean settlement and its millennium of independent Greek city-kingdoms now have their own dedicated entry; Phoenician trading posts and the later Ptolemaic and direct Roman rule remain bridged into the neighboring Byzantine-era entry rather than further subdivided, since no securely attributable flag survives from any of those phases. Since 1974 the island has been de facto divided between the Republic of Cyprus in the south and a Turkish Cypriot-administered north that proclaimed itself the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus in 1983 (recognized only by Turkey); this page follows the internationally recognized Republic of Cyprus's continuous 1960 flag as its throughline through the timeline below, and the map further down shows the real 1974 division as a genuine two-region split, framed neutrally as a documented fact. Republic of Cyprus

Republic, independentArchbishop Makarios IIINo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Turkey (north, 1974–)Emigration: post-1974 diaspora (UK, Australia, US)

Who & why: Cyprus becomes independent on 16 August 1960 under the Zurich and London Agreements, with a constitution designed to balance its Greek Cypriot majority and Turkish Cypriot minority in shared government. That power-sharing arrangement breaks down over the following years, and in July 1974 a Greek-backed coup aiming at union with Greece is followed within days by a Turkish military intervention. The island has remained de facto divided since: the Republic of Cyprus, internationally recognized and an EU member since 2004, administers the south, while a Turkish Cypriot administration controls the north and declared itself the Turkish Republic of Northern Cyprus in 1983, recognized only by Turkey. This page notes that division as a documented fact without taking a position on its resolution.

What it represents: deliberately neutral by design — a copper-colored silhouette of the island (copper mining gave Cyprus its name in antiquity) on plain white, with two green olive branches beneath standing for peace between the island's communities, avoiding both Greece's blue and Turkey's red.

1925 – 1960

Crown Colony of Cyprus

Colonial ruleGeorge VNo official state religion (British colonial)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to Britain (1930s–1950s)

Who & why: Britain formalizes Cyprus's status as a Crown Colony in 1925, ending the ambiguous arrangement that had followed the island's 1914 annexation. Greek Cypriot demands for enosis (union with Greece) grow through the 1930s and intensify into the armed EOKA insurgency from 1955, which, alongside Turkish Cypriot counter-mobilization, eventually pushes Britain toward negotiating independence rather than continued colonial rule.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign, Union Flag in the canton, with Cyprus's own colonial badge (a gold disc bearing the island's arms) in the fly — the pattern shared with most other British Crown Colonies of the period.

1878 – 1925

British administration and annexation

British administrationQueen VictoriaSunni Islam (nominal, until 1914 annexation)

Who & why: under the 1878 Cyprus Convention, the Ottoman Empire leases administration of the island to Britain in exchange for support against Russia, while nominal Ottoman sovereignty continues. That nominal link ends when the Ottoman Empire enters World War I on the side of the Central Powers in late 1914, at which point Britain formally annexes Cyprus outright; British rule continues, still short of full Crown Colony status, until that status is formalized in 1925.

What it represents: the Ottoman flag continues as the island's nominal sovereign flag through 1914 even as Britain administers day-to-day government; the Union Flag is flown over British administrative buildings throughout, and becomes the island's only flag in practice after the 1914 annexation, before a dedicated Cyprus colonial badge is introduced.

Ottoman Cyprus

Ottoman provinceSelim IISunni Islam (Ottoman)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Ottoman Empire (1570–71)Immigration: Anatolian Turkish settlers (from 1571)
Ruling house:House of Osman (Ottoman dynasty)

Who & why: the Ottoman Empire conquers Cyprus from Venice in 1570–71, taking Nicosia and, after a prolonged siege, Famagusta. Ottoman rule reorganizes land tenure and taxation and brings substantial Turkish settlement to the island, the origin of its modern Turkish Cypriot community, while Cyprus becomes a fairly ordinary, if strategically watched, Ottoman province for the following three centuries.

What it represents: the standard Ottoman state flag, a white crescent and star on red, in use in recognizable form from this period and flown over the island as an ordinary provincial territory of the empire.

1489 – 1571

Venetian Cyprus

City-republic (overseas rule)Doge Agostino BarbarigoRoman Catholic (Venetian)

Who & why: Catherine Cornaro, the Venetian-born widow of the last Lusignan king, is pressured by Venice into ceding the kingdom in 1489, formally ending Lusignan rule and bringing Cyprus under direct control of the Republic of Venice as a maritime colonial possession. Venice fortifies the island heavily against Ottoman expansion, most visibly at Famagusta and Nicosia, but ultimately cannot hold it once the Ottomans invade in force in 1570.

What it represents: the Lion of St Mark, Venice's winged-lion state emblem on a red or gold field, flown over Venetian overseas possessions including Cyprus as the standard marker of Venetian rule throughout the Mediterranean.

1192 – 1489

Kingdom of Cyprus (Lusignan)

Crusader kingdomGuy of LusignanRoman Catholic (Latin Church established)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Crusader forces (1191)Immigration: Frankish crusader settlers (from 1192)
Ruling house:House of Lusignan

Who & why: Richard the Lionheart seizes Cyprus in 1191 during the Third Crusade and sells it on to Guy of Lusignan, the deposed King of Jerusalem, in 1192, founding a French-descended crusader dynasty that rules Cyprus as an independent kingdom for nearly three centuries. Lusignan Cyprus becomes a major regional power and a key rear base for crusading efforts in the eastern Mediterranean, blending French, Byzantine Greek, and local Cypriot populations and institutions under its own royal court at Nicosia.

What it represents: the Lusignan royal arms — recorded as a barry (horizontally striped) field of silver and blue bearing a red lion rampant, crowned gold — served as both the dynasty's heraldic device and its royal banner, in keeping with medieval practice; shown here as an informed reconstruction of that recorded blazon.

c. 330 – 1191

Byzantine Cyprus (with earlier antiquity)

Byzantine provinceConstantine IEastern Orthodox Christianity (by era's end)

Who & why: this single bridging entry stands in for over a thousand years of pre-crusader history, from Phoenician trading settlements and the classical Greek city-kingdoms through Ptolemaic and Roman rule, and then the long Byzantine period proper, beginning with the 330 founding of Constantinople as capital of the eastern Roman Empire. Cyprus remains a Byzantine province, administered from Constantinople with occasional interruptions from Arab raids in the 7th–10th centuries, until Richard the Lionheart's 1191 conquest ends Byzantine rule for good.

What it represents: no securely documented flag can be attributed specifically to Cyprus for any part of this long span; pre-heraldic antiquity used no territorial flags at all, and Byzantine authority was expressed through imperial regalia and religious icons rather than a fixed vexillological standard. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 1200 BCE – 330 CE

Mycenaean Settlement & the Kingdom of Cyprus (City-Kingdoms)

Fragmented / independent city-kingdomsNo single ruler documented (10 named kings, 673 BCE)Greek polytheism (Aphrodite cult at Paphos)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Mycenaean Greek settlement (c. 1200–1050 BCE)Overlordship by: Assyria, Egypt, then Persia (709–333 BCE)

Who & why: Mycenaean Greek settlers arrive in Cyprus around the time of the Bronze Age collapse and the Trojan War tradition, founding city-kingdoms that endure for roughly a millennium under changing outside overlordship. Assyrian records name ten Cypriot kings paying tribute in 673 BCE; Egyptian and then Persian overlordship follow, and the city-kingdoms keep their own local dynasties throughout. Alexander the Great's 333 BCE conquest ends Persian rule; his successor Ptolemy I of Egypt annexes the island outright in 294 BCE, ending the city-kingdoms' long run of local self-government, and Rome in turn annexes Cyprus from the Ptolemies in 58 BCE, ruling it as an ordinary province for the next four centuries until Constantine's 330 refounding of Constantinople, where the neighboring Byzantine-era entry on this page picks up.

What it represents: no securely documented flag can be attributed to any Cypriot city-kingdom or to the island as a whole in this era; pre-heraldic antiquity used no territorial flags at all — kings were represented on coinage and seals rather than banners. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the Mycenaean settlement of Cyprus is contemporary with the traditional Trojan War and the wider Bronze Age collapse across the eastern Mediterranean; the city-kingdoms era later overlaps with the classical Greek world's construction of the Parthenon (447–432 BCE), and its final century runs alongside the Ptolemaic dynasty's own construction of the Lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, built by the same Ptolemaic kings who annex Cyprus itself in 294 BCE.
