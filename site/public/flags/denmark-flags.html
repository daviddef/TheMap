Flags of Denmark

From an Iron Age Denmark known to Roman traders and named in writing for the first time by 6th-century Byzantine and Gothic historians, through Harald Bluetooth's Jelling stones — Denmark's own 10th-century "birth certificate" — a three-crown union that briefly held all of Scandinavia, four centuries bound to Norway, the 1814 treaty that cut that tie, and a 20th-century occupation the country's own flag never actually left. Six entries, because Denmark's real history is unusually stable: one continuous kingdom, rarely conquered, whose flag changes only twice in eight hundred years.

The Dannebrog — red field, offset white Scandinavian cross — is traditionally dated to 15 June 1219, making it one of the oldest national flag designs still in everyday use anywhere in the world. Its legend, its long absence during the Kalmar Union, and its quiet survival through five years of German occupation are the spine of this page.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Denmark (metropolitan Denmark; Iceland, the Faroe Islands, and Greenland are treated as attached territories in prose, not shown as a separate internal split), c. 1 CE–present, six political entities. Kingdom of Denmark

Sovereign kingdomFrederick VILutheranism (established state church)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Nazi Germany (1940–45)Emigration: Danish Jews rescued to Sweden (1943)Immigration: Turkish & Yugoslav guest workers (1960s–)
Ruling house:House of OldenburgHouse of Glücksburg

Who & why: the January 1814 Treaty of Kiel forces Denmark, on the losing side of the Napoleonic Wars, to cede Norway to the King of Sweden — but Denmark negotiates hard and keeps its older North Atlantic dependencies, Iceland, the Faroe Islands, and Greenland, carved out of the transfer. The June Constitution of 1849 ends centuries of absolute monarchy and establishes a constitutional, parliamentary system still in force today. German forces invade on 9 April 1940; Denmark capitulates within hours and lives under an unusually "model" occupation — King Christian X remains on the throne and the elected government keeps functioning — until Berlin imposes direct martial law in August 1943 after resistance sabotage escalates. Liberation comes in May 1945. Iceland uses Denmark's own wartime paralysis to hold a 1944 independence referendum, ending their personal union for good; Denmark becomes a founding NATO member in 1949 and joins the EU (then EEC) in 1973, while Greenland and the Faroe Islands are granted home rule in 1979 and 1948 respectively and remain part of the Danish Realm today.

What it represents: the same Dannebrog flown since 1219, minus only the Kalmar Union's interruption — a design old enough, and used continuously enough, that it is widely cited as the oldest state flag still in everyday use anywhere in the world. Remarkably, occupying Germany never replaced it: the Dannebrog kept flying over Denmark for almost the entire war.

Denmark–Norway (Oldenburg personal union)

Union kingdomFrederick ILutheranism (state church, from 1536)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: colonists to the West Indies, Africa & India (17th–18th c.)
Ruling house:House of Oldenburg

Who & why: when Sweden breaks free of the Kalmar Union in 1523, Denmark and Norway stay bound together under the House of Oldenburg — a narrower, two-way union that will outlast the original three-way arrangement by nearly three centuries. Christian III's Reformation of 1536 makes Lutheranism the state religion and formally reduces Norway's own institutions to a Danish dependency in all but name, with real power sitting firmly in Copenhagen. Denmark-Norway becomes a minor colonial and mercantile power — trading posts in India and West Africa, the Danish West Indies in the Caribbean — through the 17th and 18th centuries, before the Napoleonic Wars prove disastrous: the Royal Navy seizes the Danish fleet at Copenhagen in 1807, pushing Denmark into an alliance with Napoleon that ends in defeat and, in 1814, the loss of Norway itself.

What it represents: the Dannebrog continues as the shared banner of both kingdoms — no distinct Norwegian flag exists yet in this period; Norway will not fly its own separate design until the 19th century, after leaving this union for Sweden's.

1397 – 1523

Kalmar Union (Denmark, Norway & Sweden)

Union kingdomMargaret IRoman Catholicism (state religion)
Ruling house:House of EstridsenHouse of Oldenburg

Who & why: Margaret I of Denmark — regent of Denmark, Norway, and (by conquest) Sweden, and one of medieval Europe's most formidable rulers — spends the 1380s and 1390s assembling personal control over all three crowns. At Kalmar Castle in June 1397 her great-nephew Eric of Pomerania is crowned king of a single union binding all of Scandinavia, with Denmark the dominant partner throughout. The union never merges institutions; each kingdom keeps its own laws and nobility. Swedish resentment of Danish-favoring rule builds for over a century, boiling over after the Stockholm Bloodbath of November 1520, when Danish king Christian II has dozens of Swedish nobles and clergy executed. Gustav Vasa's rebellion captures Stockholm by June 1523, ending Swedish participation in the union for good.

What it represents: a distinct union banner — gold field, red cross, three crowns representing the three united kingdoms — used alongside continued domestic use of the Dannebrog within Denmark itself; the union symbol, not the Dannebrog, is what this era is remembered for.

1219 – 1397

Kingdom of Denmark (medieval, Dannebrog era)

KingdomValdemar II "the Victorious"Roman Catholicism (state religion)
Ruling house:House of Estridsen

Who & why: King Valdemar II ("the Victorious") leads a Danish crusading army into Estonia, expanding Danish power around the Baltic. Tradition holds that at the Battle of Lindanise (near modern Tallinn) on 15 June 1219, as the battle turns against the Danes, a red cloth bearing a white cross falls from the sky — the Dannebrog — rallying the troops to victory. The legend is first recorded in a 16th-century chronicle, centuries after the supposed event, so historians treat it as a national origin myth rather than verified fact; still, the flag design itself is attested from around this period, and Denmark's claim to the world's oldest continuously used national flag rests on it. Valdemar's Baltic conquests mostly unravel after his capture and ransom in 1223, but the flag outlasts the empire that supposedly produced it.

What it represents: red field, offset white Scandinavian cross — the Dannebrog design, essentially unchanged in the eight centuries since, with only the Kalmar Union's own banner briefly displacing it as the flag of state.

Kingdom of Denmark (medieval, pre-Dannebrog)

KingdomHarald "Bluetooth" GormssonRoman Catholicism (Christianization from c. 965)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Danish Vikings settling & conquering England (9th c.–1016)
Ruling house:House of Estridsen

Who & why: Gorm the Old is traditionally regarded as the first historically attested king of a unified Denmark, ruling from Jelling until his death around 958–964. His son, Harald "Bluetooth" Gormsson, raises the larger of the two Jelling stones around 965 with a runic inscription proclaiming he "won for himself all of Denmark and Norway, and made the Danes Christian" — effectively Denmark's own birth certificate as a unified Christian kingdom, and today a UNESCO World Heritage site. Harald's descendants Sweyn Forkbeard and Cnut the Great extend Danish power into England in the early 11th century, briefly ruling a North Sea empire spanning Denmark, England, and Norway, though it does not survive Cnut's death in 1035.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this earliest two and a half centuries of the unified kingdom; heraldic flags of this kind are still generations away across most of Europe. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Iron Age Denmark & the Early Danes

Fragmented / petty kingshipsChlochilaicus/Hygelac (documented c. 515)Norse paganism
Migration & conquest:Attested by: Jordanes & Procopius (6th c. CE)

Who & why: Roman-era trade goods excavated across Jutland and the Danish isles document extensive contact between Iron Age Denmark and the Roman world without Rome ever conquering the region itself. The Nydam boat, a well-preserved rowed war vessel deposited as a bog offering around 320–350 CE, is among the best physical evidence for this period's seafaring societies. The 6th-century writers Jordanes and Procopius are the first to name the "Dani" as a people, and Gregory of Tours records a Danish king, Chlochilaicus (identified with the Hygelac of Beowulf), leading a real, dated sea raid on Frankish Gaul around 515 CE that ended in his death — one of the earliest individually named Danish rulers in the historical record, more than four centuries before Gorm the Old, whom the neighboring entry on this page credits as the first king of a unified Denmark.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this era, many centuries before heraldic flags develop anywhere in Europe. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the height of Imperial Rome and the building of the Colosseum and Pantheon, the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476, the construction of the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (completed 537), and Charlemagne's own coronation as emperor in Rome in 800 — a reminder that "Viking Age" Scandinavia and the Carolingian Renaissance were direct contemporaries before either the Dannebrog or a unified Danish crown existed.
