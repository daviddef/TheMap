Flags of Djibouti

Djibouti sits at the mouth of the Red Sea, a strip of coast historically shared by Afar sultanates and Issa Somali clans that France began securing from the 1860s and never fully let go: it was the last French territory on mainland Africa to become independent, in 1977, well over a decade after most of its neighbors.

Its flag, adopted at that late independence, carries a light blue and light green split — representing the country's two main population groups — with a red star standing for their unity.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Djibouti, c. 1800–present, three political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Republic of Djibouti

Republic, independentHassan Gouled AptidonIslam (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Somali, Ethiopian & Eritrean refugees (1991–)

Who & why: after a 1967 referendum controversially favors continued association with France, a second referendum in 1977 finally delivers independence, and the Republic of Djibouti is proclaimed on 27 June 1977 under President Hassan Gouled Aptidon — the last French territory on mainland Africa to decolonize, some fifteen years after most of its regional neighbors. Djibouti's small size and strategic position at the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, controlling access between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden, has made it host to multiple foreign military bases since, including France's, the United States', and China's.

What it represents: light blue for the sky and sea, and for the Issa Somali population, light green for the earth and the Afar population, a white hoist triangle for peace, and a red five-pointed star for the unity of the two peoples in one state — adapted directly from the flag of Aptidon's Popular Rally for Progress (LPAI) independence movement.

French Somaliland / French Territory of the Afars and the Issas

Colonial ruleFélix FaureNo official state religion (French colonial)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Yemeni Arab traders (early 20th c.–)Emigration: pro-independence Somalis expelled (1966–67)

Who & why: France secures the port of Obock from local Afar rulers from the 1860s-80s, founds Djibouti city in 1888 as the territory's new capital, and formalizes colonial control as French Somaliland from 1896. The territory is renamed the French Territory of the Afars and the Issas in 1967, explicitly naming its two main population groups, amid a period of contested independence referenda — the 1967 vote controversially favors staying French, widely seen as marred by irregularities and the expulsion of pro-independence Somalis beforehand. A second referendum in 1977 finally delivers independence.

What it represents: the French tricolor, flown as the colonial power's own national flag throughout, with no distinct territorial flag adopted before independence.

Afar sultanates & Issa Somali clans

Fragmented / pre-stateNo single ruler documented (Afar sultans, Issa clan leaders)Sunni Islam (shared faith, no single state)
Ruling house:Sultanate of Tadjoura

Who & why: before French colonization, this stretch of Red Sea coast is home to Afar sultanates — including the Sultanate of Tadjoura, a real small local polity trading across the strait with Arabia — and Issa Somali clans further south and inland, neither organized into a single larger state before European interest arrives in the 1860s.

What it represents: no securely documented unified flag survives for this era across the whole territory. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
