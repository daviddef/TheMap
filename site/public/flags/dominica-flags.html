Flags of Dominica

Dominica takes its name from Columbus's 3 November 1493 sighting of the island on a Sunday — Dominica, Latin for "Sunday." But naming an island and controlling it were two very different things: Dominica's mountainous interior and the sustained resistance of its Kalinago (Island Carib) inhabitants kept both France and Britain from establishing a lasting foothold for nearly two and a half centuries afterward.

Today Dominica is home to the Kalinago Territory, one of the last places in the Caribbean where the indigenous Kalinago retain a real, formally recognized land base — a thread that runs from Dominica's Kalinago-controlled era to the present day. This page's timeline now reaches further back still, to the island's earliest documented settlers roughly five thousand years before Columbus.

Scope of this page: the island of Dominica, c. 3100 BCE–present, five political entries. The long pre-1763 stretch is shown honestly as a "neutral island" era rather than inventing an early conquest date that never happened — Dominica's Kalinago-controlled centuries are a real, documented feature of its history, not a gap to gloss over. Commonwealth of Dominica (independent)

Independent statePatrick JohnNo established church
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the UK, US & Caribbean (1970s–)

Who & why: Dominica gains full independence from Britain on 3 November 1978 — deliberately chosen as the 485th anniversary of Columbus's 1493 sighting — becoming a republic within the Commonwealth. It is the only country in the world whose national flag uses green as its primary, background color.

What it represents: a green field carries a centered cross of three parallel bands — yellow, black, and white — representing the Kalinago people, the island's fertile soil, and its purity and clear water. At the center, a red disc bears a stylized Sisserou parrot (Amazona imperialis), an endangered species found only on Dominica, encircled by ten green five-pointed stars for the island's ten parishes.

1783/1805 – 1978

British Dominica

British colonyNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic tradition / Anglican establishment
Migration & conquest:Immigration: enslaved Africans (colonial era–1834)

Who & why: the 1783 Treaty of Paris, closing the American Revolutionary War, confirms Dominica as British after a period of French capture (see the previous entry). A brief French raid and occupation follows in 1805, during the Napoleonic Wars, when troops under Napoleon's General Lagrange land and briefly hold the island before Britain restores full control the same year, holding it continuously afterward. The colonial economy runs on sugar, then coffee and lime, worked by enslaved labor until emancipation in 1834 (with a mandatory apprenticeship period lasting until 1838); Dominica remains one of the poorer, more rugged, and less-developed British Caribbean colonies through the 19th and early 20th centuries. It joins the short-lived West Indies Federation from 1958 to 1962.

What it represents: the British colonial Blue Ensign — the Union Jack in the canton on a dark blue field — with a colonial badge on the fly bearing a Sisserou parrot, the emblem later carried forward into independence.

1763 – 1783/1805

Early British Dominica and the French recapture

British colony (contested)No single ruler documentedRoman Catholic tradition

Who & why: the 1763 Treaty of Paris, ending the Seven Years' War, formally cedes Dominica to Britain, closing out its long "neutral island" status (see the next entry). British control does not go unchallenged: during the American Revolutionary War, French forces capture Dominica in 1778 and hold it for five years, until the 1783 Treaty of Paris restores the island to Britain for good. (A further, brief French raid in 1805 is noted as an aside in the following entry rather than broken out as its own card here.)

What it represents: the flag of the dominant power at this entry's opening in 1763 — Britain's Union Jack — shown here even though the French interlude of 1778–83 is a real and honestly noted interruption within this same span.

c. 1493 – 1763

Kalinago Dominica and the "neutral island" era

Kalinago-controlled / neutral territoryNo single ruler documentedKalinago spiritual tradition

Who & why: Columbus sights Dominica on 3 November 1493, but sighting is not conquest: the island's rugged, mountainous terrain and the sustained, effective resistance of its Kalinago (Island Carib) inhabitants keep both France and Britain from establishing a lasting colonial foothold for nearly two and a half centuries — unlike almost anywhere else in the Caribbean. In 1660, France and Britain formally agree to leave Dominica (and neighboring St Vincent) as neutral territory, left to the Kalinago; the agreement holds imperfectly until the Seven Years' War changes the calculus in the 1750s–60s and Dominica is formally ceded to Britain in 1763. Dominica remains home to the Kalinago Territory today, one of the few places in the Caribbean where an indigenous community retains a formally recognized land base — a genuine, documented feature of this history worth representing honestly.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this era. The Kalinago did not use European-style heraldic banners, so this entry is shown honestly as a "no flag documented" placeholder rather than an invented design.

Pre-Kalinago Dominica: Ortoiroid & Arawak (Igneri) Settlement

Pre-ceramic & early migrant societiesNo single ruler documentedIndigenous spiritual traditions (zemí tradition)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Ortoiroid Archaic settlement (from c. 3100 BCE)Immigration: Saladoid/Igneri Arawak migration (from c. 400 CE)Conquest: Kalinago arrival & displacement of Igneri (c. 1400s)

Who & why: Dominica's documented human history stretches back roughly forty-six centuries before Columbus. Archaic, pre-ceramic Ortoiroid peoples reach the Lesser Antilles from the South American mainland by canoe by around 3100 BCE, among the earliest settlers of the eastern Caribbean. Arawakan-speaking Saladoid and then Igneri communities follow from around 400 CE, bringing pottery, agriculture, and permanent villages. Kalinago (Island Carib) peoples migrate up the island chain in turn, arriving in Dominica by roughly the 1400s and displacing or absorbing the resident Igneri population — the same Kalinago society that the neighboring entry on this page finds already in firm control of the island when Columbus sights it in 1493.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or banner survives from any part of this nearly five-thousand-year span; none of Dominica's pre-Columbian societies used heraldic flags in the European sense. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the earliest Ortoiroid arrivals in the Lesser Antilles are roughly contemporary with the raising of Stonehenge and the building of Egypt's Old Kingdom pyramids at Giza; the Saladoid/Igneri migration overlaps with the height of Imperial Rome, and the Kalinago's own arrival in Dominica comes just as the Aztec and Inca empires are consolidating power in Mesoamerica and the Andes, on the eve of Columbus's first Atlantic crossing.
