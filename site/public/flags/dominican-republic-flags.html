Flags of the Dominican Republic

Nine centuries before Columbus, Hispaniola's own Taíno cacicazgos — hierarchical chiefdoms descended from Arawak-speaking migrants out of South America — held the island in a patchwork of independent rule. Columbus's ships reached it on his first voyage, and the settlement founded at Santo Domingo in 1496–98 is the first permanent European settlement in the Americas and the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded city in the hemisphere — three centuries as the seat of Spanish power in the New World before the eastern two-thirds of the island took the long, contested road to its own republic.

That road runs through a French cession nobody could quite enforce, a Spanish restoration remembered for its own neglect, 22 years of unification under Haitian rule, Duarte's 1844 declaration and the white-cross flag his movement raised, and — uniquely in Latin America — a voluntary return to colonial status that Dominicans then fought their way back out of. Five flags, seven eras, one white cross that has flown, essentially unaltered, since 1844.

Scope of this page: the eastern two-thirds of the island of Hispaniola, c. 600–present, seven political entries. The pre-1492 Taíno cacicazgos entry is shown honestly blank of any flag — no woven banner from that era survives or was recorded. The 1795–1822 span is the messiest stretch in this lineage — nominal French cession, disrupted by the Haitian Revolution next door, with Spain reasserting real control years before that cession was ever undone on paper — and is treated here as one honest bridging entry rather than several falsely precise ones. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Who & why: the War of Restoration (1863–1865), a guerrilla-heavy popular uprising against Spain's re-annexation, ends with Spanish withdrawal in 1865 and the Dominican Republic restored for good. Rafael Trujillo's dictatorship (1930–1961) and every political upheaval since — the 1965 civil war and US intervention, the return to elected government — are changes of government, not of flag.

What it represents: the white-cross banner first raised in 1844 (see entry 4 below) becomes the permanent national flag from this restoration onward, with only minor refinements to the coat of arms and official proportions over the following century and a half — not separate flags, just one long, settled lineage.

1861 – 1865

Spanish annexation (War of Restoration)

Spanish re-annexationPedro SantanaRoman Catholic (established)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Spain (1861–65)

Who & why: President Pedro Santana, fearing renewed Haitian aggression and seeking a foreign protector, orchestrates the Dominican Republic's re-annexation to Spain in March 1861 — the only case of a Latin American republic voluntarily returning to colonial status after independence. Widespread resentment over taxation, race, and lost sovereignty sparks the War of Restoration; Spain, unable to hold the colony against sustained guerrilla resistance and disease losses among its troops, withdraws by 1865.

What it represents: Spain's own home flag of the period — the red-yellow-red Bourbon/national banner adopted in 1785 — flown here again over Santo Domingo, not a distinct colonial design.

1844 – 1861

First Dominican Republic

RepublicJuan Pablo DuarteRoman Catholic (established)

Who & why: on 27 February 1844 the Trinitarios — Juan Pablo Duarte, Ramón Matías Mella, and Francisco del Rosario Sánchez — seize the Ozama fortress in Santo Domingo and declare independence from 22 years of Haitian rule, founding the Dominican Republic. The young republic spends this entire span fending off repeated Haitian invasions and cycling through unstable presidencies, culminating in Santana's 1861 decision to seek Spanish annexation rather than keep fighting alone.

What it represents: based on the Haitian bicolor but adding a white cross to distinguish it and to symbolize the movement's Catholic and nationalist character — blue (upper hoist, lower fly) and red (upper fly, lower hoist) quadrants divided by a white cross, with the coat of arms at the crossing.

1822 – 1844

Haitian unification of Hispaniola

Under Haitian ruleJean-Pierre BoyerRoman Catholic (disestablished)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: HaitiEmigration: elite families to Cuba, Puerto Rico & Venezuela

Who & why: Haitian president Jean-Pierre Boyer, wary of Spain or another European power reasserting control next door, marches in and occupies the whole island in February 1822, ending a chaotic run that had already seen Spain's own "España Boba" restoration (1809–1821) give way to a very short-lived independent state declared by José Núñez de Cáceres in late 1821 — a state that lasted only weeks before Boyer arrived. Haiti rules the entire island as one unit for 22 years, until the Dominicans' 1844 declaration of independence ends the unification.

What it represents: Haiti's own blue-and-red flag, flown here over the whole territory it now governs — not a distinct Dominican banner, since none existed independently of Haitian rule during this span.

1795 – 1822

French cession and Spanish restoration

Contested / transitionalNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic (nominal)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Haiti (Toussaint Louverture, 1801–02)
Ruling house:House of Bourbon (Spain)

Who & why: the 1795 Treaty of Basel cedes the entire Spanish colony of Santo Domingo to France in exchange for Spanish territory in Europe, but effective French control is disrupted for years by the Haitian Revolution raging next door: Toussaint Louverture's forces briefly occupy Santo Domingo in 1801, Napoleon's expeditionary force reasserts nominal French control after 1802, then loses Haiti itself in 1803–04. Spain formally reoccupies Santo Domingo in 1809 — the "España Boba" ("foolish Spain") period, so called for its neglect of the colony — and holds it, in name if not always in strength, until 1821.

What it represents: no single settled government actually ran this territory for the whole span; the French tricolor stands in here for the era's dominant claimant, with the prose carrying the real back-and-forth rather than a false precision of flags.

1492 – 1795

Spanish Santo Domingo

Spanish colonyChristopher ColumbusRoman Catholic (established)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: SpainImmigration: enslaved Africans (1502–)
Ruling house:House of Habsburg (Spain)House of Bourbon (Spain)

Who & why: Columbus's 1492 landfall and the founding of Santo Domingo in 1496–98 make it the first permanent European settlement in the Americas and the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded city in the hemisphere. For decades it serves as the seat of Spanish colonial administration in the New World, before Spain's interest drifts toward the richer mainland viceroyalties of Mexico and Peru, leaving the colony increasingly peripheral by the time this era ends.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy — a red diagonal saltire on a white field — Spain's standard colonial and maritime banner, flown across its American possessions including Santo Domingo throughout this span.

c. 600 – 1492

Taíno Chiefdoms (Cacicazgos)

Fragmented / chiefdoms (cacicazgos)Caonabo (cacique of Maguana)Zemí worship (Taíno cosmology)
Migration & conquest:Migration: Arawak-speaking peoples from South America (via the Lesser Antilles)Raided by: Island Carib (pre-1492)

Who & why: for roughly nine centuries before Columbus, Hispaniola is home not to one nation but to several Taíno cacicazgos — hierarchical chiefdoms descended from Arawak-speaking migrants who settled the Greater Antilles in the centuries after leaving South America. Each cacicazgo answered to its own cacique and paid no tribute to any wider Taíno state; the island's political map at contact was already a patchwork the Spanish conquest would erase within a generation.

What it represents: no flag or comparable woven banner from this era survives or was ever recorded — shown here honestly blank. Spanish chroniclers describe zemí idols, feathered and shell regalia, and painted bodies as the visible marks of a cacique's authority, not a national symbol in any modern sense.

World context: this span runs alongside the Maya civilization's Classic-period height and 9th-century collapse in Mesoamerica, the Viking voyages that reached Newfoundland around 1000 CE, the Angkor Wat temple complex begun in the early 12th century in Cambodia, and the rise of the Aztec Empire in the Valley of Mexico in the century just before Columbus's 1492 landfall.
