The European Economic Area

Like the EU and Schengen hubs, this one isn't a sovereignty story — it's another voluntary institutional membership layered on top of fully self-governing countries. The European Economic Area extends the EU's own single market — the "four freedoms" of free movement for goods, services, capital, and people — out beyond the EU's own borders, to a handful of European countries that wanted that market access without full EU political membership. The mechanism is the 1992 EEA Agreement, signed in Oporto, Portugal, and in force since 1 January 1994.

The EEA is, in practice, simple arithmetic: it's the EU's 27 member states, plus three countries that are not EU members at all — Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway. Those three joined through the European Free Trade Association (EFTA), a rival trade bloc founded in 1960 by countries that wanted a common market without the EU's deeper political integration. EFTA itself was once much larger — its founding members in 1960 were the United Kingdom, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Austria, Portugal, and Switzerland — but it steadily emptied out as most of those countries left for full EU membership over the following decades. Today EFTA has only four members left: Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway, all three of whom also joined the EEA, plus Switzerland, the one EFTA member that stayed out of it.

That last point is the hinge of the whole story. Swiss voters rejected EEA membership in a December 1992 referendum, so Switzerland never joined. Instead of the EEA's single treaty framework, Switzerland built its own dense web of separate bilateral agreements with the EU over the following years, achieving much of the same market access country by country and topic by topic rather than through one overarching agreement. Switzerland is consequently absent from the country list below — it belongs to EFTA, but not the EEA.

How to read this hub — and how the EEA differs from the EU, Schengen, and EFTA. This is genuinely confusing territory: four overlapping European groupings, each with its own membership list, and none of the four lists match. The table below is the fastest way to keep them straight.
GroupingWhat it's forWho's in it that the others aren't
European Union (27)Full political and economic unionThe baseline 27 — every EEA and Schengen member that is an EU member starts here
EEA (30)Single market only (the "four freedoms")All 27 EU members, plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway
Schengen Area (29)No internal passport/border checksMost EU members plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, and Switzerland — but not Ireland or Cyprus, both EU members that stayed outside it
EFTA (4)Free trade among its own membersIceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, and Switzerland — the last surviving members of a bloc that once included the UK, Denmark, Sweden, Austria, and Portugal

So: the EU is a subset of the EEA (every EU member is automatically an EEA member too, since EEA membership for EU states is bundled into EU accession). But the EEA and Schengen are not the same list, even though they sound like they should be — Schengen includes Switzerland (not in the EEA) and excludes Ireland (which is in the EEA, as an EU member). And EFTA today is really just "Switzerland plus the three non-EU EEA members," a shell of the seven-country bloc it started as in 1960.

The 30 members

The EU's 27 member states, in their order of EU/EEA accession, plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Norway at the end — EEA members since the Agreement's 1994 entry into force (Liechtenstein followed in 1995) but never EU members at all. About this hub

Like the EU and Schengen hubs, this page deliberately skips the atlas's usual real-map, era-slider visualization built to dissolve borders wherever the same government ruled both sides of them. The EEA, like those other two institutional-membership hubs, isn't a sovereignty story at all — every member state here kept its own government, laws, and borders throughout; all that changed was market access. A genuinely accurate visualization for voluntary treaty memberships like this is deferred atlas-wide to a later phase, not improvised here. This page ships instead as a lighter placeholder: real accession history above, one card per member, each linking to that country's own flag-history page where one already exists elsewhere in the atlas.

Dates above reflect EEA and EU accession as commonly reported. The EEA Agreement was signed at Oporto, Portugal on 2 May 1992 and entered into force on 1 January 1994; Liechtenstein's own accession was delayed to 1 May 1995 pending resolution of its customs union with Switzerland.
