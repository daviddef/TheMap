Flags of El Salvador

Maya-speaking farmers built lasting settlements at Chalchuapa and Tazumal more than two thousand years before Pipil migrants arrived, weathering the catastrophic Ilopango eruption that emptied much of the region for generations. From Pipil-speaking Cuzcatlán, whose warriors gave Pedro de Alvarado's conquistadors one of the hardest fights in the isthmus, through three centuries as the smallest and most densely settled province of the Captaincy General of Guatemala, a joint 1821 independence, a brief annexation to the First Mexican Empire, two decades bound into the Federal Republic of Central America, and finally a fully sovereign republic whose blue-white-blue banner traces directly back to that federation's own flag.

El Salvador shares this federation-descended blue-white-blue lineage with Nicaragua and Costa Rica — all three broke from the same 1823–1841 union and kept variations of the same colors, while El Salvador in particular readopted and refined its own version of the design across the late 19th and early 20th centuries before settling on the flag flown today.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day El Salvador, c. 1200 BCE–present, seven political entities. The two oldest entries — Maya Chalchuapa/Tazumal and Pipil Cuzcatlán — predate any documented polity-level flag and are shown honestly blank.

1912 – present

Republic of El Salvador (modern)

Republic, independentManuel Enrique AraujoSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the United States (1979–)

Who & why: after decades of shifting flag designs following the federation's collapse, El Salvador formally readopts the blue-white-blue tricolor with a version of the old federal arms on 17 May 1912, cementing the design still flown today. The 20th century brings a series of military-dominated governments and deep land inequality, culminating in the 1979–1992 civil war between the U.S.-backed military government and FMLN guerrillas; the 1992 Chapultepec Peace Accords end the fighting without any change to the flag itself.

What it represents: the blue-white-blue bands trace directly to the Federal Republic of Central America's own flag, and the central white stripe carries a coat of arms built from the same volcano-and-rainbow imagery the federation used — a deliberate, explicit claim of descent from the shared 1823–1841 union.

1841 – 1912

Republic of El Salvador (early)

Republic, earlyNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic (traditional, gradually curtailed)

Who & why: when the Federal Republic of Central America effectively collapses in 1841, El Salvador continues as a fully sovereign state, cycling through numerous constitutions, short-lived unification attempts with its neighbors, and competing Liberal and Conservative factions across the rest of the 19th century. Flag designs vary in their fine details across this period — some issues carry stars, others a plain field — but the blue-white-blue base color scheme never changes.

What it represents: a direct continuation of the federation's own tricolor, kept as a visible statement that El Salvador, though independent, was still the federation's rightful heir.

1823 – 1841

Federal Republic of Central America (member state)

Federation memberManuel José ArceRoman Catholic (constitutionally established)

Who & why: after breaking from the First Mexican Empire, the five former provinces of the old Captaincy General — Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Costa Rica — form the United Provinces of Central America on 1 July 1823, reorganized as the Federal Republic in 1824. El Salvador, the smallest and most densely populated state, is a stronghold of the federation's Liberal cause and hosts its government-in-exile in the 1830s as the union frays under regional revolts; the federation is effectively dead by 1840–41.

What it represents: the blue-white-blue triband adopted by the federation in 1823, with a central coat of arms — five volcanoes standing for the five member states beneath a rainbow and a Phrygian liberty cap on a staff, symbolizing freedom won from Spain. Every state that emerged from the federation's collapse, including El Salvador, keeps a version of these colors.

1821 – 1823

Annexation to the First Mexican Empire

Empire (annexed)Agustín de IturbideRoman Catholic (exclusive, per the Plan de Iguala)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: First Mexican Empire (1823)

Who & why: the Captaincy General of Guatemala, including El Salvador, declares independence from Spain jointly with its neighbors on 15 September 1821. Within months, Agustín de Iturbide's new Mexican Empire absorbs the whole isthmus; San Salvador alone actively resists annexation, and Mexican troops under Vicente Filisola briefly occupy the city in 1823 before the empire itself collapses.

What it represents: the green-white-red tricolor of the First Mexican Empire, flown briefly and unevenly over a reluctant El Salvador before the empire's fall opens the way to a Central American federation instead.

Spanish colonial rule (Captaincy General of Guatemala)

Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Spain (1524–25)Immigration: enslaved Africans to indigo estates

Who & why: Pedro de Alvarado's forces first invade in 1524 but are repelled at Acajútla; only after years of hard campaigning does Spain secure the Pipil heartland around Cuzcatlán, with San Salvador founded in 1525 and Pipil resistance continuing intermittently for years after. El Salvador becomes a province of the Captaincy General of Guatemala, administered from Guatemala City under the Viceroyalty of New Spain — the smallest province but, thanks to indigo cultivation, an economically important one.

What it represents: the red raggedy saltire (Cross of Burgundy) on a plain field, the standard military and civil ensign flown across Spain's American colonies, including throughout Central America.

c. 1200 – 1525

Pipil Cuzcatlán and neighboring polities

Indigenous kingdomAtlacatl (traditional)Pipil (Nahua) polytheism
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Pipil (Nahua) migration from central Mexico

Who & why: Nahua-speaking Pipil migrants establish the kingdom of Cuzcatlán in the fertile central valley of present-day El Salvador by around the 12th–13th century, alongside Lenca and other indigenous polities elsewhere in the territory. Cuzcatlán mounts unusually stiff resistance to the Spanish invasion beginning in 1524, defeating Pedro de Alvarado's first expedition outright before finally being subdued in the years that follow.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or banner survives from pre-conquest Cuzcatlán; Mesoamerican polities used feathered standards and painted devices rather than fixed heraldic flags, and none can be attributed to Cuzcatlán with confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 1200 BCE – c. 900 CE

Maya Chalchuapa, Tazumal & the Ilopango Eruption

Fragmented / Maya city-statesNo single ruler documentedMaya polytheism
Migration & conquest:Settlement: Maya-speaking peoples (from c. 1200 BCE)Displaced by: Ilopango volcanic eruption (c. 5th–6th c. CE)

Who & why: centuries before Pipil migrants founded Cuzcatlán, Maya-speaking peoples had already built lasting settlements in what is now El Salvador — Chalchuapa's ceremonial center of Tazumal was in continuous use from the Preclassic period into the Postclassic, and the farming village of Joya de Cerén, buried intact under volcanic ash around 600 CE, preserves an ordinary Maya household in more detail than almost any other site in the region. The massive Ilopango eruption devastated central El Salvador and emptied much of the area for generations before it was resettled, eventually by the Pipil.

What it represents: no flag, standard, or comparable polity-level emblem survives or is credibly attributed to Chalchuapa, Tazumal, or any other Maya settlement in this territory; what survives is monumental architecture, ceramics, and, at Joya de Cerén, everyday household objects preserved by ash. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Chalchuapa's earliest settlement is roughly contemporary with the Olmec civilization's height in Mexico, while Tazumal's later Classic-period flourishing overlaps with Teotihuacan's dominance of central Mexico and the great Classic Maya cities of Tikal and Copán; the Ilopango eruption falls within the same turbulent decades as the global "dust veil" of 536 CE and the Plague of Justinian in the Byzantine Empire on the other side of the world.
