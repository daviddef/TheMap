Flags of Estonia

The Roman historian Tacitus already named this coast's amber-gathering inhabitants the Aestii around 98 CE, and the Salme ship burials on Saaremaa (c. 750 CE) preserve one of the best-documented Baltic Sea battles of the early Viking Age — centuries before any named Estonian polity. Estonia's own later story is the Baltic hub's simplest lineage to trace territorially — no internal duchy split like Latvia's Livonia/Courland, just one small, remarkably consistent northern coastline passed whole from conqueror to conqueror: Danish and German crusaders, then Sweden, then Russia, twice interrupted in the 20th century, and finally itself.

The blue-black-white tricolor was first raised by Estonian students at Otepää in 1884, decades before there was a state to fly it over — making it, like Lithuania's and Latvia's own colors, a flag that predates the country and was simply waiting for 1918.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Estonia, c. 100–present, eight political entities. The two oldest entries predate any surviving flag and are shown honestly blank. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Estonia doesn't split internally the way Latvia does. Latvia's own page carves out a genuine Livonia/Courland difference because those two pieces were, for real stretches of history, governed separately. Estonia has no equivalent: whichever single outside power held the territory — Danish and German crusaders, then Sweden, then Russia — held all of it at once. (The one partial exception, Danish rule of the north from 1227 to 1346 while the Livonian Order held the south, is folded into a single "Livonian" era below and covered in prose rather than mapped as a border, since both halves answered to the same crusading order's confederation and no fixed boundary between them is reliably attested.) 1991 – present

Republic of Estonia (restored)

Sovereign stateLennart MeriSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: EU labor migration to Finland & UK (2004–)Immigration: Ukrainian refugees (2022–)

Who & why: the Supreme Council declares a formal transitional break with Soviet law on 30 March 1990 and issues a fuller sovereignty declaration that May; full restoration follows on 20 August 1991, during the failed Moscow coup against Gorbachev, and international recognition (including from the USSR itself) arrives within weeks. Estonia joins NATO and the EU in 2004 and adopts the euro in 2011.

What it represents: the same blue-black-white tricolor flown by the first republic before 1940 is restored exactly, unaltered — a deliberate statement of legal continuity, treating the Soviet decades as an illegal interruption rather than a new beginning. Blue is traditionally read as sky and loyalty, black as the soil and the nation's past suffering, white as the aspiration toward a brighter future and snow.

1940 – 1991

Estonian Soviet Socialist Republic

Soviet republicJohannes KäbinState atheism
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Nazi Germany (1941–44)Emigration: mass deportations to Siberia (1941, 1949)Emigration: "Great Flight" to Sweden & Germany (1944)Immigration: Russian-speaking settlers (Soviet era)

Who & why: annexed by the USSR in August 1940 following the secret protocols of the 1939 Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, alongside Latvia and Lithuania on the same schedule (see the Baltic hub map). Mass deportations to Siberia began within a year. Nazi Germany's occupation from 1941 to 1944, administered as part of Reichskommissariat Ostland, interrupts Soviet rule entirely rather than merely contesting it — a different flag, a different regime, shown as its own map snapshot above — before the Red Army reoccupies the whole territory by autumn 1944. Forest Brothers partisans resisted into the 1950s; the USSR itself never recognized any gap in its own claim of sovereignty across the whole 1940–1991 span.

What it represents: a plain red field with hammer, sickle, and star at first (1940–41, then again 1944–53), redesigned in 1953 to add a wavy white-and-blue stripe near the bottom — a nod to the Baltic Sea and, unusually among Soviet republic flags, one of the only ones to depart from a purely symbolic design toward a literal, local geographic reference.

1918 – 1940

Republic of Estonia (first republic)

RepublicKonstantin PätsSecular, Lutheran-majority
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Baltic Germans resettled to Nazi Germany (1939)

Who & why: independence is declared on 24 February 1918, in the brief gap between the retreating Russian army and the arriving Germans — the same nine-month window in which Lithuania and Latvia also declared their own independence (see the Baltic hub map). The Estonian War of Independence follows immediately, fought through 1918–1920 against both German Freikorps/Landeswehr forces and the Bolshevik Red Army, ending with the Tartu Peace Treaty of February 1920, in which Soviet Russia formally and permanently renounced its claim to Estonia.

What it represents: the blue-black-white tricolor, already decades old as the flag of the Estonian Students' Society (consecrated in 1884 at Ötepää), is adopted as the national flag — the first time it flies over an actual sovereign state rather than a fraternal organization under Tsarist rule.

1721 – 1918

Russian Empire (Governorate of Estland)

Empire's governoratePeter I "the Great"Russian Orthodoxy (imperial state church)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Russia (1721)
Ruling house:House of Romanov (Russia)

Who & why: the 1721 Treaty of Nystad ends the Great Northern War and formally cedes Swedish Estonia and Livonia to Peter the Great's Russia — the same treaty that lets Peter proclaim Russia an empire (see the Baltic hub map). Baltic German nobility retain considerable local privilege and landholding for most of this period even as St. Petersburg governs from above; Russification campaigns intensify sharply in the 1880s–90s, provoking the same national cultural awakening that produces the tricolor's 1884 consecration.

What it represents: the standard Russian Empire civil ensign, white-blue-red, flown as ordinary imperial territory — no distinct flag existed for the Governorate of Estland itself, its administration expressed through coats of arms and seals rather than a separate banner.

1561 – 1721

Swedish Estonia

Kingdom's provinceGustav II AdolfLutheranism (state church)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Sweden (1561)
Ruling house:House of Vasa (Sweden)House of Romanov (Russia)

Who & why: as the Livonian Order collapses under Ivan the Terrible's 1558 invasion, the town council of Reval (Tallinn) and the northern Estonian nobility submit to Swedish protection in 1561 rather than face Russian conquest alone (see the Baltic hub map). Sweden later takes the rest of Estonia and Livonia through the following decades of war, holding the whole territory until Russia's victory in the Great Northern War two centuries later.

What it represents: the plain Swedish Nordic-cross flag, flown as an ordinary crown province. This period is remembered in Estonian folk memory relatively favorably as "vana hea Rootsi aeg" — "the good old Swedish time" — for reforms including expanded local schooling and comparatively lighter serfdom than what followed under Russian rule, a reputation that has outlasted the two intervening centuries since Sweden actually governed here.

1227 – 1561

Livonian Order (Terra Mariana)

Crusader stateVolquinRoman Catholicism (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: German & Danish crusaders (1227)
Ruling house:Livonian Order (Teutonic Knights)House of Oldenburg (Denmark)

Who & why: the Northern Crusades reach Estonia in the early 13th century from two directions at once: German crusaders (the Livonian Brothers of the Sword, merged into the Teutonic Order's Livonian branch after a 1237 reorganization) conquer the south, while Denmark's King Valdemar II takes the north directly, founding Reval (Tallinn) in 1219. By 1227, with the fall of Saaremaa, the whole of Estonia's mainland and islands is under crusader or Danish control, organized loosely as part of the confederation known as Terra Mariana — "Land of Mary." Denmark sells its northern holding to the Livonian Order outright in 1346, following the St. George's Night Uprising of 1343–45, unifying all of Estonia under the Order for the remainder of this period. The Order's own authority steadily erodes against the Hanseatic towns and prince-bishoprics it nominally oversees, until it collapses outright under Russian invasion in 1561.

What it represents: the plain black cross on white associated with the Teutonic Order and its Livonian branch, the crusading confederation's shared heraldic device — flown loosely across a patchwork of bishoprics, the Order's own lands, and Hanseatic town governments rather than a single centralized state.

c. 1000 – 1227

Ancient Estonia (tribal confederations)

Tribal confederationsNo single ruler documentedEstonian paganism
Ruling house:House of Oldenburg (Denmark)

Who & why: pre-conquest Estonia was never a single kingdom but a patchwork of parishes (kihelkonnad) loosely grouped into larger counties (maakonnad) such as Saaremaa, Harju, and Sakala, with no central monarch or unifying government. Estonia and Saaremaa in particular were among the very last pagan regions of Europe to be forcibly Christianized, resisting repeated Danish, Swedish, and Russian raids for over a century before the Northern Crusades finally subdued the whole territory by 1227 — Saaremaa's fall that year marking the last piece to go.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or fixed heraldic device survives from this pre-Christian, pre-state era; individual parishes and their strongholds (linnused) are not known to have flown any standardized banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

The Aestii, Amber Trade & Iron Age Estonia

Aestii / Baltic Iron Age tribesNo single ruler documentedEstonian/Baltic paganism
Migration & conquest:Trade network: Amber Road (to Rome)Salme ship burials: Baltic Sea conflict (c. 750 CE)

Who & why: the Roman historian Tacitus is the first to put this coast on the written record, naming its amber-gathering inhabitants the Aestii in his c. 98 CE Germania — centuries before any Estonian polity is named in its own right. Archaeology fills in the rest: Iron Age hill forts, farming settlements, and, most strikingly, the Salme ship burials on Saaremaa (c. 750 CE), where the mass grave of warriors killed in a Baltic Sea battle was uncovered only in 2008–2012, among the most significant Viking-Age discoveries in the region. This long Iron Age of scattered farming and trading communities gradually consolidates into the named parishes and counties (kihelkonnad, maakonnad) of the following entry.

What it represents: no flag or comparable banner survives or was likely ever made in this era; what survives is archaeological — hill forts, grave goods, amber, and ship timbers. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Tacitus's account of the Aestii dates to the height of the Roman Empire under Trajan, when Baltic amber reached Roman markets via the long-distance Amber Road; the Salme ship burials, roughly contemporary with Charlemagne's Frankish kingdom in the west, fall right at the opening of the Viking Age that would soon bring Scandinavian raiders and traders across the whole Baltic and North Sea world.
