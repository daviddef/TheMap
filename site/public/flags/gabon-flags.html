Flags of Gabon

Gabon's equatorial rainforest was home to Pygmy hunter-gatherer peoples for thousands of years before Bantu-speaking farmers and iron-workers, part of the vast Bantu migration out of the Nigeria–Cameroon borderlands, arrived in successive waves and laid the linguistic foundation for the Myene, Fang, and other peoples later encountered by Europeans. Gabon's capital, Libreville ("free town"), takes its name from its founding in 1849 by a group of Africans freed from an intercepted slave ship and resettled there under French naval protection — a genuinely distinctive origin story among the region's colonial capitals. From that deep prehistory and Myene coastal trading polities through French Equatorial Africa to a 1960 independence and more than half a century of Bongo-family rule underwritten by oil wealth, this page traces three flags across a span of millennia.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Gabon, from its earliest Pygmy and Bantu-migration inhabitants through pre-colonial coastal polities and French colonization to the modern republic. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

From the earliest Pygmy and Bantu-migration peoples through the Myene coastal polities and Fang, Punu, and other interior peoples, no era before European contact formed one mappable state with a stable internal boundary — the timeline itself leaves those eras' flags blank for the same reason. From the 1839 treaties onward, French Gabon, the autonomous republic, and the independent republic were each governed as a single unified territory, so there's no genuine internal line to draw for those eras either.

1960 – present

Gabonese Republic (independent)

Republic, independentLéon M'baSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: West/Central African labor migrants (1970s–)

Who & why: Gabon becomes independent on 17 August 1960 under Léon M'ba, its first president. Omar Bongo succeeds him in 1967 and governs for 42 years until his death in 2009; his son Ali Bongo then rules until a military coup ends the family's 56-year hold on power in August 2023. Gabon's substantial offshore oil reserves have made it one of the wealthier states per capita in the region even as that wealth has concentrated heavily around the ruling circle.

What it represents: the same green-yellow-blue horizontal tricolor adopted at independence, with the central French-tricolor canton of the pre-independence autonomous-republic flag dropped: green for the country's dense equatorial forest, yellow for the equator (which runs directly through Gabon), and blue for the Atlantic coastline.

1959 – 1960

Gabon (autonomous republic within the French Community)

Autonomous republicLéon M'baRoman Catholic (favored, not established)

Who & why: Gabon votes to join the French Community as a self-governing republic in the September 1958 referendum, gaining internal autonomy while France retains control of defense and foreign affairs; this brief transitional status runs until full independence less than two years later, in August 1960.

What it represents: the new green-yellow-blue tricolor design carries a French Tricolor canton in the upper hoist, marking Gabon's continued constitutional tie to France during this transitional period, dropped once independence is achieved.

1839 – 1959

French Gabon

ColonyLouis Édouard Bouët-WillaumezRoman Catholic (favored, not established)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: freed slaves founding Libreville (1849)
Ruling house:Orungu KingdomHouse of Bonaparte

Who & why: France signs its first treaties with Mpongwe (Myene) King Denis on the Gabon estuary in 1839, and in 1849 a French naval vessel resettles a group of Africans freed from an intercepted slave ship at a new outpost named Libreville. Gabon becomes part of French Congo, then a separate colony within French Equatorial Africa from 1910, governed from the federal capital at Brazzaville throughout.

What it represents: the French Tricolor is flown as the colonial ensign throughout; no distinct territorial flag existed for Gabon under direct French colonial rule.

Prehistory – c. 1000 CE

Pygmy Peoples and the Bantu Migration into Gabon

Pygmy hunter-gatherers, then Bantu migrationNo single ruler documentedTraditional/ancestral religion
Migration & settlement:Bantu expansion into the equatorial rainforest

Who & why: long before any of the named coastal or interior peoples recorded in the era just before French contact, Gabon's equatorial forest is home to Pygmy hunter-gatherer communities — ancestors of today's Babongo, Baka, and Bakoya — whose presence in Central Africa stretches back many thousands of years. Beginning roughly three millennia ago, Bantu-speaking farming and iron-working peoples, part of the vast Bantu expansion out of the Nigeria–Cameroon borderlands, migrate south into the region in successive waves, gradually absorbing or displacing the earlier population and laying the linguistic and cultural foundation for the Myene, Fang, and other peoples who inhabit Gabon at the time of European contact.

What it represents: no flag, state, or fixed political symbol survives from this immense span, because none existed in the modern sense; shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the Bantu expansion into Central Africa runs alongside the spread of iron-working technology across sub-Saharan Africa, the rise and fall of Egypt's New Kingdom and the Nubian Kingdom of Kush, the founding of Carthage (814 BCE) and Rome (753 BCE), and, much later, the Christianization of Europe under Rome and Byzantium — entire Old World civilizations rising and falling while Bantu-speaking communities were still in the process of settling the Gabonese rainforest.
