Flags of the German Lands

From nine centuries of stateless Germanic tribes contesting Rome's own frontier, through an eight-century patchwork of hundreds of principalities under a nominal emperor, Napoleon's client kingdoms, a loose 39-state confederation, Prussia's own black-and-white colors driving two wars of unification, an empire, a failed republic, a genocidal dictatorship, four years with no German flag permitted at all, a nation split clean in half for forty-one years, and back to one flag again — eleven entries, and a black-red-gold tricolor first raised by liberal revolutionaries in 1848 that keeps reasserting itself every time the alternative fails.

Germany's own east–west Cold War split already lives as a real traced map on the German Lands hub; this page ports that exact same geometry, but built around Germany's own full ten-entry timeline rather than the hub's six-country snapshot list.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Germany, c. 100 BCE–present, eleven political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

West and East Germany, c. 100 BCE–present

The same real, traced west/east split that lives on the German Lands hub map, ported here and driven by Germany's own timeline. The line dissolves whenever one government ruled both halves (including the two-thousand-year stretch before either half was a state at all), and solidifies hard exactly once — 1949 to 1990, the sharpest and most important division on this whole page. Press play, or drag the slider.

Forty-one years is the only time this specific line was ever real. From Charlemagne's successors to 1945, "west" and "east" Germany were never separately governed — the border below stays dissolved through the Confederation, the Empire, Weimar, and the Third Reich. It solidifies the instant the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic are founded in 1949, and dissolves again — permanently — the moment reunification takes effect on 3 October 1990.

1990 – present

Federal Republic of Germany (reunified)

RepublicHelmut KohlNo formal state religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Syrian & other refugees (2015–)

Who & why: mass protests through 1989, the fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November, and the East German Volkskammer's own vote to accede force the issue; formal reunification under the Federal Republic's Basic Law takes effect 3 October 1990, chosen deliberately over any date tied to 1871 or 1933. Germany joins the fully sovereign community of nations for the first time since 1945.

What it represents: the same black-red-gold tricolor the Federal Republic had flown since 1949, now simply extended over the whole reunified country — no redesign, no new symbol, a deliberate statement of unbroken continuity rather than a fresh start.

1949 – 1990

Federal Republic of Germany (West) / German Democratic Republic (East)

West: RepublicKonrad AdenauerSecular (no state religion)East: Communist stateWilhelm PieckState atheism
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Turkish guest workers (1961–)Emigration: East Germans fleeing west (1949–61)

Who & why: the three Western occupation zones consolidate into the Federal Republic of Germany, founded 23 May 1949 in Bonn; the Soviet zone answers five months later, founding the German Democratic Republic on 7 October 1949 in East Berlin. For forty-one years two German states exist side by side — a NATO-aligned parliamentary democracy in the west, a Soviet-aligned one-party state in the east — separated after 1961 by the Berlin Wall itself.

What it represents: both states adopt the same black-red-gold tricolor at first, each claiming to be the sole legitimate Germany. The GDR breaks the symmetry in 1959, adding a state emblem (hammer and compass ringed by a wreath of grain) once reunification looks less likely — from that point the same three colors read as two different countries at a glance.

1945 – 1949

Allied-occupied Germany

Occupied territoryNo single ruler documentedNo formal state religion
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: US / UK / France / USSRImmigration: ethnic German expellees from the east

Who & why: unconditional surrender on 8 May 1945 ends the Third Reich; the four victorious Allied powers — the United States, Britain, France, and the Soviet Union — divide the country into occupation zones under the Allied Control Council, with Berlin itself split four ways inside the Soviet zone. No sovereign German government of any kind exists.

What it represents: the Nazi swastika flag is banned outright, and no replacement German national flag is authorized for civilian use anywhere in the country during these four years — ships and public buildings fly the flags of the occupying powers instead. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

1933 – 1945

Nazi Germany (Third Reich)

Fascist / totalitarian stateAdolf HitlerNo genuine established church
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Jewish & political refugees fleeing persecution

Who & why: President Hindenburg appoints Adolf Hitler Chancellor on 30 January 1933; the Reichstag Fire Decree and the March 1933 Enabling Act dismantle democratic government within weeks, but 30 January is the standard start point for the regime itself. Hindenburg's 1934 death lets Hitler merge the presidency and chancellorship as Führer. The regime's rearmament, expansion, and the Holocaust culminate in a war that kills tens of millions before unconditional surrender in May 1945.

What it represents: the black-red-gold tricolor is abolished; from 1935 the swastika banner becomes the sole national flag, a totalizing break from every prior German state's symbolism. Shown here in stylized, abstracted form rather than reproducing the emblem itself.

1918 – 1933

Weimar Republic

RepublicFriedrich EbertSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicates on 9 November 1918 as defeat and revolution topple the Empire; the republic is proclaimed the same day, and a National Assembly meeting in Weimar — Berlin still unsettled by street fighting — adopts a democratic constitution in August 1919. Hyperinflation, the Great Depression, and relentless attacks from both the far left and far right leave it chronically unstable until Hitler's appointment ends it in January 1933.

What it represents: the black-red-gold tricolor first raised by liberal revolutionaries and the Frankfurt Parliament in 1848 becomes, for the first time, an actual national flag — a deliberate assertion of continuity with Germany's own democratic, not monarchical, tradition.

1871 – 1918

German Empire (Kaiserreich)

Empire / kingdomWilhelm IProtestant-led (no single imperial church)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Ruhr Poles (1870s–)Emigration: to the United States (peak 1870s–90s)
Ruling house:House of HohenzollernHouse of WittelsbachHouse of Welf

Who & why: victory over France in the Franco-Prussian War lets Otto von Bismarck proclaim the German Empire at Versailles on 18 January 1871, with King Wilhelm I of Prussia crowned Kaiser. It is Bismarck's deliberate "small German" solution — Austria permanently excluded — and Prussia dominates the new empire outright: two-thirds of its territory and population, and the Prussian king as hereditary emperor.

What it represents: black-white-red keeps the North German Confederation's flag exactly — Prussia's own black-and-white state colors combined with the red-and-white of the Hanseatic trading cities — now flying over a unified empire rather than a federal union. The throughline from Prussia's own banner to Germany's is now complete and unbroken.

1866 – 1871

North German Confederation

Confederal unionWilhelm I of PrussiaProtestant-led (no single established church)
Ruling house:House of HohenzollernHouse of WittelsbachHouse of Welf

Who & why: Prussia's decisive victory over Austria at Königgrätz in July 1866 ends the old German Confederation and, crucially, ends any question of Austria leading a unified Germany. The Peace of Prague that August dissolves the Confederation outright; Prussia organizes the German states north of the Main into a new federal union, formalized by a constitution that takes effect 1 July 1867, with the Prussian king as its president and Bismarck as chancellor — a Prussian-led state in almost everything but name.

What it represents: the very first appearance of black-white-red, deliberately combining Prussia's own black-and-white state colors with the red-and-white of the Hanseatic port cities that Prussia now leads — a flag literally engineered to signal "Prussia plus its northern allies," four years before that same combination becomes an empire's flag.

1815 – 1866

German Confederation

Confederal unionFrancis I of AustriaNo single state religion
Ruling house:House of HohenzollernHouse of WittelsbachHouse of Welf

Who & why: the 1815 Congress of Vienna, filling the void Napoleon left when he dissolved the Holy Roman Empire, creates a loose league of 39 sovereign German states under Austrian presidency, with Prussia as its powerful rival member from the start. The Confederation has a federal diet at Frankfurt but no real central government, no common army, and no common flag of its own — the 1848 Revolution's Frankfurt Parliament briefly tries and fails to build a unified liberal state.

What it represents: black-red-gold is adopted by student fraternities and then the 1848 revolutionaries as the symbol of the liberal-national unity the Confederation itself never delivers — it is never the Confederation's official flag. Alongside it, Prussia's own black eagle on white: the Hohenzollern kingdom's real state banner, already the strongest single member, already the state that will drive both wars of unification within the next two decades.

1806 – 1815

Confederation of the Rhine (Napoleonic client states)

Fragmented / nominal realmNapoleon BonaparteReligiously mixed (no single establishment)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Napoleonic France
Ruling house:House of HohenzollernHouse of WittelsbachHouse of Welf

Who & why: Napoleon forces the dissolution of the thousand-year-old Holy Roman Empire on 6 August 1806, organizing sixteen (later up to 36) client states — Bavaria and Württemberg elevated to kingdoms, Baden and Hesse to grand duchies — into the Confederation of the Rhine under his own protection. It collapses the moment Napoleon's power does, after the catastrophic 1812 Russian campaign and defeat at Leipzig in 1813; the 1815 Congress of Vienna replaces it with the German Confederation.

What it represents: similar to the Napoleonic-era interruptions elsewhere in this atlas, no single confederal flag existed — each client kingdom kept and flew its own colors under Paris's protection, not a shared German banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than inventing one.

c. 962 – 1806

Holy Roman Empire (German lands)

Fragmented / nominal realmOtto I “the Great”Roman Catholic (until 1555 religious division)
Ruling house:House of HabsburgHouse of WittelsbachHouse of WelfHouse of Hohenzollern

Who & why: Otto I's coronation as Emperor in 962 traditionally marks the start of what later becomes known as the Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation — never a unified state, but a shifting elective realm of hundreds of individual kingdoms, duchies, prince-bishoprics, and free cities, loosely bound to an emperor with real power that waxed and waned across eight and a half centuries, until Napoleon forces Francis II to dissolve it in 1806.

What it represents: the gold field and black double-headed eagle was the Emperor's own personal and imperial banner, not a flag of "Germany" as a sovereign state — the individual German lands within the Empire each kept their own separate coats of arms and colors throughout. Shown here honestly as the imperial banner only, with that scope made explicit rather than implying a unified German flag that never existed.

c. 100 BCE – 800 CE

Germanic Tribes & the Roman Frontier

Fragmented / tribal confederationsArminius (Hermann)Germanic paganism (until 8th c. Christianization)
Migration & conquest:Attempted conquest by: Rome (failed, 9 CE)Emigration: Gothic & Vandal peoples into the Roman Empire (3rd–5th c.)

Who & why: for roughly nine centuries before Otto I's coronation, "Germany" is not a place with a government but a linguistic and cultural zone of independent tribes east of the Rhine and north of the Danube — first in intermittent contact and conflict with Rome, then gradually consolidating under Frankish rule from the 5th century onward. Charlemagne, a Frank, is crowned Holy Roman Emperor in 800, uniting most of the Germanic-speaking lands (plus Gaul and Italy) under one crown for the first time; the Carolingian Empire's 843 partition among his grandsons leaves East Francia as the direct territorial ancestor of the kingdom Otto I inherits in 936 and crowns into an empire in 962.

What it represents: no flag from this era survives or was likely ever created in any modern sense — shown here honestly blank. Tribes carried standards (the Romans record animal totems and carved wooden or bronze figures on poles) but nothing resembling a woven national flag.

World context: this span runs alongside the building of Rome's Colosseum (70–80 CE) and Pantheon (126 CE), the height and slow decline of the Han dynasty in China, the life of Muhammad and the rapid 7th-century Arab conquests, and Charlemagne's own 800 coronation in Rome — a deliberate echo of the fallen Western Roman Empire three centuries after its last emperor was deposed in 476.
