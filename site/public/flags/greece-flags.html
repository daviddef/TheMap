Flags of Greece

Greek soil carries the longest continuously documented civilizational thread on this whole atlas: Minoan Crete's palaces at Knossos rise around 2700 BCE, followed by Mycenaean Greece, the Greek Dark Age, Archaic and Classical city-states, Alexander's Hellenistic empire, Roman rule, and a thousand years of Byzantine Empire — nearly four millennia before Crusader knights carve up a broken Byzantium in 1204. From there, this page continues through a millennium-adjacent stretch of Ottoman rule, a nine-year war of independence, three kingdoms interrupted twice by republics, a Nazi occupation split three ways, and a seven-year colonels' junta — thirteen entries in all, and, for the modern era, a blue-and-white cross that has flown, in one form or another, over almost every one of the last two centuries.

The cross itself long predates the modern striped flag: a plain white cross on blue was already flying as an improvised revolutionary banner in 1821, and blue and white were fixed as Greece's national colors by the first National Assembly at Epidaurus in January 1822 — the same colors, reasserted after every occupation, dictatorship, and regime change since.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Greece, c. 2700 BCE–present, thirteen political entities. The nearly 4,000-year ancient-through-Byzantine span is compressed into a single opening entry, since no flag or unified state exists across it and the map below is only built for the modern, post-1204 boundary history; treat that entry as a narrative bridge rather than a single flag era. A note on the map: Greece's official boundary data is a MultiPolygon — the mainland plus dozens of islands, down to tiny Aegean rocks. For the map below, this page uses the mainland polygon only (which includes the Peloponnese, joined to it in this data as one ring); Crete, the Ionian islands, and the Aegean archipelago are real Greek territory throughout this history but are simplified out of the map graphics entirely. Unlike most pages in this atlas, the map's border outline itself actually changes shape across four real turning points — 1832, 1881, 1913, and 1923/present — rather than reusing one modern outline throughout; two of those four (1913 and 1923/present) are decimated from genuine historical-boundaries GeoJSON data (aourednik/historical-basemaps and the modern johan/world.geo.json outline this atlas already uses elsewhere), while the other two (1832 and 1881, for which no machine-readable dataset was found) are careful hand approximations built from the real modern coastline cut along the documented treaty frontiers — see the note above the map for specifics. Greece's changing shape, 1832–present

Instead of reusing one modern outline throughout, this map's border shape actually grows at four real turning points — 1832 (the original small kingdom), 1881 (Thessaly and Arta annexed), 1913 (post–Balkan Wars, nearly doubling the country), and 1923/present (post–Lausanne, essentially modern). Two internal splits are layered on top of that growing outline where a single government stopped actually being true: the 1916–17 National Schism — Eleftherios Venizelos's pro-Allied "Provisional Government of National Defence" in Thessaloniki against King Constantine I's officially neutral royal government in Athens, on the 1913-era border — and the 1941–44 Axis occupation, when Germany, Italy, and Bulgaria carved up the (by then modern-bordered) country, with Bulgaria formally annexing Macedonia and Thrace outright rather than merely occupying them. Both 1916 governments flew the same flag; the 1941 occupiers didn't. During the National Schism there was no rival banner — that split was entirely political, a fight over who actually spoke for Greece. During the 1941–44 occupation, by contrast, the map below distinguishes Bulgaria's outright annexation of Macedonia and Thrace (shown in its own color, since that territory left Greek sovereignty in law, not just in fact) from the combined German/Italian occupation of the rest of the country (shown as one zone here; the map doesn't further separate German-run Athens and Crete from the Italian-run remainder). Both internal lines trace roughly the old 1881–1912 Greek-Ottoman border, the boundary between "Old Greece" and the Macedonian territory won in the Balkan Wars — close to where both the 1916–17 and 1941–44 splits actually ran. Each line solidifies only for the years its split was physically real, and dissolves the moment the country is reunified: June 1917 for the Schism, October 1944 for liberation.

Hellenic Republic (Third Republic)

RepublicConstantine KaramanlisEastern Orthodox Christianity (constitutionally "prevailing religion")
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Albanian migrants (1991–)Immigration: Syrian & other refugees (2015–16)Emigration: brain drain during debt crisis (2010s)

Who & why: the junta collapses in July 1974 after its disastrous attempt to annex Cyprus triggers a Turkish invasion; Constantine Karamanlis returns from exile in Paris to lead the "Metapolitefsi," the transition to democracy. A December 1974 referendum abolishes the monarchy for good (69% against its return), and a new constitution follows in 1975. Greece joins the EEC in 1981 and adopts the euro in 2001.

What it represents: the same nine-stripe blue-white cross flag flown since the 19th century, its official shade standardized by law in 1978 — the design itself never changed across kingdom, republic, occupation, or junta; only who stood beneath it did.

1967 – 1974

Greek military junta ("Regime of the Colonels")

Military dictatorshipGeorgios PapadopoulosEastern Orthodox Christianity (state church)
Ruling house:House of Glücksburg

Who & why: a group of army colonels led by Georgios Papadopoulos seizes power in a coup on 21 April 1967, citing a supposed communist threat ahead of scheduled elections. King Constantine II's own countercoup attempt that December fails, sending him into exile for good. A further internal coup by Dimitrios Ioannidis in November 1973 hardens the regime shortly before its own collapse over Cyprus.

What it represents: no new flag — the colonels ruled under the existing national colors, occasionally paired with a phoenix emblem on government seals and propaganda, but the flag itself stayed the unaltered blue-white cross throughout.

1944 – 1967

Kingdom of Greece (postwar)

KingdomArchbishop Damaskinos (Regent)Eastern Orthodox Christianity (established Church of Greece)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: civil-war refugees to the Eastern Bloc (1949–)Emigration: postwar labor migration (1950s–70s)
Ruling house:House of Glücksburg

Who & why: Athens is liberated in October 1944 and a government of national unity returns from exile, but tension between royalist and communist resistance factions explodes into the "Dekemvriana" clashes within weeks. A September 1946 plebiscite restores King George II; full-scale civil war between the government and the communist Democratic Army of Greece runs 1946–1949, ending in government victory with heavy American and British backing. George II dies in 1947 and is succeeded by his brother Paul, then Paul's son Constantine II in 1964.

What it represents: the same national flag, unbroken in legal continuity from the government-in-exile through liberation, plebiscite, and civil war — the monarchy restored, but the colors never needed restoring at all.

1941 – 1944

Axis occupation & government-in-exile

Occupied / foreign ruleGeorgios TsolakoglouEastern Orthodox Christianity (unchanged under occupation)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Germany / Italy / BulgariaEmigration: Holocaust deportation of Thessaloniki's Jews (1943)
Ruling house:House of Glücksburg

Who & why: after repelling Mussolini's October 1940 invasion, Greece falls to a German intervention in April 1941; the Battle of Crete ends organized resistance by early June. The country is carved into German, Italian, and Bulgarian occupation zones — Bulgaria annexing Greek Macedonia and Thrace outright — while the legitimate government flees first to Crete, then Egypt and London. Mass famine kills tens of thousands over the winter of 1941–42; resistance movements EAM-ELAS and EDES fight the occupiers and, increasingly, each other.

What it represents: the same blue-white cross, flown by the government-in-exile as its claim to unbroken legal continuity, even as three separate foreign administrations ran the country it claimed to govern.

1935 – 1941

Kingdom of Greece (restored)

KingdomGeorge IIEastern Orthodox Christianity (established Church of Greece)
Ruling house:House of Glücksburg

Who & why: a heavily manipulated November 1935 referendum restores King George II after eleven chaotic republican years. Unable to form a stable government, George appoints General Ioannis Metaxas prime minister in 1936, who suspends parliament in August and rules by decree as the "4th of August Regime" — an authoritarian, if idiosyncratic, dictatorship under a king who never fully controlled it. Metaxas's defiant "No" (Ochi) to Mussolini's October 1940 ultimatum becomes a founding national legend.

What it represents: the same restored royal tricolor-cross, with Metaxas's own regime adding fasces-adjacent symbolism to propaganda but never altering the flag itself.

1924 – 1935

Second Hellenic Republic

RepublicPavlos KountouriotisEastern Orthodox Christianity (established Church of Greece)

Who & why: the National Assembly declares a republic on 25 March 1924 after King George II is forced out, the monarchy discredited by the Asia Minor catastrophe. The republic is chronically unstable — more than a dozen governments and several coup attempts in eleven years, including a failed 1935 Venizelist coup that ends Venizelos's own political career. A rigged November 1935 referendum restores the king before the year is out.

What it represents: the identical blue-white cross flag — Greece's flag carried no royal crown or crest to strip out when the monarchy fell, unlike many of its neighbors, so the republic's break with the kingdom shows up in its institutions, not its banner.

1913 – 1924

Kingdom of Greece (Balkan Wars to catastrophe)

KingdomConstantine IEastern Orthodox Christianity (established Church of Greece)
Ruling house:House of Glücksburg
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Greek Orthodox refugees from Asia Minor (1922–23)Emigration: Muslims removed to Turkey (1923)

Who & why: the Balkan Wars of 1912–13 nearly double Greece's territory and population, adding Macedonia, Epirus, Crete, and the northern Aegean islands, just before King George I's assassination in Thessaloniki hands the throne to Constantine I. The 1916–17 National Schism (see the map above) splits the country between Constantine's neutral Athens and Venizelos's pro-Allied Thessaloniki before Venizelos reunites it. Emboldened, Venizelos pushes a 1919 landing at Smyrna that collapses into catastrophic defeat by 1922, followed by a massive forced population exchange with Turkey under the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne. Constantine, restored by an intervening 1920 plebiscite, abdicates again in the defeat's aftermath.

What it represents: the same flag flew over Greece's greatest 20th-century territorial expansion and its greatest catastrophe within the same decade — through a genuine internal partition, a war won, and a war catastrophically lost.

1832 – 1913

Kingdom of Greece (original)

KingdomOtto of BavariaEastern Orthodox Christianity (constitutionally established)
Ruling house:House of WittelsbachHouse of Glücksburg
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the United States (1890s–1913)

Who & why: the 1832 London Protocol, signed by Britain, France, and Russia, recognizes full Greek independence and installs the seventeen-year-old Otto of Bavaria as king — a foreign monarch imposed on a country that had just fought nine years to rule itself. Otto is deposed by a bloodless 1862 revolt; the Great Powers replace him with the Danish prince George I in 1863, whose fifty-year reign becomes the longest of any modern Greek monarch, ending with his 1913 assassination just after the Balkan Wars' territorial gains.

What it represents: the nine-stripe blue-white cross flag, essentially the modern design, flying continuously as the kingdom's civil and state flag for eight decades of nation-building, border wars, and slow territorial expansion beyond the small original state.

1821 – 1832

Greek War of Independence

Revolutionary / provisional governmentAlexandros MavrokordatosEastern Orthodox Christianity (revolutionary state faith)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Chios massacre & deportations (1822)

Who & why: the uprising begins in March 1821 across the Peloponnese and spreads rapidly, though early Greek unity is fragile — rival regional factions fight both the Ottomans and, at times, each other, cycling through several rival provisional governments and constitutions (Epidaurus 1822, Astros 1823, Troezen 1827) before the Great Powers' 1827 naval intervention at Navarino turns the tide. Ioannis Kapodistrias governs as the fragile state's first head from 1828 until his 1831 assassination, and the 1832 London Protocol finally installs Otto as king.

What it represents: the blue-white cross, fixed as the national colors by the First National Assembly at Epidaurus in January 1822 — blue for the sea and sky, white for the purity of the cause — already recognizably the ancestor of every Greek flag that follows, even before the state itself had settled its own government.

1460 – 1821

Ottoman rule

Occupied / foreign ruleMehmed IISunni Islam (Ottoman state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: devshirme child levy to IstanbulEmigration: merchant diaspora to Central/Eastern Europe

Who & why: the Despotate of the Morea, the Byzantines' last stronghold on the Greek mainland, falls to Sultan Mehmed II's forces in 1460, a year after Constantinople itself; the last scattered pockets of Byzantine and Venetian resistance in the Aegean mop up over the following decades. Nearly four centuries of Ottoman administration follow, organized into shifting sanjaks and eyalets, with periods of Venetian reconquest along parts of the coast (notably a 1684–1715 Venetian hold on the Peloponnese) interrupting but not ending Ottoman control until the 1821 uprising.

What it represents: the standard Ottoman red field with crescent and star, flown over Greek lands with no distinct territorial flag of their own — the same banner as the rest of the Sultan's domains.

1261 – 1460

Byzantine restoration (Despotate of the Morea)

Kingdom / empireMichael VIII PalaiologosEastern Orthodox Christianity (Byzantine state church)
Ruling house:Palaiologos Dynasty

Who & why: the Nicaean emperor Michael VIII Palaiologos retakes Constantinople in 1261, ending the Latin Empire and restoring Byzantine rule, though large parts of Greece remain under Frankish and Venetian control for generations yet. From 1349, the reclaimed Peloponnese is governed as the semi-autonomous Despotate of the Morea, often ruled by a son or brother of the reigning emperor from its capital at Mystras — a genuine cultural flowering even as the empire around it shrinks to almost nothing. Constantinople itself falls to the Ottomans in 1453; the Morea, the empire's last substantial territory, holds out seven years longer.

What it represents: the black double-headed eagle associated with the Palaiologos dynasty, symbolizing the emperor's claim over both East and West even as actual Byzantine territory dwindled to a handful of enclaves — shown here as an informed reconstruction, since no single fixed state flag in the modern sense was used.

1204 – 1261

Frankokratia (Crusader states of Greece)

Occupied / foreign ruleBaldwin I of ConstantinopleRoman Catholicism (imposed by Latin rulers)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Fourth Crusade knights & Venice (1204)

Who & why: the Fourth Crusade's catastrophic 1204 sack of Constantinople shatters Byzantine authority, and Western knights carve up Greek lands among themselves: the Duchy of Athens under the Burgundian de la Roche family, the Principality of Achaea in the Peloponnese under the Villehardouins, and scattered Venetian holdings (Crete, Negroponte, and Aegean island lordships) controlling the sea lanes. Byzantine successor states — based in Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond — contest this patchwork from the margins for the next half-century.

What it represents: no single flag, because there was no single state — each Crusader lordship flew its own Western European heraldic banner (the de la Roche and Villehardouin arms among them), and Venice its own lion of St. Mark over its island and coastal holdings. Shown here honestly blank rather than picking one lord's arms to stand in for all of them.

c. 2700 BCE – 1204 CE

Minoan Crete to Byzantine Greece

Ancient & medieval civilization (fragmented across nearly 4,000 years)No single ruler documentedGreek polytheism, later Christianity (4th c. CE onward)
Migration & settlement:Greek colonization of the Mediterranean & Black Sea (8th–6th c. BCE)

Who & why: present-day Greek territory's documented history begins with Minoan Crete, a Bronze Age palatial civilization centered on Knossos from around 2700 BCE, followed on the mainland by Mycenaean Greece (c. 1750–1050 BCE), the palace culture that later Greeks themselves remembered, half-legendarily, as the age of the Trojan War. A "Dark Age" of population decline and lost literacy follows the Bronze Age Collapse, before the Archaic period (8th–6th c. BCE) sees the rise of independent city-states (poleis) such as Athens, Sparta, Corinth, and Thebes, and a wave of Greek colonization across the Mediterranean and Black Sea. The Classical era (5th–4th c. BCE) brings Athenian democracy, the Persian Wars, and the Peloponnesian War, before Philip II and Alexander the Great of Macedon unify Greece by force and Alexander's conquests spread Greek culture across the Hellenistic world (334–30 BCE). Rome absorbs Greece as a province from 146 BCE, and when the Roman Empire itself splits, its eastern half survives for another millennium as the Byzantine Empire, ruled from Constantinople and Greek-speaking in all but name by its later centuries — until the Fourth Crusade's 1204 sack shatters it into the Frankish and Venetian patchwork that opens the very next entry on this page.

What it represents: no single flag or fixed heraldic symbol unifies this nearly 4,000-year span, because nothing resembling a modern national flag existed for most of it, and no single state was continuously in control throughout it; Byzantium's later Palaiologos dynasty did carry a double-headed eagle emblem on some of its own banners, but that belongs to a narrow final slice of this entry's span, not to Minoan Crete, Mycenae, Periclean Athens, or Alexander's empire alike, so no single image is offered here. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span opens as Egypt's Old Kingdom pharaohs are still building the Great Pyramids of Giza, runs through the traditional date of the Trojan War and the Bronze Age Collapse, the construction of the Parthenon atop the Acropolis (447–432 BCE) during Athens's Golden Age, the building of the Library of Alexandria and the Colossus of Rhodes (one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, raised 280 BCE) under Alexander's Hellenistic successors, the completion of Constantinople's Hagia Sophia under Justinian (537 CE), and closes on the eve of the Fourth Crusade — by which point Rome itself has risen, ruled the known world, and fallen (476 CE) entirely within the span of this single Greek entry.
