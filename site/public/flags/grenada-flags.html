Flags of Grenada

Grenada's human history begins nearly two thousand years before Columbus: Saladoid-culture settlers reach the island around the 3rd century BCE, part of a migration up the Lesser Antilles from South America, and their Igneri descendants hold it for well over a millennium before Kalinago (Island Carib) peoples arrive and establish the dominance Europeans find in 1498. From there, Grenada — the Kalinago's Camerhogne — passed from indigenous control to a violently established French settlement in 1650, then bounced between France and Britain through the eighteenth century's wars before settling into British hands for good in 1783. Independence in 1974 produced the flag Grenada still flies today, a banner that outlasted a Marxist revolution, an American invasion, and everything in between.

Six entries across nearly 2,300 years: no documented banner for the Saladoid, Igneri, or Kalinago eras, the French royal ensign, Britain's early colonial flag through the French interlude, the lasting British colonial Blue Ensign, and the red-yellow-green independence flag with its star and nutmeg pod — the mark of the Spice Isle.

Scope of this page: the island of present-day Grenada (with Carriacou and Petite Martinique), c. 300 BCE–present, six political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Independent Grenada

Independent stateEric GairyNo established church
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the US, Canada & UK (1970s–)
Ruling house:British Crown

Who & why: Grenada gains independence from Britain on 7 February 1974 under Premier Eric Gairy, who becomes its first Prime Minister. This same flag flies through the major political upheaval that follows without ever changing: the 13 March 1979 Grenada Revolution, in which Maurice Bishop's Marxist-Leninist New Jewel Movement overthrows Gairy in a coup and installs the People's Revolutionary Government; Bishop's arrest and execution amid internal party conflict in October 1983; and the US-led “Operation Urgent Fury” invasion days later, on 25 October 1983, which ends the revolutionary government and restores parliamentary rule. Every one of these is a change of government, not of flag.

What it represents: a rectangle split into yellow triangles (top and bottom) and green triangles (hoist and fly), framed by a red border carrying a small yellow star in each corner and a larger star in a red disc at the center; a stylized golden nutmeg pod sits on the hoist-side green triangle, marking Grenada's identity as the “Spice Isle,” one of the world's leading nutmeg producers.

British Grenada (final and lasting British rule)

British colonyNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic tradition / Anglican establishment
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Indian indentured laborers (1850s–80s)Emigration: Windrush-era migration to Britain (1950s–60s)
Ruling house:British Crown

Who & why: the 1783 Treaty of Paris, ending the American Revolutionary War, confirms Grenada as British again after France's wartime recapture of the island. Britain then holds it continuously from here through independence — a long span of sugar-then-nutmeg-and-cocoa plantation agriculture built on enslaved labor, with emancipation arriving in 1834 (full freedom by 1838), followed by indentured labor from India and elsewhere, and membership in the short-lived West Indies Federation from 1958 to 1962.

What it represents: the British colonial Blue Ensign — the Union Jack in the canton on a blue field — with a Grenada colonial badge in the fly bearing a stylized crescent-shaped harbor and hill scene, the standard pattern for British Caribbean colonies of the era.

1763 – 1783

Britain's first possession, and the French recapture

British / French, contestedNo single ruler documentedRoman Catholic tradition
Ruling house:British Crown

Who & why: a bridging span. France cedes Grenada to Britain in the 1763 Treaty of Paris, which closes the Seven Years' War, after nearly a century and a quarter of French control (see the next entry). Britain's hold does not last: French forces recapture the island in 1779, during the American Revolutionary War, and hold it until the 1783 Treaty of Paris restores it to Britain for good. Rather than split this short, contested stretch into separate cards, it is shown here honestly as one bridging entry, with the French interlude explained in the prose.

What it represents: the flag of the power controlling Grenada at the start of this span — Britain's Red Ensign in 1763, the Union flag on a red field, the common maritime and colonial ensign of the period before the Blue Ensign became the settled convention for colonial government use.

French Grenada

French colonyJacques Dyel du ParquetRoman Catholic (established)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: France (1650)Immigration: enslaved Africans (from 1650s)
Ruling house:House of BourbonBritish Crown

Who & why: French colonists from Martinique, led by Jacques Dyel du Parquet, purchase Grenada's claim from the Compagnie des Îles de l'Amérique and establish a permanent settlement in 1650, after a period of fierce Kalinago resistance that ends brutally. A 1651 massacre at what is still called Le Morne des Sauteurs (“Leapers' Hill”) — where the last Kalinago defenders are said to have jumped to their deaths rather than surrender — effectively ends organized indigenous resistance on the island.

What it represents: the plain white French royal and colonial ensign, carrying the gold Bourbon fleur-de-lis, flown over French overseas possessions of this era.

Kalinago Grenada

Kalinago / pre-colonialNo single ruler documentedKalinago spiritual tradition

Who & why: Columbus sights the island in 1498 but does not land. Grenada, which the Kalinago called “Camerhogne,” remains under Kalinago control, with repeated Spanish and other European attempts at settlement resisted, until the French finally establish a permanent, violently imposed foothold in 1650.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this era; the Kalinago did not use heraldic banners in the European sense, and inventing one would misrepresent them. Shown here honestly as a “no flag documented” placeholder.

Saladoid and Igneri Settlement of Grenada

Saladoid & Igneri settlementNo single ruler documentedAmerindian spiritual tradition (specifics undocumented)
Migration & conquest:Saladoid migration from South America, later displaced/absorbed by Kalinago arrival

Who & why: Grenada's first known inhabitants arrive from the South American mainland around the 3rd century BCE as part of the broader Saladoid migration up the Lesser Antilles island chain, bringing distinctive painted pottery and settled village life; archaeological sites such as Pearls, on the island's northeastern coast, preserve this occupation. Their Igneri (Arawakan-speaking) descendants continue to inhabit Grenada for well over a thousand years, until Kalinago (Island Carib) peoples, migrating up the same island chain in turn, arrive and establish the dominance that Columbus's ships find in 1498, the point at which this page's next entry begins.

What it represents: no flag or comparable political symbol survives, or is documented, from this era; shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the Saladoid migration up the Caribbean island chain runs alongside the height of Classic Maya civilization in Mesoamerica, the flourishing of the Nazca culture and its enigmatic desert lines in Peru (c. 500 BCE–500 CE), and, much later, the rise of the Aztec Empire in the 14th century — a reminder that Grenada's earliest history belongs to the pre-Columbian Americas' own timeline, entirely apart from the Old World civilizations referenced elsewhere in this atlas.
