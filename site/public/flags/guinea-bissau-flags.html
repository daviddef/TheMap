Flags of Guinea-Bissau

From the western reach of the Mandinka Kingdom of Kaabu — a successor and one-time vassal of the Mali Empire that dominated this coast for over six centuries — through nearly five hundred years of Portuguese colonial rule built on one of the earliest and most exploitative Atlantic slave-trade footholds in Africa, into Amílcar Cabral's guerrilla war for independence, and finally a sovereign republic flying Pan-African colors: five flags across roughly eight centuries.

This is a small country with an outsized role in African decolonization — the PAIGC's armed struggle from 1963 is one of the era's defining liberation wars, and Guinea-Bissau's 1973 declaration of independence, forced through before Portugal itself had thrown off dictatorship, helped trigger the 1974 Carnation Revolution that ended Portuguese colonialism everywhere at once.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Guinea-Bissau, c. 1230–present, five political entities. Kaabu's inland Mandinka rule and Portugal's coastal trading-post presence genuinely overlapped for centuries — Portuguese ships were trading (and taking captives) along this coast from the 1440s while Kaabu still governed the interior. This page follows the convention used elsewhere in the atlas: each entry covers the entity whose flag and rule are the clearest continuous thread for the territory during that span, with Kaabu's fall to Fula forces in 1867 marked as the hinge point where Portugal's presence hardens from coastal trading posts into an inland colonial claim. 1973 – present

Republic of Guinea-Bissau

Republic, independentLuís CabralNo official state religion (secular)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to Portugal & Senegal (1990s–)

Who & why: the PAIGC unilaterally declares independence on 24 September 1973, months after Amílcar Cabral's January 1973 assassination robs him of the moment he had spent a decade building toward. Portugal does not recognize it at first, but the April 1974 Carnation Revolution topples the Estado Novo dictatorship in Lisbon, and the new Portuguese government formally recognizes Guinea-Bissau's independence on 10 September 1974. Luís Cabral, Amílcar's half-brother, becomes the first president.

What it represents: the flag adopted at independence is the PAIGC's own party banner, unchanged, made into a national flag — Pan-African red, yellow, black, and green, closely related to Ghana's flag and to those of other PAIGC-aligned movements, and a visible echo of the party's founding vision of a single Guinea-Bissau–Cape Verde federation, a merger the two countries in the end never actually completed.

1963 – 1973

PAIGC armed struggle (liberated zones)

Liberation movementAmílcar CabralNo official religion (secular liberation movement)

Who & why: the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde (PAIGC), founded in 1956 by Amílcar Cabral, turns to armed struggle in January 1963 after peaceful organizing is met with colonial violence, most notoriously the August 1959 Pidjiguiti dockworkers' massacre. Fighting a genuinely effective guerrilla campaign, the PAIGC controls large stretches of the rural interior by the mid-1960s, running schools, clinics, and courts in what it calls the liberated zones even as Portugal still holds the towns. Cabral, the movement's strategist and voice abroad, is assassinated in Conakry on 20 January 1973, almost certainly on the orders of a rival within the party working with Portuguese intelligence.

What it represents: the PAIGC's party flag — a black hoist band with a red star, yellow over green in the fly — flies over the territory the movement actually governs. It is the direct template for the national flag adopted a few months after Cabral's death, with the hoist recolored red and the star black.

1911 – 1963

Portuguese Guinea (colony, Republic era)

ColonyManuel de ArriagaNo official state religion (Portugal's Catholic Church disestablished in 1911)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: forced contract labor to São Tomé

Who & why: Portugal's 1910 revolution replaces the monarchy with a republic and, in 1911, a new national flag that flies over every Portuguese possession including Guinea, formally organized as a separate colony (Guiné Portuguesa) since 1879. Portuguese control on the ground stays thin and heavily contested for decades — so-called “pacification” campaigns against Bijagós, Balanta, and other resisting peoples continue into the 1930s under the later Salazar dictatorship, which treats Guinea as a source of forced labor and cash-crop exports while investing almost nothing in schools or infrastructure, conditions that feed directly into the independence movement that follows.

What it represents: Portugal's green-and-red national flag, adopted in 1911 and still in use today, carries the armillary sphere and shield from the old royal arms into the new republican era; it is flown, not a distinct colonial design, across the whole Portuguese empire during this span.

1867 – 1911

Portuguese Guinea (colony, Kingdom era)

ColonyLuís I of PortugalRoman Catholicism (Portugal's state religion)
Ruling house:House of Braganza

Who & why: Portuguese traders have worked this coast since Nuno Tristão's 1446 voyage, running one of the earliest and most exploitative Atlantic slave-trade operations in Africa from coastal posts like Cacheu and Bissau, but for centuries their real reach stops at the shoreline. That changes as Kaabu, the Mandinka kingdom that had governed the interior for over six centuries, is destroyed at the 1867 Battle of Kansala by an alliance of Fula forces from Fouta Djallon; the loss of Kaabu's counterweight opens the interior to a Portuguese colonial claim that hardens over the following decades, formalized as the separate colony of Guiné Portuguesa in 1879 and contested at the Berlin Conference of 1884–85.

What it represents: the blue-and-white flag of the Kingdom of Portugal, carrying the old royal coat of arms, flies over this claim until Portugal's own 1910 revolution replaces both the monarchy and its flag.

c. 1230 – 1867

Kingdom of Kaabu (western reach)

KingdomTiramakhan TraoréTraditional Mandinka animist beliefs
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Atlantic slave trade via Kaabu & the coast
Ruling house:Kaabu Empire (Nyancho Dynasty)House of Braganza

Who & why: Kaabu emerges around 1235 as a Mandinka province carved out by generals of Sundiata Keita, founder of the Mali Empire, and governs the western savanna and coastal hinterland that today straddles Guinea-Bissau, Guinea, and Senegal. It rules for a time as a tributary of Mali, then, as Mali's own power fades through the 15th and 16th centuries, emerges as a fully independent kingdom by around 1537 under its own Mansa at the capital, Kansala. Kaabu remains the dominant power of this region for centuries, ruling a mixed Mandinka, Balanta, Papel, and Bijagós population, until an alliance of Fula forces from neighboring Fouta Djallon — driven partly by Kaabu's continued raiding for the slave trade that Fula Muslim reformers opposed — storms and destroys the capital at the November 1867 Battle of Kansala, ending Kaabu as a state.

What it represents: no securely documented territorial flag survives for Kaabu; like much of pre-colonial West Africa, political authority here was expressed through royal regalia, drums, and oral praise-titles rather than a fixed banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
