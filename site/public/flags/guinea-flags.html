Flags of Guinea

Long before that theocracy, the Guinea highlands and coast lay under the Susu (Sosso) kingdom and then under the Mali Empire's suzerainty from Sundiata Keita's 1235 victory at Kirina — the same empire whose gold wealth, drawn partly from this region, made Mansa Musa a legend across the medieval Islamic world a century later. In the highland interior, the Fouta Djallon rose in the 18th century as a Fulani Muslim theocracy, one of West Africa's most durable and well-organized pre-colonial Islamic states, with a rotating almami (imam-ruler) drawn from two leading clerical lineages. France absorbed the territory piece by piece through the late 19th century, formalizing French Guinea as a colony within French West Africa. Guinea's break from that colonial system was unlike any of its neighbors': in the September 1958 referendum on Charles de Gaulle's proposed French Community, Guinea alone among France's West African territories voted "no."

That single vote brought immediate, full independence on 2 October 1958 under Sékou Touré — but at a cost. France responded punitively, withdrawing personnel, equipment, and aid on short notice, famously stripping out even light fixtures and paperwork before departure, and Guinea entered independence more isolated than any of its 1960-wave neighbors. The red-yellow-green tricolor adopted that day has flown, unchanged, ever since.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Guinea, from the medieval Susu kingdom and Mali Empire suzerainty (c. 1050) through the 18th-century Fouta Djallon theocracy and French colonization to the modern republic, four political entities. No flag is attested for Fouta Djallon's own religious-political authority, nor for the Susu kingdom or Mali's provincial rule that preceded it; this page notes both honestly rather than inventing one. Republic of Guinea (independent)

Republic, independentAhmed Sékou TouréNo official state religion (secular; predominantly Muslim)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: political exiles under Touré (1958–84)Immigration: Liberian & Sierra Leonean refugees (1990s–2000s)

Who & why: Guinea alone among France's West African territories votes "no" in the September 1958 referendum on de Gaulle's proposed French Community, choosing full immediate independence over continued association with France. Independence comes on 2 October 1958 under Ahmed Sékou Touré, but France responds by abruptly withdrawing aid, technical staff, and equipment, leaving the new state to build institutions largely on its own. Touré governs as an increasingly authoritarian one-party ruler until his death in 1984; Guinea has since passed through further periods of military rule alongside moves toward multiparty government.

What it represents: a red-yellow-green vertical tricolor adopted at independence, Pan-African colors read locally as red for the blood and sacrifice of the independence struggle, yellow for the sun and the country's mineral wealth, and green for its vegetation and the unity of its people.

French Guinea (colony)

ColonyJean-Marie BayolNo official state religion (predominantly Muslim population)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: French colonial forces (to mid-1890s)

Who & why: France separates Guinea from the wider "Rivières du Sud" administration as its own colony in 1891 (formalized 1893), completing conquest of the Fouta Djallon highlands by the mid-1890s against resistance including that of Samory Touré further east. French Guinea is administered as part of French West Africa (AOF) from 1904, its colonial economy built around bauxite, iron ore, and agricultural exports.

What it represents: the French Tricolore flew as the sovereign flag across French West Africa; no distinct territorial flag existed for Guinea itself. Imamate of Fouta Djallon

Theocratic state, independentKaramoko AlfaSunni Islam (theocratic state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: captives sold into the Atlantic slave trade
Ruling house:Alfaya lineageSoriya lineage

Who & why: Fulani Muslim clerics, led by Ibrahima Sori and Karamoko Alfa, establish a jihadist theocratic state in the Fouta Djallon highlands from around 1727, one of the earliest and longest-lived Islamic states in West Africa, governed by a rotating almami drawn from two rival clerical lineages and organized into semi-autonomous provinces. The imamate controls trans-Saharan and coastal trade routes and remains a regional power into the late 19th century before French forces complete its conquest by the mid-1890s.

What it represents: no flag is attested for Fouta Djallon's own political-religious authority, which relied on Islamic scholarly and clan legitimacy rather than territorial banners. Shown here as an honest, clearly labeled reconstruction referencing Islamic crescent-and-star iconography and the highland's green landscape, not a documented design.

Kingdom of Sosso & Mali Empire Suzerainty

Fragmented / regional suzeraintySundiata KeitaTraditional Mande religion, with gradual Islamization from the 13th c.
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Mali Empire (c. 1235, Battle of Kirina)

Who & why: after the decline of the Ghana Empire's trans-Saharan trading power, the Susu (Sosso) people build a regional kingdom across the area of present-day upper Guinea and southern Mali, reaching its height under Soumaoro Kanté in the early 13th century. Sundiata Keita's Mande coalition defeats him at the Battle of Kirina, c. 1235 — the founding victory of the Mali Empire, which holds the Guinea highlands and coast under its suzerainty (directly, then loosely, as the empire slowly fragments after the 15th century) until Fulani clerics establish the independent Imamate of Fouta Djallon in 1727.

What it represents: no banner is attested for the Susu kingdom or for Mali's own provincial rule over this stretch of the Guinea highlands and coast — the Mali Empire is remembered through oral epic, Arabic chronicles, and archaeology, not a surviving vexillological record. Shown here honestly blank rather than inventing one.

World context: Sundiata's 1235 victory falls within a generation of Genghis Khan's death (1227) and the Mongol Empire's peak expansion, and not long before the Angkor temple complex reaches its final medieval form in Cambodia; a century later, Mali's Mansa Musa passes through Cairo on his legendary 1324–25 pilgrimage to Mecca, astonishing observers across the Islamic world with gold wealth this same Guinea highland region helped supply.
