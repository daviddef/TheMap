Flags of the Carpathian Basin

Before the Magyars ever crossed the Carpathians, the same basin had already hosted Attila's Hunnic Empire and, for two and a half centuries, the Avar Khaganate — steppe powers that rose and fell here first. From the Magyar tribal federation crossing the Carpathians in 895, through a Christian kingdom that lasted 500 years under one crown, a fifteen-year civil war between rival claimants, a century and a half split three ways by the Ottomans, and two catastrophic 20th-century ruptures — fourteen flags, and a red-white-green tricolor that keeps reasserting itself the moment each regime falls.

Hungary's own flag colors are older than the modern tricolor format: red for strength, white for faithfulness, green for hope, already fixed as the "land's colors" by the 15th century, centuries before 1848 turned them into three horizontal bands.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Hungary, c. 400–present, fourteen political entities, opening with the Hunnic and Avar khaganates that ruled the Carpathian Basin before the Magyars' own arrival in 895. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Two Hungaries, 1541–1699

Hungary's own real border, split into the two pieces that were, for a century and a half, governed by entirely different powers: the northwestern strip that stayed under Habsburg "Royal Hungary," and the rest — including Buda itself — under direct Ottoman rule. Press play, or drag the slider.

Budapest spent longer under the Ottomans than under any Hungarian government of its own era. Buda fell in 1541 and wasn't retaken until 1686 — a hundred and forty-five years, longer than the entire span of Austria-Hungary. The border line below only solidifies where the two pieces were actually ruled apart — it dissolves the moment one government held both, which happens exactly once, in 1699, and never un-happens again.

Who & why: the Communist party's own reformers negotiate a peaceful transition through 1989, symbolically completed when the republic is re-proclaimed on 23 October 1989 — the anniversary of the crushed 1956 uprising. Hungary joins NATO in 1999 and the EU in 2004.

What it represents: the coat-of-arms-bearing star of the communist era is simply removed, leaving the plain red-white-green tricolor — the same design first fixed in the 1848 revolution, restored now for the third time in a century.

1949 – 1989

Hungarian People's Republic

Communist stateMátyás RákosiState atheism
Migration & conquest:Emigration: 1956 revolution refugees

Who & why: Mátyás Rákosi's Communist Party consolidates one-party rule by 1949 through show trials and rigged elections. The 1956 Revolution — briefly successful, crushed within days by Soviet tanks after Imre Nagy announces withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact — is the era's defining trauma; János Kádár's subsequent decades of "goulash communism" trade political loyalty for modest consumer comfort.

What it represents: the historic tricolor is kept, but topped with a Soviet-style coat of arms (wheat sheaf, red star, hammer and sickle) replacing the old Holy Crown emblem — a 1957 redesign following the 1956 uprising, when revolutionaries had cut the coat of arms out of the flag entirely, leaving a hole that became the revolution's own visual symbol.

1946 – 1949

Second Republic of Hungary

RepublicZoltán TildySecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: ethnic Germans expelled (1946–48)Immigration: ethnic Hungarians from Czechoslovakia (population exchange)

Who & why: declared 1 February 1946 after the war's devastation and Soviet occupation, briefly genuinely multi-party — the Smallholders' Party wins the free 1945 election outright — before Communist "salami tactics" dismantle the coalition piece by piece, ending in the 1949 single-list election that installs the People's Republic.

1944 – 1945

Government of National Unity (Arrow Cross)

Fascist / puppet stateFerenc SzálasiNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Emigration: deportation of Hungarian Jews to Nazi camps (1944)

Who & why: as Horthy attempts to negotiate an armistice with the Soviets in October 1944, German forces depose him and install Ferenc Szálasi's fascist Arrow Cross Party in a coup. What it represents: the Arrow Cross Party's own emblem, a geometric cross-like symbol distinct from both the Hungarian tricolor and Nazi Germany's own symbols, reflecting the party's specifically Hungarian brand of fascism even while operating as Berlin's client in its final months.

1920 – 1944

Kingdom of Hungary (Horthy Regency)

Kingdom (no monarch)Miklós HorthyRoman Catholic (traditional)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Trianon refugees from ceded territories

Who & why: the June 1920 Treaty of Trianon strips Hungary of roughly two-thirds of its historic territory and three-fifths of its population, a national trauma that dominates the whole interwar period. Admiral Miklós Horthy governs as Regent of a kingdom with no king — two restoration attempts by the last Habsburg claimant, Charles IV, are blocked by force in 1921.

What it represents: the tricolor restored with the traditional coat of arms — the Holy Crown of St. Stephen atop a shield combining the Árpád stripes and the double cross — asserting continuity with the pre-1918 kingdom even as most of that kingdom's actual territory is now Romanian, Czechoslovak, or Yugoslav.

Communist stateBéla KunState atheism

Who & why: Béla Kun's Communists seize power in March 1919 amid the chaos following Hungary's WWI collapse, briefly allying with the fragile Democratic Republic before pushing it aside. What it represents: a plain red field, the standard international Communist banner of the moment, with no Hungarian national symbol at all — explicitly rejecting the tricolor as a bourgeois-national relic rather than reinterpreting it, unlike the later 1949 People's Republic.

1918 – 1919

Hungarian Democratic Republic

RepublicMihály KárolyiSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: Count Mihály Károlyi proclaims the republic on 16 November 1918 as Austria-Hungary disintegrates, hoping liberal, pacifist credentials might secure better peace terms from the Allies. The strategy fails; territorial losses mount regardless, and the government's own weakness opens the door to Béla Kun's Communists within months.

What it represents: the tricolor stripped of any royal or imperial emblem for the first time in Hungary's history — a deliberate, if short-lived, assertion that the nation's colors belong to the republic, not the crown.

1867 – 1918

Austria-Hungary (Kingdom of Hungary)

Kingdom (Transleithania)Franz Joseph IRoman Catholic (traditional)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the United States (1880–1914)
Ruling house:House of Habsburg

Who & why: the 1867 Ausgleich gives Hungary its own government, parliament, and prime minister in Budapest, equal in status to Austria's own Vienna-based half, sharing only the monarch, foreign policy, and military with Franz Joseph's Cisleithania. Budapest itself unifies from Buda, Pest, and Óbuda in 1873, growing into a genuine European capital in its own right.

What it represents: Hungary's own red-white-green, distinct from Austria's black-and-gold imperial colors — a visible, everyday reminder that the Dual Monarchy was two kingdoms under one crown, not one empire with two names.

Kingdom (Habsburg crown)Leopold ICatholic (Habsburg)

Who & why: the 1699 Treaty of Karlowitz confirms Habsburg control over the whole reunited kingdom; Maria Theresa and Joseph II's later 18th-century reforms centralize power in Vienna over increasingly vocal Hungarian objections. 1541 – 1699

Ottoman Hungary (Budin Eyalet)

Occupied territorySuleiman the MagnificentSunni Islam (Ottoman)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Ottoman Empire (1541–1699)
Ruling house:House of Habsburg

Who & why: Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent takes Buda in 1541, organizing central Hungary as the Eyalet of Budin administered from the captured royal palace. The rest of the historic kingdom splits three ways: this Ottoman core, Habsburg "Royal Hungary" in the north and west (see the map above), and the semi-autonomous Principality of Transylvania to the east, outside modern Hungary's own borders.

What it represents: the standard Ottoman red field with crescent and star, flown over what had been the kingdom's own capital — a century and a half during which no Hungarian state of any kind exists in Buda itself.

1526 – 1541

Kingdom of Hungary (Disputed Succession)

Contested kingdomFerdinand I of HabsburgCatholic
Ruling house:House of Habsburg

Who & why: King Louis II drowns fleeing the catastrophic defeat at Mohács in August 1526, dying without an heir. The nobility splits: one faction elects the Transylvanian magnate John Zápolya, who leans on Ottoman backing; another elects Ferdinand of Habsburg, brother-in-law of the dead king, who controls the west and north. Both crown themselves King of Hungary in the same year, and neither fully controls the country — a civil war between the two claimants runs alongside continued Ottoman raiding until Suleiman simply takes Buda outright in 1541, ending the argument by force.

What it represents: no new flag emerges in these fifteen years of dual kingship — both Zápolya and Ferdinand fly the same inherited Árpád-stripe royal banner, each claiming to be its rightful bearer, shown here with a broken border to mark a kingdom whole in symbol only.

1000 – 1526

Kingdom of Hungary (Medieval)

KingdomStephen I (St. Stephen)Roman Catholic
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Cuman refugees settled by Béla IV (13th c.)
Ruling house:House of ÁrpádHouse of AnjouHouse of Hunyadi

Who & why: Stephen I, crowned on Christmas Day 1000 (or 1 January 1001) with a crown sent by Pope Sylvester II, completes the conversion to Christianity his father Géza began and is later canonized as Hungary's patron saint. The kingdom becomes a major medieval power under rulers like Matthias Corvinus (r. 1458–90), ending catastrophically at the 1526 Battle of Mohács, where King Louis II dies and the Ottoman advance becomes unstoppable.

What it represents: the Árpád stripes — four red and four white horizontal bands — attested in seals from the 13th century onward, among the oldest heraldic devices associated with the Hungarian crown, still part of the national coat of arms today.

c. 895 – 1000

Principality of Hungary

Tribal principalityÁrpádTengrism (Magyar paganism)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Magyar tribal migration into the Carpathian Basin
Ruling house:House of Árpád

Who & why: Árpád leads the Magyar tribal federation across the Carpathians in the "Honfoglalás" (Land-Taking) of c. 895, settling the Carpathian Basin after decades as feared raiders across Europe — the 955 Battle of Lechfeld, a crushing defeat by Otto I, finally ends the raiding era and pushes the Magyars toward settled statehood and, within two generations, Christianity.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic, pre-Christian era; the Magyar tribes used totemic standards (a turul, a mythical falcon, features heavily in later national symbolism) rather than a fixed territorial banner. c. 400 – 895

Hunnic & Avar Khaganates (Carpathian Basin)

Steppe khaganate / empireAttila the HunSteppe shamanism / Tengrism (pre-Christian)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Hunnic migration from Central Asia (early 5th c.)Immigration: Avar migration from Central Asia (567 CE)Conquered by: Frankish campaigns under Charlemagne (791–803 CE)

Who & why: nearly five centuries before the Magyars' own arrival, the Carpathian Basin already hosts two major steppe empires in succession. Attila's Huns, ruling from a court somewhere on the Great Hungarian Plain, terrorize both halves of the fracturing Roman Empire until his death in 453 causes the empire to disintegrate almost immediately. From 567 CE, the Avars — another Central Asian steppe people — build a far longer-lived khaganate on the same ground, one of early medieval Europe's most powerful polities, until Charlemagne's repeated campaigns of 791–803 CE break it apart. The resulting power vacuum, contested by Franks, Moravians, and Bulgars, is what the Magyar tribes ride into in 895.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or banner survives for either the Hunnic Empire or the Avar Khaganate; both were mobile steppe polities organized around a khagan's court and retinue rather than fixed territorial symbols. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Attila's Huns push west as the Western Roman Empire is already crumbling, turning back from an invasion of Italy in 452 after meeting Pope Leo I and dying the following year — a generation before Rome's last emperor is deposed in 476. In 626, the Avars besiege Constantinople jointly with the Sassanid Persian Empire and Slavic allies, a coordinated assault the Byzantines survive and credit, in later tradition, to the Virgin Mary's protection; the Avar Khaganate's own final collapse under Charlemagne comes just a few years before his 800 coronation as Holy Roman Emperor in Rome.
