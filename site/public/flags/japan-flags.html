Flags of Japan

Japan's political history runs from the rice-farming Yayoi chiefdoms known mainly through Chinese chronicles, through the early Yamato court's consolidation of the home islands, more than six centuries in which real power sat not with the emperor but with a series of shogunates — military governments that kept the imperial line as a ceremonial and religious figurehead while they actually ruled — to the 1868 Meiji Restoration's rapid modernization, the WWII-era empire, and the pacifist constitution written under postwar American occupation. The Hinomaru sun-disc has ancient roots in Japanese art and religious symbolism, and was already in wide informal and naval use for centuries, but it was only formally written into law as Japan's national flag in 1999 — a detail worth noting on its own.

This page states Japan's World War II-era conduct — its invasion and occupation of large parts of Asia and the Pacific, and its eventual defeat — factually and without dwelling on it beyond what a flag-history page needs.

Scope of this page: the Japanese home islands, from the Yayoi period (c. 300 BCE) to the present, nine political periods. No internal governing split exists within Japan's own modern territory, so this page carries no internal-partition map. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

1947 – present

Japan (postwar constitutional era)

Constitutional monarchyShigeru YoshidaNo state religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Nikkei Brazilian & Peruvian return migration (1990s)
Ruling house:Imperial House of Japan

Who & why: Japan's new postwar constitution, drafted under Allied occupation authority and taking effect in May 1947, renounces war as a sovereign right (Article 9) and reduces the emperor to a symbolic head of state with no political power, ending the wartime imperial system. Full sovereignty returns with the 1952 San Francisco Peace Treaty, and Japan rebuilds into one of the world's largest economies over the following decades. The Hinomaru continues in everyday use throughout, but only receives formal legal status as the national flag — alongside "Kimigayo" as the national anthem — under the 1999 Act on National Flag and Anthem, following decades in which it carried no explicit statutory basis at all.

What it represents: a red sun disc on white, referencing Japan's own name (Nihon, "origin of the sun") and centuries of use on ships, temple banners, and military standards long before any law formalized it.

1945 – 1947

Allied occupation of Japan

Occupied territoryDouglas MacArthurNo state religion (State Shinto disestablished)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Allied powers (US-led)Immigration: mass repatriation from the former empire
Ruling house:Imperial House of Japan

Who & why: Japan surrenders on 15 August 1945 after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Soviet Union's entry into the Pacific War, ending World War II. American-led Allied occupation authorities under General Douglas MacArthur govern the country, restrict civilian use of the Hinomaru in the occupation's early years, demilitarize and democratize Japanese institutions, and oversee the drafting of the new constitution that takes effect in 1947.

What it represents: the same Hinomaru, its use curtailed but not abolished, pending the postwar constitutional settlement.

1868 – 1945

Empire of Japan (Meiji through Shōwa)

Constitutional empireEmperor MeijiState Shinto
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to Hawaii, the Americas (1885–1920s)Immigration: Korean laborers, later forced conscription (1910–45)
Ruling house:Imperial House of Japan

Who & why: the 1868 Meiji Restoration abolishes the Tokugawa shogunate, formally returns political authority to Emperor Meiji, and launches rapid industrial and military modernization along Western lines. Japan defeats Qing China (1894–95) and Russia (1904–05), annexes Korea (1910) and Taiwan (1895), and, under the Shōwa emperor from 1926, pursues militarist expansion across China from 1931 and into the wider Pacific War from 1941 as part of World War II, a period of invasion, occupation, and mass casualties across the region stated here factually, ending in Japan's 1945 defeat.

What it represents: the plain Hinomaru serves as national flag throughout, formally specified for merchant shipping as early as 1870; the naval and military Rising Sun ensign, with sixteen rays radiating from an off-center sun, flies alongside it for the Imperial Japanese Navy and Army.

1603 – 1868

Tokugawa Shogunate (Edo period)

Shogunate (military government)Tokugawa IeyasuBuddhism (temple registration system)
Ruling house:Imperial House of JapanTokugawa clan

Who & why: Tokugawa Ieyasu wins the decisive Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and is named shogun in 1603, founding a military government that rules Japan for two and a half centuries from Edo (modern Tokyo) while the emperor remains a largely powerless court figure in Kyoto. The shogunate enforces a strict class system, near-total national seclusion (sakoku) from the 1630s, and over two centuries of internal peace, before American Commodore Perry's 1853 arrival forces Japan open and triggers the crisis that ends shogunate rule in 1868.

What it represents: no single national flag exists under the Tokugawa; ships and processions instead flew clan mon (heraldic crests), most famously the Tokugawa family's own triple hollyhock crest, shown here only as a color reference rather than an invented national banner.

1336 – 1573

Ashikaga Shogunate (Muromachi period)

Shogunate, fragmentedAshikaga TakaujiZen Buddhism (shogunal patronage)
Ruling house:Imperial House of JapanAshikaga clan

Who & why: Ashikaga Takauji establishes a second shogunate in 1336 after brief imperial-restoration fighting (including a rival Southern Court that persists until 1392), governing from Kyoto's Muromachi district. Central authority weakens steadily, collapsing outright into the Sengoku ("Warring States") period of near-constant regional warfare among competing daimyo lords from roughly 1467 until Oda Nobunaga's rise begins reunifying the country in the 1560s–70s, a process the succeeding Tokugawa complete.

What it represents: no unified national flag exists through this fragmented era; regional warlords flew their own clan banners and mon rather than any single Japanese symbol.

1185 – 1333

Kamakura Shogunate

Shogunate (military government)Minamoto no YoritomoBuddhism and Shinto (no single establishment)
Ruling house:Imperial House of JapanMinamoto clan

Who & why: Minamoto no Yoritomo wins the Genpei War and is named the first shogun in 1192 (with his government effectively established from 1185), founding the pattern that will govern Japan on and off for the next six-plus centuries: an emperor kept as ceremonial and religious head of state in Kyoto while a military government actually rules from elsewhere, here Kamakura. The shogunate famously repels two attempted Mongol invasions (1274 and 1281), aided by typhoons later mythologized as the "kamikaze" (divine wind), before internal strain and the costs of those invasions weaken it into collapse in 1333.

What it represents: no national flag exists yet; samurai clans fly their own war banners (sashimono and uma-jirushi) rather than any unified Japanese symbol.

794 – 1185

Heian period (imperial court rule)

Imperial court ruleEmperor KanmuBuddhism and Shinto (court-patronized)
Ruling house:Imperial House of JapanMinamoto clan

Who & why: Emperor Kanmu moves the capital to Heian-kyō (modern Kyoto) in 794, opening nearly four centuries in which the imperial court, and the powerful Fujiwara regents who dominate it for much of the period, still governs directly — the last era before military shogunates take over that role. Heian Japan produces a celebrated flowering of court literature and aesthetics, including "The Tale of Genji," often cited as the world's first novel, before rising samurai clan power culminates in the Genpei War and the Kamakura shogunate's founding in 1185.

What it represents: no flag exists in this era; imperial authority is expressed through court ritual, regalia, and the chrysanthemum symbolism later associated with the throne, not through any territorial banner.

Yamato period (early imperial Japan)

Fragmented, consolidatingNo single ruler documentedShinto, then Buddhism (6th century)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Korean & Chinese toraijin settlers (4th–7th c.)
Ruling house:Imperial House of Japan

Who & why: the Yamato court, centered in the Kinai region around modern Nara and Osaka, gradually consolidates authority over other clan groupings across the Japanese home islands from around the 3rd century CE onward, traditionally (and legendarily, not historically) tracing the imperial line back to 660 BCE. This span includes the Kofun period's monumental tomb-building, the 6th-century introduction of Buddhism from Korea and China, and the Nara period's (710–94) construction of a permanent capital and centralized law codes modeled on Tang China, before the court relocates to Heian-kyō in 794.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic era; shown here honestly blank rather than invented, with a neutral placeholder field only.

c. 300 BCE – 250 CE

Yayoi period

Fragmented, pre-YamatoHimiko (per Chinese chronicles)Unrecorded (pre-Shinto, archaeological only)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: rice-farming & metalworking migrants from Korea/mainland Asia

Who & why: wet-rice paddy agriculture and bronze and iron toolmaking arrive in the Japanese islands from the Korean peninsula and mainland Asia from around the 3rd century BCE, ending roughly ten thousand years of the preceding Jomon hunter-gatherer culture and giving rise to dozens of small, competing farming chiefdoms across western and central Japan. Japan itself had no writing of its own in this era; almost everything known about named rulers comes from Chinese dynastic chronicles, most notably the Wei Zhi's account of Himiko, ruler of a polity called Yamataikoku, who sends an embassy to the Chinese Wei court in 238 CE. The Yayoi period gradually gives way to the Kofun period's monumental tomb-building and the Yamato court's own consolidation (see the entry above).

What it represents: no flag survives from Yayoi Japan, and no written Japanese record of this era exists at all; shown here honestly blank rather than invented, with a neutral placeholder field only.

World context: this span runs alongside the Qin and Han dynasties' unification and consolidation of China, the Silk Road's opening overland trade between China and the Mediterranean world, and, at its far end, the Roman Empire's own height under the Pax Romana — a reminder that Himiko's 238 CE embassy to the Wei court sits closer in time to the fall of the Han dynasty than to the Yamato court that would eventually succeed her polity.
