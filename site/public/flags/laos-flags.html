Flags of Laos

From the megalithic Plain of Jars culture and the early Mekong-valley principality of Muang Sua, through the Lan Xang kingdom — the "Land of a Million Elephants" — its later split into three rival principalities, French colonization as part of Indochina, a monarchy restored after Japanese occupation, and a communist government in power since 1975 — six flags across a landlocked country's long path between larger neighbors.

Laos is unusual in swapping its national symbol entirely at the point of revolution: the royal three-headed elephant flag was fully retired in 1975 in favor of a plain red-blue-red design with a white disc, a deliberate break from every royal and colonial flag that came before it.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Laos, c. 500 BCE–present, six political entries. The post-Lan Xang split into the Luang Prabang, Vientiane, and Champasak kingdoms, and their later vassalage to Siam, is shown as a single bridging entry rather than three invented sub-periods, since no single flag distinguishes them individually in the historical record. Three kingdoms did once fragment this territory — but not on flags anyone can honestly draw. After Lan Xang split around 1707–13, Luang Prabang, Vientiane, and Champasak each had their own crown for nearly two centuries. But only Luang Prabang's own elephant banner is well documented; Vientiane and Champasak's individual flags are not, so drawing three separately-colored regions here would mean inventing two of them. Lao People's Democratic Republic

Republic, socialistKaysone PhomvihaneSecular (no official state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Hmong & lowland Lao refugees (1975–)

Who & why: the communist Pathet Lao movement, which had fought alongside North Vietnam through the wider Indochina wars, takes full power as the Kingdom of Laos collapses in December 1975; King Savang Vatthana abdicates, ending the monarchy, and a one-party communist state remains in place to the present, among the last of its kind in the world.

What it represents: a white disc, representing the moon over the Mekong and the unity of the people, centered on a blue band flanked by red — a wholesale break from every royal and colonial flag that preceded it.

Kingdom of Laos (independent)

Kingdom, constitutionalSisavang VongTheravada Buddhism (constitutional state religion)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: North Vietnamese forces (eastern Laos)Emigration: wartime refugee flight to Thailand (1960s–70s)
Ruling house:House of Lan Xang / Luang Prabang

Who & why: Laos gains full independence from France in October 1953 under King Sisavang Vong, but spends nearly its entire independent existence as a monarchy drawn into the wider regional conflict, with a three-way civil war between royalist, neutralist, and communist Pathet Lao factions, heavily overlapping with, and covertly bombed during, the Vietnam War. That civil war ends with the communist takeover in 1975.

What it represents: a white three-headed elephant, Erawan, standing on a five-tier pedestal beneath a royal parasol on a red field — the Lan Xang kingdom's own elephant imagery, adapted as the modern royal kingdom's flag.

1893 – 1953

French Protectorate of Laos

Colonial (France)Oun KhamNo official state religion (Buddhism practiced)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: FranceImmigration: Vietnamese colonial administrators & laborers
Ruling house:House of Lan Xang / Luang Prabang

Who & why: France establishes a protectorate over Luang Prabang in 1893, following gunboat pressure on Siam, and gradually extends control over the other Lao principalities, folding them all into French Indochina by 1899, with a single reunified Laos administered under the Luang Prabang monarchy. Japan occupies Indochina through the war years and briefly installs an independent Lao government in 1945 before France reasserts colonial control after Japan's surrender, holding on until 1953.

What it represents: the plain French tricolor, the colonial power's own national flag, with the Luang Prabang monarchy retained as a French-supervised figurehead over the reunified protectorate.

c. 1707 – 1893

Fragmented Lao kingdoms (Luang Prabang, Vientiane, Champasak)

Fragmented kingdomsNo single ruler documentedTheravada Buddhism (traditional, all three kingdoms)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: forced Siamese deportation to Isan (1827–28)
Ruling house:House of Lan Xang / Luang Prabang

Who & why: succession disputes split Lan Xang into three rival kingdoms — Luang Prabang, Vientiane, and Champasak — around 1707–1713, none of which ever reunites the old kingdom's territory. All three progressively fall under Siamese suzerainty over the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, with Vientiane's own 1827–28 uprising against Siam crushed and the city itself destroyed, before France's 1893 protectorate over Luang Prabang ends outright Siamese control.

What it represents: Luang Prabang, the most durable of the three and the eventual seat of the later unified monarchy, is understood to have used its own elephant banner by this era; the other two kingdoms' individual flags are not well documented, so this entry represents the fragmented period through the best-attested of the three.

1353 – 1707

Lan Xang Kingdom

Kingdom, independentFa NgumTheravada Buddhism (established as state religion)
Ruling house:House of Lan Xang / Luang Prabang

Who & why: Fa Ngum founds Lan Xang ("Land of a Million Elephants") in 1353, unifying Lao-speaking principalities along the middle Mekong with Khmer military backing and establishing Theravada Buddhism as the state religion. The kingdom reaches its greatest extent and prosperity under King Sourigna Vongsa in the mid-1600s, but succession disputes following his death fracture it into three rival kingdoms by the early 1700s.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from Lan Xang itself; the three-headed elephant later associated with the region only appears clearly in the historical record from the successor kingdoms onward, so this entry is shown honestly blank rather than projecting that later symbol backward.

c. 500 BCE – 1353

Plain of Jars culture & Muang Sua (pre-Lan Xang)

Fragmented / no unified polityNo single ruler documentedNo unified state religion (animist & early Buddhist practice)
Migration & conquest:Overlordship: intermittent Nanzhao & Khmer suzerainty over Muang Sua

Who & why: long before Lan Xang, the plateau that is now Laos held the Plain of Jars, thousands of massive carved stone jars scattered across Xiangkhouang province, which archaeological and radiocarbon dating places at roughly 500 BCE to 500 CE — one of Southeast Asia's most striking prehistoric sites, evidently used for mortuary purposes, though the culture that carved and used them left no written record of its own. By the early medieval period, the Mekong valley around what is now Luang Prabang was home to Muang Sua (also called Xieng Dong Xieng Thong), a Mon-Khmer, and later Tai-Lao, principality documented in regional chronicles and in Chinese and Khmer records from around the 7th century CE onward, passing under intermittent Nanzhao and Khmer overlordship before Fa Ngum, raised at the Khmer court of Angkor, conquered and absorbed it in 1353 to found Lan Xang.

What it represents: no flag survives from either the Plain of Jars culture, which predates the very concept, or from Muang Sua's own local rule; no fixed emblem is documented for either. Shown here honestly blank.

World context: this long span runs alongside the rise and fall of the Roman Empire and the Han and Tang dynasties in China, and, closer to home, the flourishing of the Khmer Empire and the construction of Angkor Wat in the early 12th century — a temple-city whose religious and political influence reached directly into the Mon-Khmer river principalities of the middle Mekong, Muang Sua among them.
