Flags of the Levant

Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, and Israel — four countries whose modern borders were drawn only a century ago, on land that had already changed hands a dozen times before that. Below, one map traces who governed each of the four across thirteen centuries, one slider-turn at a time.

This region's most recent history includes genuinely contested, still-unfolding events. Where the record below reaches into the 20th century, it sticks to dates, names, and documents — the same standard already used elsewhere in this atlas for Crimea and Transnistria — rather than characterizing anything still actively disputed today.

Four lineages, in progress

antiquity–present Syria Damascus as an Umayyad capital, Zengid and Ayyubid Damascus, four Ottoman centuries, a flag that left and came home. antiquity–present Lebanon The County of Tripoli, an autonomous Ottoman Mutasarrifate, the 1943 flag that has never changed since. antiquity–present Jordan The Crusader Lordship of Oultrejordain, four Ottoman centuries, the 1928 flag still flying today. antiquity–present Israel The 1917 Balfour Declaration, the 1947 UN Partition Plan, the 1948 declaration of statehood.

Thirteen centuries, one map

A real, traced map — actual Mediterranean and Red Sea coastlines, not schematic shapes — with Turkey, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, and Egypt held faintly in the background for orientation. Drag the slider or press play; each snapshot simplifies a messier reality into the dominant power of the moment.

Borders here are data, not decoration. A line between two of the four countries only draws solid when they were actually governed separately at that moment. When they weren't — Lebanon, Jordan, and the land that is now Israel, all under the Kingdom of Jerusalem in 1115, say — the line dissolves and their names merge into whoever actually ruled them. Colors blend between eras instead of jump-cutting.
700

Real traced borders (simplified). Turkey, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, and Egypt are held faintly for context; the open water beyond the four core countries is the Mediterranean in the west and the Gulf of Aqaba / Red Sea in the south.

Try: drag from 700 to 1291 and watch Syria stay a color apart from the other three the whole way through 1115 — Damascus and Aleppo were never held by the Crusader states, even while Jerusalem, Tripoli, and Oultrejordain (Jordan) all were. Then jump to 1920 and watch the map split cleanly along the Sykes–Picot line: Syria and Lebanon turn French-mandate blue together, Jordan and Israel turn British-mandate purple together, with a solid line only where those two zones actually met. By 1946, three of the four already have their own flag; only the fourth is still a Mandate territory — watch what happens to it two steps later.

The same story, as a timeline

Maps are bad at showing when; timelines are bad at showing where. This uses real transition years instead of the eight arbitrary snapshots above — one horizontal band per country, colored the same way as the map, so you can see exactly how long each era lasted and compare all four side by side (watch Jordan and Israel fall to the Crusaders decades apart, and to the Mamluks decades apart too).

The same story, eight frames — only the change lights up

Every country keeps its true color, but anything unchanged from the previous frame fades to low opacity; only the country or countries that actually changed hands stay bold with a solid outline. A caption under each frame spells out exactly what flipped. No slider to operate — scan left to right, top to bottom.

About this atlas

This started as a family history project: tracing the flags that flew over the lands our ancestors came from, close enough to paint accurately — correct colors, correct proportions, correct dates. The Levant is the atlas's first stop in the Middle East, and by far its most politically sensitive: every date and event above is drawn from the well-documented historical record, and the story deliberately stops short of characterizing anything still actively contested today, including the exact status of Palestinian territory since 1948 and the situation inside Syria since the fall of the Assad government in December 2024.

Every future country page will carry its own timeline of flags with paint-ready color specs and a per-era location map. Colors are close historical approximations; pre-19th-century swatches especially are informed reconstructions, not official specifications, and are labeled as such throughout. The regional map above simplifies thirteen centuries of messy, overlapping, disputed control into eight snapshots — useful for orientation, not a substitute for the detail each country's own page will eventually carry.
