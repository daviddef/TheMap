Flags of Libya

Libya's modern flag history is defined by two remarkable bookends: in 1951 it became the first country ever brought to independence directly by the United Nations, and in 2011 it became the only country in this atlas whose national revolution was fought, in part, to bring back a flag — the tricolor of the 1951 kingdom, hauled out of exile as the banner of the uprising against Muammar Gaddafi. Between Ottoman rule, Italian colonization, a brief Allied military administration, a monarchy, and Gaddafi's 42-year Jamahiriya, this page traces eight eras.

Gaddafi's 1969 coup eventually produced the only national flag in modern history to be a single plain color with no design of any kind — a solid green field flown from 1977 to 2011, representing his political philosophy rather than any traditional heraldic device. The page's own roots run further back still, to the two genuinely ancient civilizations that first put this coastline on the map: Greek Cyrenaica in the east, founded from the island of Thera in 631 BCE, and the Phoenician-Carthaginian trading cities of Tripolitania in the west, ancestors of Tripoli's own name.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Libya, c. 630 BCE–present, eight political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

This page doesn't split internally, even though Libya's own history would support it. Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and Fezzan were governed separately for long stretches — including the 1943–51 Allied administration, when Britain ran the first two regions and France ran Fezzan under entirely different flags. But no single Libyan flag existed for any of those separately-run pieces; the division was administrative, not symbolic, so a map split would invent borders no flag ever marked. The story below flags that exception honestly instead.

Libya (post-revolution)

Republic, independentMustafa Abdul JalilSunni Islam (constitutional state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: transit hub for African migrants (2011–)Emigration: conflict-driven displacement

Who & why: protests beginning in February 2011, part of the wider Arab Spring, escalate into a full civil war; NATO intervenes under a UN mandate, and Gaddafi is captured and killed near Sirte in October 2011, ending his 42-year rule. Libya has since struggled with fragmented governance and recurring conflict between rival authorities, a genuine and difficult aftermath stated here factually.

What it represents: revolutionaries deliberately readopt the 1951 kingdom's red-black-green tricolor with its white crescent and star as their banner from the earliest days of the uprising, and it becomes Libya's official flag again upon the fall of Gaddafi's government.

1977 – 2011

Great Socialist People's Libyan Arab Jamahiriya

Personalist ruleMuammar GaddafiSunni Islam (constitutional state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Sub-Saharan African labor migrants

Who & why: Muammar Gaddafi seizes power in a September 1969 coup against King Idris, and by 1977 declares Libya a "Jamahiriya" (state of the masses) under his idiosyncratic "Third International Theory," ruling for 42 years until the 2011 revolution ends his government.

What it represents: a plain green field, adopted in 1977 as a deliberate break from all traditional flag design and a reference to Gaddafi's political manifesto, the Green Book — the only national flag in modern history to carry no emblem, pattern, or additional color whatsoever.

Personalist ruleMuammar GaddafiSunni Islam (state religion)

Who & why: Gaddafi's Revolutionary Command Council replaces the monarchy's flag with a red-white-black tricolor (mirroring Egypt's own republican flag) after the 1969 coup, then briefly joins the 1972–77 Federation of Arab Republics, a loose and short-lived pan-Arab union with Egypt and Syria that shared a common hawk-emblem flag before dissolving.

1951 – 1969

Kingdom of Libya

Kingdom, independentIdris IIslam (constitutional state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: early oil-industry workers (1959–)
Ruling house:House of Senussi

Who & why: the United Nations General Assembly resolves in 1949 to grant Libya independence, and on 24 December 1951 it becomes fully sovereign under King Idris — the first country ever brought to independence by the UN, unifying Tripolitania, Cyrenaica, and Fezzan into a single federal (later unitary) kingdom. Oil discoveries in the late 1950s transform its economy before Gaddafi's 1969 coup ends the monarchy.

What it represents: a new tricolor designed for independence, based on the flag of the wartime Cyrenaican resistance, with a white crescent and star recalling the Sanussi order's own banner — the flag Libya's 2011 revolutionaries would later revive.

Italian Libya

Colonial ruleVictor Emmanuel IIINo official state religion imposed (Sunni Islam predominant)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: ItalyImmigration: Italian colonial settlers
Ruling house:House of Senussi

Who & why: Italy invades Ottoman Tripolitania and Cyrenaica in 1911, formally annexing the territory in 1912 and consolidating it under the name "Libya" from 1934 — the first use of that name for the unified territory in modern times. What it represents: the Italian Tricolor, flown as the colony's sole official flag throughout Italian rule.

c. 900 – 1911

Ottoman Tripolitania, Cyrenaica & Fezzan

Ottoman ruleNo single ruler documentedSunni Islam (established, across this Islamic-era span)
Ruling house:House of Senussi

Who & why: earlier medieval rule by various Berber and Arab dynasties, and a brief 16th-century period under the Knights of Malta and Spain at Tripoli, gives way to direct Ottoman conquest in 1551. From 1711 to 1835 the local Karamanli dynasty rules as a largely autonomous Ottoman vassal before direct Ottoman control is reimposed until the 1911 Italian invasion.

What it represents: the standard Ottoman red field with white crescent and star, flown across this territory’s long Ottoman period, including through the semi-autonomous Karamanli years.

c. 630 BCE – c. 900 CE

Greek Cyrenaica & Phoenician-Carthaginian Tripolitania

Ancient colonies & provincesBattus I (founder of Cyrene)Greek & Punic polytheism, later Roman religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Greek colonists from Thera (c. 631 BCE)Immigration: Phoenician & Carthaginian settlers

Who & why: in the east, Greek colonists from Thera found Cyrene around 631 BCE on the advice of the Delphic oracle; it grows into one of the leading cities of the Greek world under Battus I's own Battiad dynasty, later passing to Ptolemaic Egypt and then becoming the Roman province of Cyrenaica in 74 BCE. In the west, Phoenician traders establish Oea (modern Tripoli), Sabratha, and Leptis Magna from around the 7th century BCE; the region falls under Carthaginian control before Rome absorbs it after the Punic Wars, and Leptis Magna itself becomes the birthplace of Roman Emperor Septimius Severus (r. 193–211 CE). Vandal invaders take the coast in the 5th century CE, a Byzantine reconquest under Justinian follows in the 6th, and Arab armies complete the conquest of the region by the 640s–660s CE, opening the centuries of Berber and Arab rule that the page's next entry picks up.

What it represents: no flag in the modern territorial sense existed anywhere in the ancient or classical Mediterranean world; cities and empires used civic emblems, coinage, and military standards rather than a fixed banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Cyrene's founding in 631 BCE comes a generation before Solon's reforms in Athens (594 BCE); Leptis Magna and Sabratha's Phoenician origins predate Carthage's own rise to Mediterranean power. The height of Roman rule over both regions runs alongside the building of Rome's Colosseum (70–80 CE) and Pantheon (126 CE) and the reign of Leptis Magna's own native son Septimius Severus; the Vandal conquest of the 5th century coincides with the wider collapse of the Western Roman Empire (476 CE), and the Arab conquest of the 640s–660s follows within decades of the life of Muhammad and the founding of Islam.
