Flags of Liechtenstein

Long before either lordship existed, this same sliver of the Alpine Rhine valley was Roman Raetia: conquered in 15 BCE by Augustus's own stepsons Drusus and Tiberius, it stayed under Roman, then Alemannic and Frankish rule for the better part of two millennia before any prince named Liechtenstein set foot in it. A principality assembled on purpose: in 1699 and 1712 the wealthy Liechtenstein family, already prominent in the Habsburg court but landless in a way that mattered for imperial politics, bought two small lordships along the upper Rhine specifically because owning territory held directly of the Holy Roman Emperor — rather than as a vassal of some other prince — qualified a family for a seat in the Imperial Diet. Vaduz and Schellenberg were united into the Principality of Liechtenstein in 1719, named for a family that would not set foot in the place for decades.

What began as a legal instrument for court standing outlived the Empire that created it: Napoleon dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in 1806 and folded Liechtenstein into his Confederation of the Rhine, then the 1815 Congress of Vienna moved it into the German Confederation, and only that body's own 1866 dissolution left the principality fully sovereign for the first time — a status it has held, continuously and without invasion, ever since.

Scope of this page: the territory of the two former lordships of Vaduz and Schellenberg, now the modern Principality of Liechtenstein, from Roman Raetia (c. 15 BCE) to the present, five political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Migration & conquest:Immigration: Russian Liberation Army asylum (1945)

Who & why: after discovering at the 1936 Berlin Olympics that Liechtenstein's plain blue-and-red flag was identical to Haiti's, the principality adds a gold princely crown to the canton in 1937 to distinguish it. Liechtenstein stays neutral through both World Wars, joins the UN only in 1990, and today runs one of the world's highest per-capita incomes on finance, industry, and (fittingly, given its 1719 origin) a still-reigning princely house.

What it represents: the same blue-over-red bicolor traced to the 19th century, now marked with a gold crown signifying the ruling prince, formally adopted by law in 1937 and unchanged since.

Principality of Liechtenstein (fully sovereign, plain bicolor)

Principality, independentJohann IIRoman Catholic (traditional, National Church)
Ruling house:House of Liechtenstein

Who & why: the German Confederation, which had absorbed the old Holy Roman Empire's successor states after 1815, dissolves itself in 1866 following the Austro-Prussian War. With no overlord left above it, Liechtenstein becomes fully sovereign for the first time in its history. It disbands its small army in 1868 as an economy measure and, having no plausible path to defending itself militarily, commits instead to permanent neutrality — a stance that keeps it out of both World Wars.

What it represents: the blue-over-red bicolor, drawn from the Liechtenstein princely house's own livery colors and in use since at least the mid-19th century, flown here plain, before the 1937 addition of the crown.

Who & why: the 1815 Congress of Vienna, redrawing Europe after Napoleon's defeat, folds the former Confederation of the Rhine states into a new German Confederation, a loose association of 39 sovereign German-speaking states under Austrian presidency. Liechtenstein keeps its own prince and internal government throughout, as it had under Napoleon's arrangement, but remains bound into a larger confederal structure rather than standing fully alone.

What it represents: Liechtenstein flew no single fixed national flag distinct from its princely house arms during this period; shown here schematically as the princely red-and-gold heraldic coloring used on seals and court livery of the era, the direct ancestor of the blue-and-red flag adopted later in the century.

c. 15 BCE – 1719

Raetia & the medieval lordships of Vaduz and Schellenberg

Roman province, then feudal lordshipsDrusus & Tiberius (Roman conquest, 15 BCE)Local paganism, then Christianized (5th–8th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Rome (15 BCE)Settlement: Alemannic peoples (5th–8th c.)

Who & why: Celtic-speaking Rhaetian tribes inhabit the Alpine upper Rhine valley before Emperor Augustus's stepsons Drusus and Tiberius lead Rome's conquest of the Alpine regions in 15 BCE, folding the territory into the province of Raetia. As the Western Roman Empire recedes in the 5th century, Alemannic settlers move into the valley, which later passes under Frankish and Carolingian rule. Vaduz and Schellenberg emerge as separate, minor feudal lordships within the Holy Roman Empire during the high Middle Ages, held by a succession of noble families, until the Liechtenstein family purchases Schellenberg in 1699 and Vaduz in 1712, setting up their 1719 unification into a single princely territory.

What it represents: no flag in the territorial sense is documented for this valley across Roman rule or the medieval lordships that followed; military standards, provincial insignia, and individual lords' own heraldic arms stood in for any fixed banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Rome's 15 BCE conquest of Raetia falls within the reign of Augustus, the same era as the height of the early Pax Romana; the valley remains under Roman rule through the building of the Colosseum (70–80 CE) and beyond, until the Western Roman Empire's collapse in 476 CE. Charlemagne's coronation as Holy Roman Emperor in 800 CE draws the valley into the Carolingian world, and its medieval lordships take shape during the era of the Crusades and the rise of the Empire's princely states — ending only as the Liechtenstein family's purchases close out, just ahead of the War of the Spanish Succession (1701–14) that dominates European politics as the deal is finished.
