Flags of the Vardar Valley

Long before the Slavic migrations that gave rise to the region's later population, the Vardar valley was home to the independent kingdom of Paeonia, conquered by Philip II of Macedon in 359 BCE and then absorbed into Rome's province of Macedonia in 146 BCE, surviving as a Byzantine province for centuries more. Skopje served as Stefan Dušan's imperial capital in 1346; five centuries of Ottoman rule followed, briefly interrupted by a ten-day revolutionary republic in 1903 that raised a plain red flag over the town of Kruševo — a color every Macedonian flag since has kept. A 1990s dispute over an ancient royal sun symbol cost the new country a Greek trade embargo and, eventually, its own name.

No flag in this atlas changed for a more explicitly diplomatic reason than this one: red stayed constant from 1903 onward, but the sun on it was redesigned twice, the second time as a term of a treaty with a neighbor.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day North Macedonia, c. 700 BCE–present, twelve entries. An ancient bridging entry (Paeonia, Macedon, Rome, and Byzantium) precedes the 6th–7th century Slavic migrations ancestral to the region's later Macedonian Slav population; it is included for historical completeness, not as a claim of ethnic continuity. 1995 – present

Republic of North Macedonia

Sovereign stateKiro GligorovSecular (Macedonian Orthodox Church specially recognized)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Kosovo refugees (1999)

Who & why: adopted 5 October 1995, negotiated directly as part of the Interim Accord signed with Greece that September — Macedonia agreed to remove the Vergina Sun "in all its forms" from state symbols in exchange for Greece lifting the embargo. The country was renamed the Republic of North Macedonia on 12 February 2019 under the Prespa Agreement; the flag design itself did not change.

What it represents: designed by Miroslav Grčev, red is kept from the 1903 Ilinden/Kruševo tradition, but the sun is deliberately redrawn — eight broadening rays, no historical Vergina iconography — a generic "new dawn" motif chosen specifically to defuse, rather than assert, an ancient claim.

1992 – 1995

Republic of Macedonia (Vergina Sun flag)

Sovereign state (contested)Kiro GligorovSecular (Macedonian Orthodox Church specially recognized)

Who & why: after the 8 September 1991 independence referendum, the new parliament adopted this flag on 11 August 1992, centering a 16-ray gold sun excavated in 1977 at Vergina, Greece, from artifacts linked to the royal tombs of Philip II and Alexander the Great's dynasty.

What it represents: a proud claimed link to ancient Macedon — and, to Greece, an implied territorial claim on Greek Macedonia using what Athens considered exclusively Hellenic patrimony. Greece closed the port of Thessaloniki from February 1994, an embargo that cut roughly 30% from the new country's GDP and forced the flag's replacement within three years.

Socialist Republic of Macedonia

Socialist republicMetodija Andonov-ČentoState atheism
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Aegean Macedonian refugees (1946–49)Emigration: guest workers to Western Europe (1960s–)

Who & why: established as a federal unit at the Anti-Fascist Assembly for the National Liberation of Macedonia (ASNOM), 2 August 1944 — itself dated to echo Ilinden Day — formalized within Democratic Federal Yugoslavia and given its flag on 31 December 1946.

What it represents: a plain red field with a gold-bordered red star in the canton — notably the only Yugoslav republic flag that did not use the shared blue-white-red palette, instead honoring the 1903 Ilinden Uprising and the Kruševo Republic's red flag directly, with the standard Communist star layered on top.

Bulgarian Occupation

Occupation / annexationBoris IIIBulgarian Orthodox Christianity (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: BulgariaEmigration: Jewish deportation to Treblinka (1943)
Ruling house:House of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha (Bulgarian branch)

Who & why: after the April 1941 Axis invasion of Yugoslavia, Bulgaria — pursuing territorial claims going back to the San Stefano era — occupied most of Vardar Macedonia by late May 1941 and formally annexed it on 14 May 1941, enabled by its Axis alliance. A small western strip near Debar went instead to Italian-controlled "Greater Albania." Bulgaria switched sides in September 1944 and withdrew by November.

What it represents: the plain Kingdom of Bulgaria tricolor, white-green-red, flown as ordinary annexed Bulgarian territory — no separate flag was created for the occupied region, reflecting Sofia's claim that this was simply Bulgaria reunified, not a foreign conquest.

Annexed territoryPeter ISerbian Orthodox Christianity (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Kingdom of SerbiaEmigration: Muslims to Turkey (1913–30s)
Ruling house:House of Karađorđević

Who & why: the Treaty of Bucharest ceded the region to Serbia after the 1912–13 Balkan Wars; it joined the Kingdom of SHS from 1918 (informally "South Serbia" through the 1920s), then was formally organized as the Vardar Banovina in 1929, capital Skopje, under King Alexander I's unitarist reorganization — the river name "Vardar" deliberately replacing "Macedonia" to suppress a distinct regional identity.

What it represents: first the Kingdom of Serbia's own red-blue-white tricolor (1912–18), then the Kingdom of Yugoslavia's blue-white-red — no distinct symbol for the region itself, banovinas having no flags of their own.

3 – 13 August 1903

Kruševo Republic

Revolutionary republicNikola KarevNo official state religion declared (Orthodox Christian population)

Who & why: during the Ilinden-Preobrazhenie Uprising against Ottoman rule, IMRO rebels proclaimed a provisional government at Kruševo on St. Elijah's Day, 3 August 1903, led by the socialist Nikola Karev. Ottoman forces crushed the uprising within ten days.

What it represents: a plain red flag, chosen by the socialist-leaning Karev both as a revolutionary symbol and as the color of shed blood and freedom. Short-lived as it was, this is the flag every Macedonian state flag since — socialist and post-independence alike — has traced its red field back to.

Annexed territoryNo single ruler documentedSunni Islam (Ottoman state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Ottoman EmpireImmigration: Turkish & Yörük settlers
Ruling house:Mrnjavčević dynasty

Who & why: Ottoman conquest was completed by the 1390s (Skopje fell in 1392); the region was administered first under the Rumelia Eyalet, then, after the 1860s–70s Tanzimat reforms, split between the Kosovo Vilayet (northwest, capital moved to Skopje in 1888) and the Manastir Vilayet (south, capital Bitola). What it represents: vilayets flew the standard Ottoman red flag with white crescent and star — provincial identity was expressed through administrative seals, not a distinct regional flag.

1371 – 1392

Fragmentation Among Local Lords (Marko's Prilep and Neighbors)

Fragmented Ottoman vassal lordshipsNo single ruler documentedNo unified state religion (Orthodox Christianity practiced)
Ruling house:Mrnjavčević dynasty

Who & why: the Serbian Empire's central authority collapsed after the Ottoman victory at the Battle of Maritsa in September 1371 and the death of the last Nemanjić, Tsar Uroš V, that December. Macedonia splintered among competing regional lords rather than passing to a single successor: King Marko (the "Krali Marko" of South Slavic epic) ruled from Prilep as an Ottoman vassal from the 1370s, while the Dragaš/Dejanović family held territory around Velbužd and Kumanovo and other local rulers, including Constantine Dragaš, controlled remaining pockets. Direct Ottoman annexation was completed piecemeal through the 1380s, with Skopje's fall in 1392 marking the end of local lordship.

What it represents: no unified flag or heraldic symbol covered this era — each lordship, already an Ottoman tributary in practice, used its own seal or none at all, and no vexillological record survives for any of them. This entry is shown honestly blank rather than assigning one ruler's arms to the whole fragmented period.

Serbian Empire (capital at Skopje)

Empire's province / capitalStefan DušanSerbian Orthodox Christianity (established, Patriarchate founded 1346)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Serbian Empire
Ruling house:Nemanjić dynastyMrnjavčević dynasty

Who & why: Stefan Dušan was crowned "Emperor of the Serbs and Greeks" at Skopje on 16 April 1346, making the Macedonian lands his empire's political core rather than a periphery. After his death in 1355 the empire fragmented; regional lords, including King Vukašin and Prince Marko, held Macedonian territory until Ottoman conquest through the 1390s.

What it represents: a map in the Bibliothèque Nationale de France shows a red double-headed eagle on gold over Skopje — the reverse of the red-field, white-eagle scheme used elsewhere in the empire (see the Serbia atlas), possibly a regional or provincial variant.

1018 – 1346

Byzantine and Bulgarian Rule (Contested Macedonia)

Empire's province (contested)Basil IIEastern Orthodox Christianity (Byzantine state religion; contested)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Byzantine Empire (1018)Conquered by: Second Bulgarian Empire (1230)
Ruling house:Nemanjić dynasty

Who & why: Byzantine emperor Basil II, remembered as the "Bulgar-Slayer," completed his conquest of the First Bulgarian Empire in 1018, absorbing Macedonia into Byzantine provincial administration (the Theme of Bulgaria, later reorganized). Byzantine control was not continuous: the Second Bulgarian Empire, risen in 1185, pushed back into Macedonia under Tsar Kaloyan in the 1200s and especially Ivan Asen II after his 1230 victory at Klokotnitsa, briefly holding much of the region. Byzantine forces — first Epirote, then Nicaean and Palaiologan — recovered it by the mid-1240s to 1250s, after which Macedonia was fought over repeatedly by Byzantium, Bulgaria, Epirus, and an increasingly assertive Kingdom of Serbia, until Serbian expansion under Stefan Dušan absorbed it definitively in the 1340s.

What it represents: this span is shown as one bridging entry rather than invented as a tidy sequence of stable governments — three-plus centuries of frequently changing, often simultaneous and overlapping claims, not one continuous regime. No securely attested flag survives for any of these medieval administrations over this territory; Byzantine themes and Second Bulgarian Empire forces used imperial and religious banners and personal/dynastic devices, not fixed national flags, so this entry is left honestly blank.

Paeonians, Macedon, Rome & Byzantium

Ancient kingdom, then imperial provincePhilip II of Macedon (conqueror of Paeonia)Greek polytheism, then Roman/Byzantine Christianity (4th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Kingdom of Macedon (from 359 BCE)Conquered by: Rome (146 BCE)Migration: South Slavic settlement (6th–7th c.)

Who & why: centuries before the Slavic migrations that gave rise to the region's later population, the Vardar valley is home to the independent kingdom of Paeonia, attested by Greek writers including Herodotus from at least the 7th century BCE and geographically and ethnically distinct from the neighboring kingdom of Macedon to its south. Philip II of Macedon campaigns against and subdues Paeonia from 359 BCE, and it is absorbed into his kingdom's rule; Rome defeats the last independent Macedonian king at Pydna in 168 BCE and formally organizes the region as the province of Macedonia in 146 BCE. It remains a Roman, then East Roman (Byzantine), province for nearly a millennium, its population gradually Christianized and its administration surviving the Western Empire's own 476 CE collapse, until South Slavic peoples migrate into the Balkans from the 6th–7th centuries CE — the population ancestral to the region's later Macedonian Slavs — and Bulgarian expansion finally ends Byzantine control in the 840s.

What it represents: no flag or banner survives from Paeonian, Macedonian, Roman, or early Byzantine rule of this territory; ancient and late-antique states did not use fixed territorial flags in any modern sense, and the vergina sun and other royal Macedonian emblems from this era are symbols on coins and artifacts, not flags. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs from the era of Herodotus and the Persian Wars in Greece, through Philip II and Alexander the Great's conquests and the building of the Library of Alexandria under his Hellenistic successors, to Rome's Colosseum and Pantheon, the Western Empire's 476 CE fall, and Emperor Justinian's 6th-century Hagia Sophia in Constantinople — the same Byzantine capital whose weakening grip on this province the Slavic migrations and Bulgarian expansion finally break.
