Flags of Malaysia

Centuries before Malacca, Indian Ocean trade brought Hindu and Buddhist religious practice to small port kingdoms on the northern peninsula, above all Kedah Tua's Bujang Valley, whose temple ruins still stand; Kedah's court legendarily converted to Islam in 1136, well before Parameswara founded Malacca. From the Malacca Sultanate's trading empire commanding the strait that still bears its name, through more than a century of British colonial rule, brutal Japanese wartime occupation, independence for the Malayan peninsula in 1957, and a broader federation formed in 1963 — six flags across the peninsula and Borneo territories that became modern Malaysia.

The Jalur Gemilang ("Stripes of Glory") still carries fourteen stripes and a fourteen-point star today, a design fixed in 1963 for the federation's original fourteen states and territories — unchanged even after Singapore's 1965 departure left the count symbolically, rather than literally, accurate.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Malaysia (Peninsular Malaya, Sabah, and Sarawak), c. 2nd century CE–present, seven entries. The timeline cards below follow the peninsula's own lineage for readability; the map just above the timeline splits the two halves apart, since Sabah and Sarawak's own separate colonial and wartime histories genuinely diverged from the peninsula's until 1963. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Two Malaysias, c. 100–1963

Peninsular Malaysia and East Malaysia (Sabah & Sarawak, on Borneo) are separated by more than 600 km of the South China Sea — and for most of their history, by entirely different governments too. Press play, or drag the slider, to watch the two halves converge only once, in 1963.

This is the one page in this batch with a genuine, documented internal split. The peninsula and Borneo were never part of the same polity before 1963: different sultanates, then a crown colony (Straits Settlements) on one side against the private Brooke Raj and the British North Borneo Chartered Company on the other, then separate Crown Colonies even after both were nominally British. They are shown here as two disconnected regions, colored independently, because that is what they were — not one country with an internal border, but two countries that later merged.

Malaysia (federation)

Federation, independentTunku Abdul RahmanIslam (constitutional state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Indonesian migrant workers (1970s–)
Ruling house:House of JohorHouse of PerakHouse of Kedah

Who & why: on 16 September 1963, the Federation of Malaya merges with Singapore, Sabah, and Sarawak to form Malaysia. Singapore's own uneasy fit — both economic tension and communal politics between its majority-Chinese population and the peninsula's Malay-majority leadership — leads to its separation, by mutual (if strained) agreement, on 9 August 1965, after which the remaining thirteen states plus federal territories continue as Malaysia.

What it represents: fourteen alternating red and white stripes and a fourteen-point star, originally for the federation's fourteen states and territories at founding — the design was kept unchanged after Singapore's departure, now read as representing the states plus the federal government.

Federation of Malaya (independent)

Federation, independentTunku Abdul RahmanIslam (constitutional state religion)
Ruling house:House of JohorHouse of PerakHouse of Kedah

Who & why: the Federation of Malaya gains independence from Britain on 31 August 1957, led by Tunku Abdul Rahman, following negotiations that also addressed the ongoing communist insurgency of the Malayan Emergency (1948–60). Independence covers only the peninsula's states at this stage; Singapore, Sabah, and Sarawak remain separate British territories.

What it represents: eleven red-and-white stripes and an eleven-point star, one for each of Malaya's original states, with the same crescent and star canton later carried forward into the 1963 federation flag.

British Malaya (post-war)

Colonial (Britain)Edward GentNo colony-wide state religion (Islam recognized within states)
Ruling house:House of JohorHouse of PerakHouse of Kedah

Who & why: Britain returns after Japan's surrender, first proposing the centralizing Malayan Union in 1946, then, after fierce Malay political opposition, replacing it with the more devolved Federation of Malaya in 1948, restoring the individual sultans' status. This same period sees the outbreak of the Malayan Emergency, a communist insurgency Britain and Commonwealth forces spend over a decade suppressing before independence.

What it represents: the British colonial Union flag, flown as the territory's own ensign under the reconstituted post-war administration.

Who & why: Japanese forces sweep down the peninsula after landing in December 1941, taking Singapore — Britain's supposedly impregnable fortress — in February 1942 in one of the war's most significant defeats for Britain. Occupation is marked by harsh treatment, particularly of the ethnic Chinese population, and by the growth of resistance movements, including communist guerrillas who later become central to the postwar Emergency.

What it represents: the Japanese naval and military ensign, flown over the occupied peninsula and Singapore; no distinct Malayan flag flew during this period.

British Malaya (colonial)

Colonial (Britain)Robert FullertonNo colony-wide state religion (Islam recognized within sultanates)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Chinese tin-mine laborers (mid-1800s–)Immigration: Indian (Tamil) plantation laborers
Ruling house:House of JohorHouse of PerakHouse of Kedah

Who & why: Britain consolidates Penang, Malacca, and Singapore into the Straits Settlements crown colony in 1826, then extends control over the Malay peninsula's individual sultanates through a series of protectorate treaties from the 1870s to early 1900s — never fully annexing them outright, but governing through British Residents advising each sultan. Rubber and tin exports drive major Chinese and Indian immigration, reshaping the peninsula's demographics through this era.

What it represents: the British colonial Union flag, flown as the collective territories' own ensign; individual sultanates kept their own state flags for internal use throughout.

Malacca Sultanate and successor sultanates

Sultanate, independentParameswaraIslam (Malacca court religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Portugal (1511)Conquered by: Netherlands (1641)
Ruling house:House of MelakaHouse of JohorHouse of PerakHouse of Kedah

Who & why: the Malacca Sultanate, founded around 1400 by Parameswara, becomes the region's dominant trading power, commanding the strait between Sumatra and the peninsula until Portugal conquers it in 1511. Its royal line and Islamic-Malay court culture continue through successor states, notably Johor, and the peninsula settles into a patchwork of independent Malay sultanates that persists until British commercial and colonial expansion begins in earnest in the early 1800s.

What it represents: no single flag unified the peninsula's sultanates; plain red, the color traditionally associated with Malacca's own royal banners, is shown here as a schematic reconstruction representing the era, not a single documented national flag.

Kedah Tua, Langkasuka & early Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms

Kingdom, independent (Hindu-Buddhist era)No single founder documented (Kedah Tua)Hinduism & Buddhism (Islamized from 1136)
Migration & conquest:Contact/settlement: Indian Ocean traders (Hindu-Buddhist)Tributary to: Srivijaya (from c. 7th c.)

Who & why: from around the 2nd century CE, Indian Ocean trade brings Hindu and Buddhist religious practice to small port kingdoms on the northern Malay peninsula, most notably Kedah Tua (centered on the Bujang Valley, where temple ruins and inscriptions survive) and Langkasuka on the east coast, both known to Indian, Chinese, and Arab records under various transliterated names over the centuries. From around the 7th century these kingdoms fall within the tributary orbit of the Sumatra-based Srivijaya empire, and later that of Java's Majapahit, before Kedah's court legendarily converts to Islam in 1136 under Phra Ong Mahawangsa — setting the peninsula's later Malay-Islamic sultanate tradition in motion well before Parameswara founds Malacca around 1400.

What it represents: no flag or banner survives from Kedah Tua, Langkasuka, or any other Hindu-Buddhist-era Malay port kingdom; the surviving Bujang Valley temple remains are religious architecture, not a vexillological record. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span opens within the era of Indian Ocean trade described in the 1st-century Greco-Roman Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, and runs through Srivijaya's maritime heyday alongside the construction of Java's Borobudur (8th–9th century) and Cambodia's earliest temples at Angkor (9th century onward) — contemporaneous Hindu-Buddhist wonders built by the same trading world that reached Kedah's own shores.
