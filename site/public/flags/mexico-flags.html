Flags of Mexico

Mexico's own civilizational lineage runs nearly three thousand years deeper than its flag does: the Olmec, rising at San Lorenzo around 1500 BCE, are Mesoamerica's foundational "mother culture," and the region passes in turn through Teotihuacan, the Classic Maya city-states, and the Toltec before the Mexica (Aztec) people, told by their god to build a city wherever they saw an eagle perched on a cactus devouring a serpent, found that sign on a lake island and build Tenochtitlan there in 1325 — the same eagle-and-cactus emblem anchors Mexico's tricolor today. From that empire's 1521 fall through three centuries as New Spain, two short-lived empires, a war that cost half the national territory, and the 1910–20 Revolution, this page traces eight eras.

The 1846–48 Mexican-American War, triggered by a border dispute following Texas's earlier secession, ends with the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, in which Mexico cedes roughly half its national territory — including present-day California, Nevada, Utah, and most of Arizona and New Mexico — to the United States.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Mexico, c. 1500 BCE–present, eight political-flag eras. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

This page's real drama is a border that moved, not one that split. Every era shown here governed the whole of what is now Mexico at once; the 1846–48 war changed where the line around the country ran (cutting away roughly half its territory to the US), not who ruled the parts still inside it. Republic, independentVenustiano CarranzaSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Spanish Republican refugees (1939)Emigration: Bracero Program guest workers to the US (1942–64)

Who & why: the Mexican Revolution (1910–1920), a sweeping and costly conflict against Porfirio Díaz's long dictatorship and its successors, produces the progressive 1917 Constitution that still governs Mexico today. The eagle emblem is refined again in 1968, ahead of the Mexico City Olympics, into its current, more naturalistic form.

What it represents: green (independence), white (the Catholic religion, historically; today unity), and red (the union of European and Indigenous peoples, historically; today the martyrs' blood) frame the founding eagle-and-cactus-and-serpent emblem from the Mexica migration legend.

Restored Republic, the Porfiriato & the Revolution

Republic, independentBenito JuárezSecular (Reform Laws separate church and state)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Revolution refugees to the US (1910–20)

Who & why: Benito Juárez restores the republic after Maximilian's execution in 1867, followed by Porfirio Díaz's long, modernizing but increasingly authoritarian rule (the "Porfiriato," 1876–1911). Díaz's fall triggers the Mexican Revolution (1910–1920), a prolonged and often violent struggle involving figures like Francisco Madero, Pancho Villa, and Emiliano Zapata that reshapes Mexican society and eventually produces the 1917 Constitution.

What it represents: the tricolor continues through this turbulent half-century with only minor emblem revisions, shown here as one bridging entry across a genuinely fast-changing and consequential period.

Second Mexican Empire

Foreign-imposed empireMaximilian IRoman Catholic (traditional, with liberal tolerance)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: France (1862–67)
Ruling house:House of Habsburg-Mexico

Who & why: France, seeking to collect Mexican debts and exploit its own internal Liberal-Conservative conflict, invades in 1862 (defeated at first at the Battle of Puebla, 5 May, now Cinco de Mayo) and installs Austrian Archduke Maximilian as emperor in 1864. President Benito Juárez's republican government never surrenders, waging continued resistance until French troops withdraw and Maximilian is captured and executed by firing squad in 1867.

What it represents: the same green-white-red tricolor, with four golden eagles added to the corners marking Maximilian's imperial (rather than republican) government.

First Mexican Empire

Empire, independentAgustín de IturbideRoman Catholic (established, per the Plan of Iguala)
Ruling house:House of Iturbide

Who & why: Mexico wins independence from Spain in September 1821 after an 11-year war beginning with Miguel Hidalgo's 1810 revolt; Agustín de Iturbide, architect of the final independence pact, has himself proclaimed emperor in 1822, but growing republican opposition forces his abdication within less than two years.

What it represents: the first Mexican tricolor, designed under the 1821 Plan of Iguala — green, white, and red for independence, religion, and unity — centered on a crowned eagle perched on a cactus, referencing the Mexica founding legend of Tenochtitlan.

1521 – 1821

Viceroyalty of New Spain

Colonial ruleHernán CortésRoman Catholic (Spanish colonial establishment)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Spain (1521)Immigration: enslaved Africans & Spanish colonists

Who & why: Hernán Cortés, aided by Indigenous allies resentful of Aztec rule and by a devastating smallpox epidemic, completes the conquest of Tenochtitlan in August 1521, ending the Aztec Empire and founding Mexico City on its ruins. Nearly three centuries of Spanish colonial rule follow as the Viceroyalty of New Spain, marked by the fusion (and frequently forced imposition) of Spanish, Catholic, and Indigenous cultures, and by a war of independence that begins with Miguel Hidalgo's 1810 Grito de Dolores.

What it represents: the Cross of Burgundy (a red saltire on white), the common flag of the Spanish Empire's overseas viceroyalties, flown across New Spain's territory for most of this colonial period.

1325 – 1521

Aztec (Mexica) Empire

Empire, independentTenochMexica state religion (Huitzilopochtli cult)
Ruling house:Mexica (Aztec) tlatoani dynasty

Who & why: the Mexica people found Tenochtitlan in 1325 on a lake island, guided (per their founding legend) by the sight of an eagle devouring a serpent atop a cactus — the same emblem still on Mexico's flag today. By the early 1500s Tenochtitlan is one of the largest cities on Earth, anchoring an empire covering much of central Mexico, until Hernán Cortés's Spanish-led conquest (aided by Indigenous rivals of the Aztecs and by smallpox) topples it in 1519–1521.

What it represents: no flag in the European heraldic sense existed in Mesoamerica; Aztec military and religious standards (often feathered devices carried on the back) served a related signaling purpose. Shown here as an informed reconstruction of the eagle-cactus-serpent founding emblem on a plain field, not a documented banner, since the true device would not have taken this rectangular flag form.

c. 1500 BCE – 1325 CE

Olmec and Pre-Aztec Mesoamerican Civilizations

Fragmented / successive civilizationsNo single ruler documentedMesoamerican polytheism (varied by civilization)
Migration & conquest:Migration: Chichimeca peoples into the Valley of Mexico (12th–13th c.)

Who & why: the Olmec, centered at San Lorenzo (from c. 1500 BCE) and later La Venta in Mexico's Gulf Coast lowlands, are Mesoamerica's earliest major civilization and are widely regarded by archaeologists as its foundational "mother culture" — the source of artistic, religious, and political patterns (including the colossal carved stone heads for which they are best known) that reappear across every later Mesoamerican civilization. After Olmec decline by around 400 BCE, the torch passes in turn to Teotihuacan (whose Pyramid of the Sun rises around the 1st–2nd century CE, and whose city grows to perhaps 100,000 people at its peak before collapsing around 550 CE), the Classic Maya city-states of the southern lowlands (Tikal, Palenque, Copán, flourishing especially 250–900 CE), and the Toltec at Tula (c. 900–1150 CE), whom the Mexica themselves later claimed as their civilizational model. Tenochtitlan's founders inherit this millennia-deep Mesoamerican tradition rather than starting one from scratch.

What it represents: no flag or banner in any modern sense survives, or was likely ever created, by any of these civilizations — shown here honestly blank. Olmec rulers are depicted on stone monuments with rank regalia rather than flags; Teotihuacan and the Classic Maya used painted murals, carved stelae, and elite costume to signal authority and identity instead.

World context: Olmec San Lorenzo rises as Egypt's New Kingdom is winding down and as Shang-dynasty China is inscribing its earliest oracle-bone writing; La Venta's peak overlaps the traditional 753 BCE founding of Rome. Teotihuacan's height in the 4th–5th centuries CE runs alongside Rome's fall in the west (476 CE), and its collapse around 550 CE is roughly contemporary with the Maya Classic city-states' own rise; those Classic Maya centers flourish through the same centuries as the early Islamic caliphates' rapid expansion and Tang-dynasty China, and the Toltec capital of Tula falls at almost exactly the moment the Crusader states are being founded in the eastern Mediterranean.
