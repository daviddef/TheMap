Flags of the Black Mountain

A mountain refuge that never fully fell to the Ottomans: an eagle banner from the last independent medieval Serbian principality, three and a half centuries ruled by warrior prince-bishops from Cetinje, a brief kingdom, absorption into Yugoslavia twice over, and a 2006 return to independence that consciously skipped back over the 20th century to the old royal heraldry. That royal lineage itself reaches back further still, through the medieval Kingdom of Duklja — crowned by the Pope in 1077 — to Roman Doclea and the independent Illyrian tribes Rome conquered in 168 BCE.

The double-headed eagle survives on Montenegro's flag today in an unbroken (if reconstructed) line back to the 15th-century Crnojević princes — the deepest continuity of any symbol in this atlas.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Montenegro, c. 168 BCE–present, twelve political entities. Part of a larger atlas covering the former Yugoslav lands — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Two coastlines, one country — eventually

Montenegro's own real border, split into the two pieces that were, for most of history, governed apart: the highland interior around Cetinje — the mountain redoubt "Montenegro" is named for — and the Bay of Kotor coastline, which didn't join Montenegro until 1918. Press play, or drag the slider.

The Bay of Kotor was foreign soil for longer than it's been Montenegrin. Venice held it from 1420, Austria from 1797 — the highlands and the coast didn't share a government once in over four and a half centuries, right through two Montenegrin monarchies. The border line below only solidifies where the two pieces were actually ruled apart — it dissolves the moment one government held both.

2006 – present

Republic of Montenegro

Sovereign stateFilip VujanovićSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: the flag design was adopted 13 July 2004, while Montenegro was still part of the State Union — a deliberate assertion of distinct identity ahead of the 21 May 2006 independence referendum, which passed with 55.5%, just over the required threshold. Independence was formally declared 3 June 2006.

What it represents: a conscious revival of pre-communist, monarchical-era heraldry — the gold double-headed eagle with scepter and orb, a green shield bearing a gold lion passant on its breast, a golden crown above — explicitly bypassing both the socialist star and the plain, Serbia-linked tricolor, tying the new state back to the medieval Zeta and Petrović-Njegoš royal line this page opened with.

1992 – 2006

FR Yugoslavia / State Union of Serbia and Montenegro

FederationMomir BulatovićSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: refugees from the Yugoslav wars & Kosovo (1990s)

Who & why: after the other four republics seceded in 1991–92, Serbia and Montenegro formed a rump "third Yugoslavia," renamed the looser State Union of Serbia and Montenegro in 2003 under EU pressure to avert full Montenegrin secession. Each republic kept significant autonomy, including separate economic policy.

What it represents: the shared blue-white-red Yugoslav tricolor, star stripped away — though domestically, as independence sentiment grew, Montenegro increasingly flew its own separate tricolor-and-eagle flag alongside it.

1943/45 – 1991/92

Socialist Republic of Montenegro

Socialist republicBlažo JovanovićState atheism

Who & why: Tito's Partisan-led federal restructuring recognized Montenegro as a distinct constituent republic at the November 1943 AVNOJ sessions, formalized 31 December 1946. Its borders essentially matched modern Montenegro — smaller than the pre-1918 kingdom (the Sanjak/Metohija areas stayed with Serbia) but consolidating the Bay of Kotor.

What it represents: the historic red-blue-white tricolor, reviving pre-1918 Montenegrin identity within the federation, with the standard Yugoslav-republic gold-bordered red star replacing the royal eagle — continuity and communism laid over each other, same formula as every other Yugoslav republic.

1941 – 1943/45

Kingdom of Montenegro (Italian-occupied)

Puppet stateAlessandro Pirzio BiroliOrthodox Christianity (traditional, unofficial)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Italy (1941–43)

Who & why: after invading and dismembering Yugoslavia in April 1941, Italy detached Montenegro and, following the crushed July 1941 Cetinje uprising, sponsored a Montenegrin "Federalist" separatist faction to proclaim a restored kingdom under Italian protection. No king was ever actually installed — Prince Michael Petrović-Njegoš, offered the crown, refused it — leaving the "kingdom" a governing committee propped up by occupying troops while Partisan and Chetnik resistance fought on through the territory.

What it represents: a nostalgic revival of the pre-1918 royal tricolor and crowned eagle, restaged as propaganda to lend the occupation a veneer of restored Montenegrin statehood; shown with a faint broken border, since real sovereignty never accompanied the symbol.

1918 – 1941

Zeta Banovina, Kingdom of Yugoslavia

Provincial administrationAlexander I KarađorđevićSerbian Orthodox Christianity (dynasty's faith)

Who & why: the former Montenegrin lands were administered first as an undifferentiated military/civil district after the 1918 union, then folded into the Zetska oblast created by the Kingdom of SCS's 1922 administrative reform. King Alexander I's 1929 royal dictatorship replaced the oblasts with nine banovinas named for rivers and mountains rather than historic nations; the Zeta Banovina, centered on Cetinje, stretched from Montenegro through parts of Herzegovina to the Dalmatian coast around Dubrovnik.

What it represents: deliberate erasure of national naming and symbols — the banovina flew the plain blue-white-red flag of the Kingdom of Yugoslavia, same as every other province, with no Montenegrin eagle, no Montenegrin name, and no distinct provincial flag of its own.

1918

Union with the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes

Disputed transitionPeter I of SerbiaSerbian Orthodox Christianity

Who & why: the Podgorica Assembly, convened 24–29 November 1918 by a pro-union committee coordinating with the Kingdom of Serbia, voted to depose King Nikola I and unite Montenegro unconditionally with Serbia; three days later Serbia itself merged into the Kingdom of SCS. The vote's legitimacy was disputed by pro-independence "Greens" loyal to the Petrović-Njegoš dynasty, whose failed Christmas Uprising followed in January 1919 — a rupture still echoed in Montenegrin politics.

What it represents: Montenegro's royal symbols were simply retired; the territory became first "the Montenegro region," then the Zeta Banovina from 1929, spanning Montenegro plus parts of Herzegovina and the Dalmatian coast around Dubrovnik — 462 years of Petrović-Njegoš-linked statehood folded into someone else's flag.

1910 – 1918

Kingdom of Montenegro

KingdomNicholas I Petrović-NjegošSerbian Orthodox Christianity
Migration & conquest:Emigration: economic migrants to the United States (early 1900s)
Ruling house:House of Petrović-Njegoš

Who & why: Nikola I proclaimed the Kingdom on 28 August 1910, the 50th anniversary of his reign — recognition of Montenegro's international standing after full independence at the 1878 Congress of Berlin. Montenegro fought alongside Serbia in the 1912–13 Balkan Wars, gaining territory around Plav-Gusinje and Peć, before Austro-Hungarian occupation from January 1916 truncated effective control.

What it represents: the same tricolor and eagle, now topped with a royal rather than princely crown — full monarchical sovereignty asserted alongside Europe's other kingdoms, at what would prove to be Montenegro's territorial and diplomatic peak before its abrupt dissolution.

1852 – 1910

Principality of Montenegro (secular)

PrincipalityDanilo I Petrović-NjegošSerbian Orthodox Christianity
Ruling house:House of Petrović-Njegoš

Who & why: Danilo I ended the prince-bishopric in 1852 by renouncing the ecclesiastical office, declaring Montenegro a secular hereditary principality with Russian backing. His nephew Nikola I (r. 1860–1918) introduced Montenegro's first written constitution in 1905, formalizing the state tricolor. Wars with the Ottomans, especially after 1876–78, roughly doubled Montenegro's territory, adding Nikšić, Podgorica, and Bar.

What it represents: a red-blue-white tricolor — the pan-Slavic colors, echoing Russia — with a white double-headed eagle, bearing scepter, orb, and a lion-shield on its breast, centered on the red band.

1516 – 1852

Prince-Bishopric of Montenegro

TheocracyVladika VavilaSerbian Orthodox Christianity
Ruling house:House of Petrović-Njegoš

Who & why: beginning with Danilo I Petrović-Njegoš (Metropolitan from 1697), a hereditary line of Orthodox prince-bishops (vladikas), seated at Cetinje, governed Montenegro as a fused church-and-state theocracy, functionally self-ruling despite nominal Ottoman claims. Petar II Petrović-Njegoš (r. 1830–1851) is remembered as a towering figure of South Slavic literature and nation-building.

What it represents: a red war/state banner with a white cross pattée, first flown during the Great Turkish War of the late 17th century — church and resistance fused into one symbol, the Orthodox cross standing for both spiritual authority and defiance of Ottoman rule.

1496 – 1516

Sanjak of Montenegro (Ottoman rule)

Ottoman provinceBayezid IISunni Islam (Ottoman)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Ottoman Empire (1496)

Who & why: the Ottomans completed their conquest of the Crnojević lands around 1496, ending the last of Zeta's independent rule; the territory was organized into the directly-administered Sanjak of Montenegro (Sandžak-i Karadağ) under an Ottoman sanjak-bey. Direct rule proved difficult to sustain in the mountainous interior, and from 1513–1516 the Ottomans began shifting toward a looser arrangement, granting the local Orthodox church and clan leaders a degree of self-government under the vladikas of Cetinje.

What it represents: two decades with no distinct local flag — the sanjak flew generic Ottoman provincial banners, not a symbol specific to this territory, so no honest reconstruction is shown here.

c. 1451 – 1496

Principality of Zeta (Crnojević)

PrincipalityStefan CrnojevićSerbian Orthodox Christianity
Ruling house:House of Crnojević

Who & why: the Crnojević noble family ruled Zeta as the last independent medieval Serbian principality after the wider Serbian Despotate fell to the Ottomans. As Ottoman pressure mounted, the Crnojevići retreated into the mountainous interior around Cetinje by roughly 1482 — the retreat Venetians began calling "Monte Negro," Black Mountain.

What it represents: a gold double-headed eagle on red — the same Byzantine-derived symbol claimed by the Nemanjić dynasty and its Serbian successor states, asserting Zeta's place in that same royal lineage even as its territory shrank to a mountain redoubt.

c. 168 BCE – 1451

Ancient Doclea, and the Kingdom of Duklja/Zeta

Fragmented / successive ruleMihailo I of Duklja (crowned king, 1077)Illyrian & Roman paganism, then Eastern Orthodoxy (from c. 9th–10th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Rome (168 BCE)Migration: South Slavic peoples into the Balkans (6th–7th c.)

Who & why: the Illyrian tribes of this coast fall to Rome in 168 BCE, after which the territory is organized into the province of Praevalitana, centered on the city of Doclea; that city's ruins, just outside modern Podgorica, give their name to the early medieval Slavic principality of Duklja that emerges after South Slavic peoples settle the region in the 6th–7th centuries. Duklja rises to a kingdom under Mihailo I, crowned by Pope Gregory VII in 1077, and reaches its height under his son Constantine Bodin; it is absorbed into the growing Nemanjić Serbian state by the late 12th century, re-emerges under its older name "Zeta" as a semi-autonomous Nemanjić province, and passes to the Balšić noble family after the Serbian Empire's collapse, before the Crnojević dynasty takes over around 1451.

What it represents: no flag or banner survives from the Illyrian tribes, Roman Doclea, or the early Slavic Kingdom of Duklja itself; the double-headed eagle later flown by the Crnojević (shown one card up) descends from Nemanjić and Byzantine heraldry adopted only once Duklja/Zeta came fully within that world. Shown here honestly blank for the deeper era rather than projecting that later symbol backward.

World context: Rome's 168 BCE conquest of the Illyrian coast comes in the same generation as the Roman destruction of Carthage and Corinth (146 BCE), completing Rome's domination of the wider Mediterranean world. Mihailo I's 1077 papal coronation falls in the very same year as Henry IV's famous penitential "Walk to Canossa" during the Investiture Controversy, a high point of medieval papal authority across Europe; and the Nemanjić absorption of Duklja/Zeta by the late 12th century runs alongside the Third Crusade and the Ayyubid Sultan Saladin's recapture of Jerusalem (1187).
