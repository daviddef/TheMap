Flags of Myanmar

From the Pyu city-states' millennium-long river-trade network and the Bagan, Toungoo, and Konbaung dynasties that in turn unified and reunified the Irrawaddy basin, through British colonization following three Anglo-Burmese wars, brutal Japanese wartime occupation, independence in 1948, and a political history dominated for most of the decades since by the military — eight entries tracing one of the region's longest-running struggles between civilian and military rule.

The current flag, adopted in 2010, has flown over both a short-lived democratic opening (2011–2021) and the military rule that returned after the February 2021 coup — a reminder that a flag's design and a country's freedoms don't always change together.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Myanmar, c. 200 BCE–present, eight political entries. The timeline opens with the Pyu city-states, the region's first well-documented urban civilization, predating unified Burmese statehood by well over a thousand years. The Bagan, Toungoo, and Konbaung dynasties are combined into one long bridging entry, since no single flag is attested for the earlier two and the Konbaung royal peacock banner is used here to represent the whole pre-colonial span, with that scope made explicit. Japanese occupation (State of Burma)

Occupation (Japan)Ba MawNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Imperial Japan (1942–45)Emigration: Indian residents' overland trek (1942)

Who & why: Japanese forces invade in early 1942 and drive out British administration by May; Japan installs a nominally independent State of Burma under Ba Maw in August 1943, hoping to win nationalist support, while the exiled British colonial government continues to plan for reoccupation. Aung San, who had initially collaborated with Japan, switches sides in 1945 to join the Allied advance that retakes the country.

What it represents: a golden peacock, a traditional Burmese royal and national symbol dating to the Konbaung dynasty, on a green field — the flag of Ba Maw's Japanese-sponsored republic.

1886 – 1942

British Burma

Colonial (Britain)Queen VictoriaNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Indian laborers & administrators (colonial era)

Who & why: the third Anglo-Burmese War in 1885 ends the Konbaung dynasty entirely; Britain formally annexes what remains of the kingdom on 1 January 1886, initially administering Burma as a province of British India before separating it into its own colony in 1937. A nationalist movement builds through the interwar decades, and the country's push for self-rule is interrupted by the Japanese invasion of 1942.

What it represents: the British colonial Union flag, flown as the territory's own ensign, with no distinct Burmese national symbol under direct colonial rule.

c. 1044 – 1886

Bagan, Toungoo, and Konbaung dynasties

Kingdom, independentAnawrahtaTheravada Buddhism
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Mongol Empire (1277–87 invasions)
Ruling house:Pagan DynastyTaungoo DynastyKonbaung Dynasty

Who & why: King Anawrahta founds the Bagan Empire around 1044, the first major unification of the Irrawaddy basin, before Mongol invasion breaks it apart in the late 1200s. The Toungoo dynasty reunifies the region in the 1500s, building briefly one of Southeast Asia's largest empires, and the Konbaung dynasty, founded by Alaungpaya in 1752, is the last independent Burmese kingdom — ended only by three nineteenth-century Anglo-Burmese wars (1824–26, 1852, 1885) that progressively strip its territory before Britain annexes what remains in 1886.

What it represents: no flag survives from Bagan or Toungoo; the golden peacock, the Konbaung dynasty's own royal emblem from the eighteenth century onward, is shown here representing the whole pre-colonial span as an informed reconstruction, not a documented banner from Bagan's own era.

c. 200 BCE – 9th c. CE

Pyu City-States (Irrawaddy Valley)

Fragmented / city-state networkSri Ksetra (leading city-state)Buddhism (Theravada & Mahayana, with Hindu influence)
Migration & conquest:Migration: Tibeto-Burman peoples into the Irrawaddy valley (from c. 200 BCE)Conquered by: Nanzhao Kingdom (832–835 CE campaigns)
Leading city-state:Sri Ksetra

Who & why: Tibeto-Burman-speaking Pyu peoples move south into the Irrawaddy valley by around the 2nd century BCE, building a network of walled, moated brick city-states — Sri Ksetra, Beikthano, and Halin foremost among them, now jointly inscribed as the Pyu Ancient Cities UNESCO World Heritage Site — that never unify under one crown but together form Myanmar's first well-documented urban civilization. Trade contact with India brings Buddhism and a Brahmi-derived Pyu script; the network flourishes as a link on overland and riverine routes between India and China for roughly a thousand years, until the Yunnan-based Nanzhao kingdom's campaigns of 832–835 CE sack Pyu cities and scatter their population, fatally weakening the network in the same span that incoming Bamar (Burman) migrants settle the region and, within two centuries, found the unified kingdom at Bagan.

What it represents: no flag or banner of any kind survives from the Pyu era; shown here honestly blank. Pyu material culture instead survives as monumental brick stupas, burial urns, and Pyu-script inscriptions at Sri Ksetra, Beikthano, and Halin.

World context: this millennium runs alongside the late Roman Republic and the Roman Empire it becomes, the Han dynasty's rise and fall in China, India's Gupta Empire (4th–6th c. CE) whose trade and Buddhist missionary contacts helped shape Pyu religion and script, and the construction of Java's Borobudur temple in the early 9th century CE — roughly contemporary with the Nanzhao campaigns that break the Pyu network apart.
