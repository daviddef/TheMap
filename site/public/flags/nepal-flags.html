Flags of Nepal

Nepal is the one entry in this atlas that was never colonized by a European power. Its story runs instead through the Kathmandu Valley's own first securely documented dynasty, a patchwork of later hill and valley principalities, a single military unification campaign that built the modern state from the Gorkha hills outward, a war and treaty with the East India Company that fixed Nepal's borders without ever putting it under the Crown, a century of hereditary prime ministers ruling behind a titular throne, and a long, often interrupted push toward democracy that finally ends the monarchy altogether in 2008.

Through nearly all of that, one emblem persists: a crimson double pennant bearing a moon and a sun, the world's only national flag that is not a quadrilateral. Its colors and shape trace to Shah-era royal banners and Rana-era military pennants; its exact geometry was fixed only in 1962, when the sun and moon lost the human faces they had carried for generations.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Nepal, c. 400 CE–present, eight political entities. Nepal was never a European colony — the 1814–16 Anglo-Nepalese War cost it border territory under the Sugauli Treaty but left it a sovereign, if British-influenced, buffer state throughout. No single flag can be documented for either the ancient Licchavi-era Kathmandu Valley or the later fragmented pre-unification era, so both entries are shown honestly blank rather than invented. 2008 – present

Federal Democratic Republic of Nepal

Federal republicGirija Prasad KoiralaSecular (declared 2006, confirmed 2008)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: labor migration to the Gulf & Malaysia (2008–)

Who & why: the newly elected Constituent Assembly meets on 28 May 2008 and votes to abolish the 240-year-old Shah monarchy, proclaiming the Federal Democratic Republic of Nepal. The move follows the 2006 peace accord that ended a decade-long Maoist insurgency and a mass pro-democracy movement earlier that same year; a 2015 constitution formalizes Nepal's present structure of seven federal provinces.

What it represents: the flag itself does not change at the republic's founding — only the state beneath it does. The crimson double pennant, blue border, and white sun and moon continue unaltered from their 1962 geometric standardization.

1990 – 2008

Restored constitutional monarchy

Kingdom, constitutional monarchyKing BirendraHinduism (constitutional Hindu Kingdom)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Bhutanese (Lhotshampa) refugees (early 1990s)
Ruling house:Shah Dynasty

Who & why: the 1990 People's Movement forces King Birendra to lift the ban on political parties and accept a new constitution restoring multiparty democracy under the crown. A Maoist insurgency against the monarchy and government breaks out in 1996 and runs for a decade, killing an estimated 17,000 people before a November 2006 peace accord brings the rebels into mainstream politics. The June 2001 royal massacre, in which Crown Prince Dipendra killed King Birendra and much of the royal family before dying himself, passes the throne to the unpopular King Gyanendra, whose own 2005 seizure of direct power collapses within a year under renewed mass protest.

What it represents: the standardized 1962 double pennant continues unchanged through this turbulent constitutional period.

1960 – 1990

Panchayat system

Kingdom, absolute (Panchayat)King MahendraHinduism (constitutional Hindu Kingdom)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Tibetan refugee resettlement (1960s–70s)
Ruling house:Shah Dynasty

Who & why: unhappy with the elected government, King Mahendra stages a royal coup in December 1960, dissolves parliament, bans political parties, and imprisons the elected prime minister. In their place he installs the Panchayat system: a tiered structure of indirectly elected councils that keeps real authority with the palace behind a facade of local representation. It lasts three decades, through Mahendra's reign and into his son Birendra's, until mass protest forces its end in 1990.

What it represents: the crimson double pennant reaches its present, legally fixed geometric form in the 1962 constitution, which also strips the human faces that the sun and moon had carried since at least the 19th century.

Kingdom, constitutional monarchyKing TribhuvanHinduism (traditional state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Tibetan refugees (1959–60)
Ruling house:Shah Dynasty

Who & why: the 1950–51 revolution, backed by India and the Nepali Congress, ends more than a century of Rana rule and restores King Tribhuvan to actual power. Nepal holds its first democratic general election in 1959, bringing B. P. Koirala and the Nepali Congress to office — a brief opening that King Mahendra closes with his own coup at the end of 1960.

1846 – 1951

Rana oligarchy

Kingdom, Rana oligarchyJung Bahadur RanaHinduism (state religion, Muluki Ain)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Gurkha recruitment into British service
Ruling house:Shah DynastyRana Dynasty

Who & why: the September 1846 Kot Massacre, in which Jung Bahadur Kunwar (later Rana) orchestrates the killing of dozens of rival courtiers and nobles in the royal palace courtyard, leaves him in control of the army and government. He takes the hereditary title of prime minister for his family, later renamed Rana, reducing the Shah kings to titular monarchs while real power passes down a hereditary line of Rana prime ministers for the next 104 years.

What it represents: the flag itself does not change with the Kot Massacre — the same crimson pennant continues as Nepal's national and royal symbol, flown now over a state actually run by the Rana family rather than the Shah kings it nominally still served.

1768 – 1846

Kingdom of Nepal (Shah dynasty, unification)

Kingdom, independentPrithvi Narayan ShahHinduism (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Gurkha recruitment begins (1815)
Ruling house:Shah Dynasty

Who & why: Prithvi Narayan Shah, king of the small hill principality of Gorkha, begins a military campaign of conquest in the 1740s–50s and captures the Kathmandu Valley in 1768–69, ending Malla rule there and founding the Shah dynasty's Kingdom of Nepal on the unified territory. Continued Gorkhali expansion northward and westward eventually provokes the British East India Company, and the 1814–16 Anglo-Nepalese War ends with the March 1816 Sugauli Treaty: Nepal cedes border territory (including much of the modern Nepal-India borderlands) but is never annexed or made a colony, remaining a sovereign, British-influenced buffer state.

What it represents: the crimson double pennant with a human-faced sun and moon, combining royal Shah and later Rana-era military pennant traditions, is Nepal's earliest well-documented national banner; the specific proportions shown here are an informed reconstruction rather than an official specification, since none existed yet.

c. 1200 – 1768

Fragmented hill kingdoms and Kathmandu Valley principalities

Fragmented hill kingdomsNo single ruler documentedHinduism / Buddhism (varied by kingdom)
Ruling house:Malla Dynasty

Who & why: from around 1200, the Kathmandu Valley is ruled by the Malla dynasty, which by 1482 splits into three rival city-kingdoms — Kathmandu, Patan, and Bhaktapur — each with its own court. Outside the valley, dozens of small principalities scattered across the middle hills, including the small kingdom of Gorkha, govern independently with no shared crown. This patchwork ends only when Gorkha's Prithvi Narayan Shah conquers the valley in 1768–69 and begins unifying the surrounding hill states under one throne.

What it represents: no single flag can be attributed with confidence to this fragmented era — the Malla courts used royal seals, coats of arms, and temple banners rather than fixed territorial flags, and no one design can stand in for dozens of separate principalities. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 400 – 750 CE

Licchavi Dynasty (Kathmandu Valley)

Kingdom, Kathmandu Valley (independent)Manadeva I (earliest securely dated king)Hinduism & Newar Buddhism (both patronized)
Migration & conquest:Military alliance: Tang China & Tibet (648 CE campaign)Marriage alliance (traditional): Tibetan Empire
Ruling house:Licchavi Dynasty

Who & why: the Licchavi dynasty is the earliest polity ruling the Kathmandu Valley for which a hard documentary record survives: dozens of dated Sanskrit stone inscriptions, beginning with King Manadeva I's own inscription of 464 CE, fix a firm chronology centuries before any surviving Nepalese flag or comparable emblem. Licchavi kings, likely originating from northern India, preside over a golden era under Amshuverma in the early 7th century and later Narendradeva, whose restoration to the throne comes with Tibetan and Chinese backing; a marriage alliance with Tibet's Songtsen Gampo (by tradition) and a Nepali contingent that joined a 648 CE Tang Chinese campaign into northern India mark the dynasty's documented reach beyond the valley itself. The Licchavi line fades from the record after about 750 CE into a poorly documented transitional ("Thakuri") period that precedes the rise of the Malla dynasty around 1200.

What it represents: no flag or comparable territorial banner survives from the Licchavi era, centuries before flags in any modern heraldic sense existed anywhere in South Asia; royal authority is instead recorded through stone inscriptions, coinage, and seals. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this dynasty's documented span runs alongside the golden age of India's Gupta Empire immediately to the south, the fall of the Western Roman Empire (476 CE) and Byzantium's construction of the Hagia Sophia (completed 537 CE), the rise of Islam and the rapid Arab conquests from the 630s, and the height of Tang dynasty China, whose 648 CE embassy into India drew directly on Licchavi Nepal's own troops.
