Flags of the Dominion of Newfoundland

Six centuries before any permanent European colony, Norse voyagers out of Greenland — the expeditions the Icelandic sagas link to Leif Erikson — made brief landfall around 1000 CE at what is now L'Anse aux Meadows, the only confirmed Norse site in North America, on an island and mainland already home to the Beothuk, Mi'kmaq, Innu, and Inuit. A fully separate British Dominion followed much later — the same constitutional status Canada, Australia, and New Zealand held at the time, with its own parliament, currency, and Olympic team — from 1907 until the Great Depression bankrupted it into surrendering self-government altogether in 1934. Newfoundland spent the next fifteen years back under direct British colonial administration before voting, twice, in a bitterly contested pair of 1948 referendums, to join Canada as its 10th province in 1949.

Newfoundland is the only Dominion in this atlas that gave up its self-government voluntarily and never got it back as an independent state — its arc runs from a fleeting pre-colonial Norse camp and centuries of indigenous life, through colony, to full Dominion, back down to colony in all but name, and finally into another country entirely.

Scope of this page: the island of Newfoundland and mainland Labrador, c. 1000 CE–present, five political stages. Newfoundland's 1907 Dominion status was a change in constitutional standing, not a change in flag — like several entries elsewhere in this atlas, the same banner can carry different political meaning across eras. Part of the atlas's Vanished States hub, alongside four other real sovereign states with no surviving successor — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Newfoundland and Labrador, one government

The island of Newfoundland and the Labrador mainland, both traced whole — two separate landmasses that have always been governed as a single territory, from the earliest colony through Dominion status to Canadian confederation. Press play, or drag the slider, to watch who held it, era to era.

This is a single-region page — Labrador and the island of Newfoundland have shared one government since 1809. Despite being geographically separate (and despite Labrador's own 1927 boundary being disputed with Quebec into the 20th century), no era here saw the island and the mainland coast administered as genuinely separate polities.

1949 – present

Province of Newfoundland (and Labrador), Canada

Canadian provinceJoseph SmallwoodSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: outmigration following the 1992 cod moratorium

Who & why: two closely fought 1948 referendums — the first inconclusive among three options (independence, Confederation, or continued Commission government), the second a narrower 52–48 vote for joining Canada over restored independent Dominion status — lead to Newfoundland's entry into Confederation on 31 March 1949 as Canada's 10th province, under Premier Joseph Smallwood. The province formally adds "and Labrador" to its name in 2001, and adopts its own distinct provincial flag (a red, gold, blue, and white stylized banner by artist Christopher Pratt) in 1980.

What it represents: Canada's own Red Ensign, flown as the national and provincial flag from 1949 until the 1965 Maple Leaf flag replaced it nationwide; Newfoundland's own distinct provincial flag follows only in 1980.

1934 – 1949

Commission of Government

Suspended self-governmentSir David Murray AndersonSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to mainland Canada & the US (Depression era)
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: crushed by war debt, a collapsed fish trade, and the Great Depression, Newfoundland's own parliament votes in 1933 to dissolve responsible government altogether, formally surrendering it to Britain on 16 February 1934 in exchange for financial rescue. A six-member Commission of Government, three Newfoundlanders and three Britons, appointed rather than elected, rules by decree for the next fifteen years — a Dominion voluntarily stepping back down to colonial status, something no other Dominion in the British Empire ever did.

What it represents: the plain Union Jack, flown as Newfoundland's official flag through the Commission years in place of the earlier Red Ensign, marking the reversion to direct colonial administration.

1907 – 1934

Dominion of Newfoundland

Dominion, self-governingSir Robert BondSecular (no state religion)
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: the 1907 Colonial Conference in London upgrades Newfoundland, alongside Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and the Union of South Africa, to full Dominion status — self-governing in every internal matter, with foreign affairs still coordinated through Britain, but constitutionally equal in standing to Canada. As its own Dominion, Newfoundland raises the Newfoundland Regiment for the First World War, suffering catastrophic losses at Beaumont-Hamel on 1 July 1916, an event still marked as Memorial Day in Newfoundland every year.

What it represents: the same Red Ensign flown as a colony continues unaltered as the Dominion flag — 1907 changed Newfoundland's constitutional status, not its banner.

1610 – 1907

Colony of Newfoundland

British colonyJohn GuyChurch of England (informal)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: English West Country & Irish settlers (18th–19th c.)
Ruling house:House of Stuart

Who & why: England establishes its first formal colony on the island at Cupers Cove in 1610, building on nearly a century of seasonal fishing settlement following John Cabot's 1497 landfall; Newfoundland becomes Britain's oldest colony. Responsible (self-)government arrives in 1855, giving the colony its own elected assembly and premier while still formally under the Crown — a stepping stone toward the 1907 Dominion upgrade.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Red Ensign pattern, Union Jack canton with a distinguishing colonial badge in the fly, flown from the mid-to-late 19th century onward as the colony's recognized flag.

c. 1000 CE – 1610

Norse Vinland & the Beothuk, Mi'kmaq & Innu

Indigenous nations & brief Norse camp, pre-colonialLeif Erikson (Norse voyage, c. 1000 CE)No single shared religion documented
Migration & conquest:Norse voyage & brief settlement (c. 1000 CE, abandoned within years)

Who & why: long before any European colony, the island of Newfoundland was home to the Beothuk, while the Labrador mainland was travelled and occupied by Innu and Inuit peoples, with Mi'kmaq presence extending from the Maritimes; none of these were organized as a single state with one flag. Around 1000 CE, Norse voyagers sailing out of Greenland — the same expeditions the Icelandic sagas associate with Leif Erikson — established a small base camp at what is now L'Anse aux Meadows on Newfoundland's northern tip. Excavated from 1960 onward, it is the only confirmed Norse archaeological site in North America and predates Columbus's 1492 Atlantic crossing by nearly five centuries, though it was occupied only briefly before being abandoned. No sustained European presence followed until Cabot's 1497 landfall and eventual permanent settlement six centuries later.

What it represents: no flag survives or was likely ever made by either the Norse camp or the island's and mainland's indigenous peoples in this era — shown here honestly blank. The Icelandic sagas describe Norse ships and shields but no national banner of the kind that would not become standard in Scandinavia itself for centuries.

World context: the Norse voyage to Vinland falls within decades of Leif Erikson's father Erik the Red founding Greenland's first Norse colonies (c. 985) and just before the Great Schism of 1054 split Latin and Byzantine Christianity; further afield, Song dynasty China's economy and printing culture were flourishing, and the Chola Empire held sway over much of South India and the Bay of Bengal.
