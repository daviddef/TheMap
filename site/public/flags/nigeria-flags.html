Flags of Nigeria

Nigeria's borders were drawn in London, not by any Nigerian state: before the 20th century the territory now called Nigeria held hundreds of independent polities — the walled Hausa city-states, the 19th-century Sokoto Caliphate born of Usman dan Fodio's jihad, the bronze-casting Kingdom of Benin, the once-mighty Oyo Empire, and decentralized Igbo communities with no king at all — none of them flying anything resembling a shared national flag. Frederick Lugard's 1914 amalgamation welded a British coastal colony to two inland protectorates into a single administrative unit; independence in 1960 kept those borders and added a flag, hand-picked from a public design competition, that has now outlasted every government that has flown it — civilian or military, republic or the war fought to break it apart.

Nigeria's green and white have flown continuously since 1 October 1960 — through a 1963 republic, a string of military coups, a civil war that nearly split the country in two, and a return to democracy in 1999 — the only flag in this lineage that has never once changed since the day it was first raised. The deepest root of this page's timeline reaches back further still, to the Nok culture's terracotta sculptors and early ironworkers on the Nigerian plateau some two thousand years before any of that.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Nigeria, from the ancient Nok culture (c. 1500 BCE) to the present, six political entries. Before the 20th century this territory held no single government — the Sokoto Caliphate, the Kingdom of Benin, the Oyo Empire, and hundreds of Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba, and other polities existed side by side on their own separate timelines, none flying a shared or even a comparable flag. Rather than invent artificial start and end dates for each of these coexisting states, this page groups the whole pre-colonial era into one honest “fragmented” entry, and notes the individual conquests that ended several of them — Benin in 1897, Sokoto in 1903 — within the following entry's own narrative rather than as separate timeline splits. The Northern and Southern Nigeria Protectorates were real, separate entities, each with its own High Commissioner and administration. Federal Republic of Nigeria

Federal republicNnamdi AzikiweSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: West African migrant workers (1970s oil boom)Emigration: skilled-worker brain drain (1980s–)

Who & why: Nigeria becomes a republic on 1 October 1963, replacing Queen Elizabeth II as head of state with a Nigerian president while remaining in the Commonwealth. The First Republic collapses in a January 1966 military coup, followed by a countercoup that July; worsening regional and ethnic tension leads the Eastern Region to declare itself the independent Republic of Biafra on 30 May 1967, triggering a civil war that runs until Biafra's surrender in January 1970. Nigeria returns to civilian rule in 1979, endures further military governments through the 1980s and 1990s, and has held continuous civilian democratic rule since 1999.

What it represents: the flag itself never changes through any of this — the same green-white-green tricolor adopted at independence in 1960 has flown over every government since, civilian or military, republic or the four years Biafra tried to leave it.

1960 – 1963

Independent Nigeria (Commonwealth dominion)

Dominion, independentAbubakar Tafawa BalewaSecular (no state religion)

Who & why: Nigeria becomes independent on 1 October 1960 as a Commonwealth realm, with Queen Elizabeth II as head of state represented locally by a Nigerian Governor-General, Nnamdi Azikiwe. The new flag, chosen through a 1959 nationwide public design competition, is designed by Michael Taiwo Akinkunmi, a Nigerian student then studying in London; the competition committee removes a red sun he had placed at the center of his original entry, leaving the plain tricolor that is hoisted for the first time at the independence ceremony in Lagos.

What it represents: green bands for Nigeria's agriculture and forests, the white band for peace and unity — a deliberately simple design meant to represent all of Nigeria's regions and peoples equally, with no ethnic or regional symbol.

1914 – 1960

Colony and Protectorate of Nigeria

ColonyFrederick LugardAnglican (colonial establishment)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Lebanese & Syrian traders (from c. 1900s)

Who & why: Governor-General Frederick Lugard amalgamates the Colony of Lagos, the Protectorate of Southern Nigeria, and the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria into one administrative unit on 1 January 1914, ruled thereafter mainly through indirect rule via existing local authorities. Nationalist parties (the NCNC, the Action Group, the NPC) grow rapidly after the Second World War, and a sequence of constitutions — Richards 1946, Macpherson 1951, Lyttelton 1954 — steadily builds toward a federal structure and self-government, culminating in the independence granted in 1960.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign — a dark blue field with the Union Jack in the canton and a colonial badge in the fly — used here for the unified Colony and Protectorate; badge artwork for British colonial Nigeria is not consistently documented and is shown as a simplified, schematic reconstruction.

1861 – 1914

Colony of Lagos & the Niger protectorates

ProtectorateOba Dosunmu (Docemo)Anglican (colonial establishment)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: British Empire (1861–1903)Immigration: Saro & Aguda returnees to Lagos

Who & why: Britain annexes Lagos on 6 August 1861 — primarily to suppress the slave trade and secure coastal trade — and formalizes it as a Crown Colony in 1862. The Royal Niger Company administers much of the interior under royal charter until 1900, when the Crown takes over directly and proclaims the Protectorate of Southern Nigeria and the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria on 1 January 1900 (Lagos Colony merges into Southern Nigeria in 1906). British conquest of the remaining independent African states continues through this period: the 1897 Benin Expedition ends the Kingdom of Benin's independence, and the Sokoto Caliphate falls to British forces in 1903.

What it represents: the British Blue Ensign, used with locally distinct badges by Lagos Colony, Southern Nigeria, and Northern Nigeria; shown here without a badge since the three administrations did not share one, and no single design covers this whole span.

Pre-colonial polities of the Niger region

Fragmented kingdomsNo single ruler documentedNo single state religion
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Atlantic slave trade (16th–19th c.)Conquered by: Sokoto Caliphate (Fulani jihad, 1804–08)
Ruling house:Sokoto Caliphate (Fodio dynasty)Kanem-Bornu Empire (Sayfawa dynasty)Oyo Empire (Alaafin dynasty)Kingdom of Benin (Eweka dynasty)

Who & why: the territory that becomes Nigeria is never one state before colonization. In the north, Hausa city-states and the Bornu Empire give way in 1804–08 to the Sokoto Caliphate, founded by Usman dan Fodio's Fulani jihad and eventually the largest state in Africa at the time. In the southwest, the Oyo Empire dominates much of Yorubaland from roughly the 1400s before collapsing into civil wars by the 1830s. In the south, the Kingdom of Benin, tracing its ruling dynasty to around the 1180s, becomes renowned for its fortified capital and bronze and ivory casting. Across the southeast, Igbo communities are governed by decentralized village councils and title societies rather than kings at all. These states rise, fall, war, and trade with one another and with Atlantic Europeans across this entire span without ever forming one political unit.

What it represents: no single flag survives, and none could — this is not one nation but hundreds. Regalia, beaded crowns, ceremonial staffs, and (for the Sokoto Caliphate) Islamic banners carried identity and authority, but no fixed, standardized territorial flag comparable to a modern national flag is attested for any of these states. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 1500 BCE – 500 CE

Nok Culture

Ancient culture, structure unknownNo single ruler documentedReligious practice unknown (ritual use inferred)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Bantu expansion begins in the Nigeria–Cameroon borderland (from c. 1500 BCE)

Who & why: named for the village of Nok in central Nigeria where its distinctive terracotta sculptures were first identified in 1928 (during tin-mining), the Nok culture produced sophisticated life-sized and near-life-sized ceramic figures across a large area of the Nigerian plateau for roughly two thousand years. Radiocarbon and thermoluminescence dating place the culture from around 1500 BCE to as late as 500 CE, and some of its sites show iron-smelting furnaces among the earliest securely dated in West Africa — though whether ironworking here was independently developed or diffused from elsewhere remains debated among archaeologists.

What it represents: no flag, script, or comparable state symbol survives or is inferred for the Nok culture — its social and political organization is reconstructed almost entirely from terracotta fragments and smelting sites, not from any textual or heraldic record. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Nok's terracotta tradition runs roughly alongside ancient Egypt's Third Intermediate and Late Periods, the rise of the Kingdom of Kush at Napata and then Meroë in what is now Sudan (whose rulers built more pyramids than Egypt itself), the founding of Carthage on the North African coast (traditionally 814 BCE), and the Assyrian and Achaemenid Persian empires further east — a reminder that Iron Age West Africa was developing in parallel with, not isolated from, the wider ancient world's own great building projects and empires.
