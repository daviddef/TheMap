Flags of Northern Ireland

Northern Ireland is the youngest political entity in this whole atlas, created by the 1921 partition of Ireland, and it remains the only nation in the United Kingdom with no single flag of its own officially recognized in law today. This page follows the province of Ulster from its early medieval Gaelic over-kingdoms, through the Anglo-Norman invasion, plantation and union, to partition, to a two-decade experiment with an official government flag, and finally to the present, deliberately unresolved status quo.

The history here is contested and, in living memory, violent. It is presented factually and without taking a side: this is a record of which flags had legal or governmental standing when, not an endorsement of any of the communities or causes associated with them.

Scope of this page: the six counties that became Northern Ireland in 1921 (historically part of the province of Ulster, though Ulster itself has nine counties), c. 450–present, six political entities. Before 1921 this territory's flag history is identical to the rest of Gaelic Ireland/the Kingdom of Ireland/the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland — see the Ireland page for the fuller island-wide account; entries here are written specifically from Ulster/Northern Ireland's own vantage point. No internal governing split exists within Northern Ireland's own modern borders at any point covered here, so this page carries no internal-partition map. Northern Ireland (direct rule to devolution, no official flag of its own)

Part of the United KingdomWilliam WhitelawNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Eastern European workers (2004–)Emigration: Troubles-era departures (1970s–90s)
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: escalating violence in the conflict known as the Troubles leads the UK government to prorogue the Stormont Parliament in March 1972, imposing direct rule from Westminster; devolved government is restored only intermittently thereafter, most durably after the Good Friday Agreement of April 1998 establishes the Northern Ireland Assembly and a power-sharing executive (itself suspended for various periods since, including 2017–2020 and 2022–2024). Roughly 3,600 people are killed in the Troubles between the late 1960s and the 1998 Agreement, across republican, loyalist, and state actors; this page states that fact neutrally without assigning blame to any single community.

What it represents: with Stormont's own governmental flag (the Ulster Banner, next entry) losing official status in 1972, the Union Flag becomes and remains the region's only officially flown state flag; Northern Ireland today has no single national flag of its own recognized in law, and flag display remains a genuinely contested, politically sensitive question, regulated in narrow circumstances by the Flags Regulations (Northern Ireland) 2000.

Northern Ireland (Stormont government flag, the Ulster Banner)

UK, devolved government bannerBasil Brooke (Lord Brookeborough)No official state religion
Migration & conquest:Emigration: refugees fleeing south (Aug 1969)
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: Northern Ireland's devolved government at Stormont, established by the 1920 Government of Ireland Act and opened in 1921, initially flies only the Union Flag. In 1953, to mark Elizabeth II's coronation, the Governor of Northern Ireland is granted the right to fly a new banner derived from the Northern Ireland government's own coat of arms; it comes into use as the government's flag from 1953 until the Stormont Parliament is prorogued in 1972.

What it represents: the Ulster Banner is built on the St George's Cross (marking continued union with England/the UK) charged with a six-pointed star bearing the Red Hand of Ulster — an ancient symbol long associated with the O'Neill dynasty and with Ulster generally — topped by the Crown of St Edward, referencing the monarchy. It carried official status only as the Stormont government's own flag, never as Northern Ireland's national flag in the way the Union Flag or the Republic's tricolor are national flags; it lost that status in 1972 and has no official standing today, though it is still used informally, including by some sporting bodies and within loyalist communities.

1921 – 1953

Northern Ireland (created by partition)

Part of the United KingdomJames Craig (Viscount Craigavon)No official state religion
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: the Government of Ireland Act 1920 partitions Ireland, creating two devolved territories within the United Kingdom: Northern Ireland (six northeastern counties, with a unionist, majority-Protestant electorate built up since the Plantation era) and Southern Ireland, which never functions as intended. The Northern Ireland Parliament opens at Belfast City Hall in June 1921; the December 1921 Anglo-Irish Treaty gives the new territory the explicit right to opt out of the emerging Irish Free State, which its own Parliament exercises within the first month, making the 1921 border permanent after a Boundary Commission's findings are quietly shelved in 1925.

What it represents: Northern Ireland flies only the Union Flag as its official flag from the outset — there is, at this point, no separate government flag or Ulster Banner yet; that only arrives in 1953.

Ulster (within the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland)

Part of the United KingdomWilliam Pitt the YoungerChurch of Ireland (established until 1871)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Great Famine & after (1845–)
Ruling house:House of HanoverHouse of Windsor

Who & why: the Act of Union 1800, in force from 1 January 1801, abolishes the separate Parliament of Ireland and merges it into Westminster, creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. Ulster, like the rest of Ireland, is governed directly from London for the whole of this span. The 19th century sees Ulster's economy, particularly around Belfast, industrialize far more heavily than the rest of Ireland (shipbuilding, linen), reinforcing a distinct regional identity; growing nationalist demands for Irish Home Rule from the 1880s onward are met with fierce unionist resistance concentrated in Ulster, including the mass 1912 signing of the Ulster Covenant, setting up the partition to come.

What it represents: the 1801 Union Flag, adding St Patrick's saltire to the earlier 1606 design to mark Ireland's inclusion, flies over the whole of Ireland including Ulster throughout this period.

Gaelic and Plantation Ulster (Lordship / Kingdom of Ireland)

Part of the Lordship / Kingdom of IrelandJohn de CourcyRoman Catholic, later Church of Ireland
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Anglo-Norman & English Crown (from 1177)Immigration: Ulster Plantation settlers (from 1606)Emigration: Ulster Scots to American colonies (18th c.)
Ruling house:O'Neill dynastyO'Donnell dynastyHouse of Stuart

Who & why: John de Courcy's 1177 invasion of eastern Ulster establishes the short-lived Earldom of Ulster, but for most of the medieval period Ulster remains the most thoroughly Gaelic part of Ireland, dominated by the O'Neill and O'Donnell dynasties well after Anglo-Norman control elsewhere had taken hold. That changes decisively after the Nine Years' War (1593–1603) ends in Gaelic defeat and the 1607 Flight of the Earls, when the Ulster Plantation (from 1606, formalized 1610) confiscates land from Gaelic lords and settles Protestant tenants from England and, especially, Scotland — the demographic and political foundation for the unionist-versus-nationalist divide that shapes everything after 1921. Ulster is governed throughout this span as part of the wider Lordship of Ireland (from 1177) and then the Kingdom of Ireland (from 1541), both under the English/British crown.

What it represents: the gold harp on blue is the badge of the Lordship, then Kingdom, of Ireland throughout this period — no separate Ulster flag existed under the crown at this stage; the Red Hand of Ulster, shown here alongside it, is the province's own much older heraldic device, traditionally linked to the O'Neill dynasty and already in informal use as Ulster's own regional symbol well before it was later incorporated into the 1953 Ulster Banner.

Early Christian & Gaelic Ulster (Uláid, Airgíalla, Cénél nEógain)

Fragmented Gaelic over-kingdomsSaint Patrick (traditional 5th-century mission)Christianization (5th–7th c.), then Roman Catholic
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Dál Riata settlement of western Scotland (from c. 500 CE)Viking raids on Ulster's monasteries (from c. 795 CE)
Ruling house:Cénél nEógain (proto-O'Neill)

Who & why: for over seven centuries before John de Courcy's 1177 invasion, Ulster is governed by rival Gaelic over-kingdoms rather than any single crown: the Uláid along the eastern coast (the kingdom that gives Ulster its name), the Airgíalla client-kingdoms in the south, and the Cénél nEógain, who expand steadily from the north-west to become the region's dominant power by the eve of the Anglo-Norman invasion. Christianity, traditionally associated with a 5th-century mission linked to Saint Patrick, takes root across this same span, and Armagh, in the heart of Ulster, becomes Ireland's leading monastic and ecclesiastical center.

What it represents: no heraldic flag survives or is credibly attested for any Gaelic Ulster over-kingdom in this era — authority was expressed through kingship, church foundations, and annal-recorded genealogy, not a fixed territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span opens as the Western Roman Empire falls (476) and runs through the Viking Age that also battered Ulster's own monasteries from the 790s, alongside Charlemagne's Frankish empire and, in Ulster's own scriptorium tradition, the production of lavishly illuminated gospel manuscripts in the same Insular art style as the Book of Kells (c. 800) — Ireland's peripheral position did not mean isolation from the wider currents remaking early medieval Europe.
