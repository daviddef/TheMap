Flags of Norway

A convoluted, fjord-cut coastline with no obvious internal partition to draw — Norway's dramatic story runs outward instead, through two long personal unions with its Scandinavian neighbors and a five-year wartime occupation, bookended by a unification cult built around a dead king and a 1905 divorce settled without a shot fired.

Nine entries, newest first: from over a millennium of undocumented Iron Age farming communities, through the Viking-Age patchwork of petty kings, the unified medieval kingdom, four centuries bound to Copenhagen, a century bound to Stockholm, and the modern cross flag that has now flown — interrupted once, by force — for well over a hundred years.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day mainland Norway, c. 500 BCE–present, nine political entities. Norway has no clean internal governance split within its own historic territory worth inventing — unlike Hungary's Royal/Ottoman divide or Yugoslavia's republics, its major ruptures are external unions and their endings, so each era below is shown as a single whole-country color change rather than a partitioned map. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

One country, four crowns, c. 500 BCE–present

Norway's real traced coastline, colored by whichever crown actually governed it at each snapshot. There is no internal line to draw here — when Norway's sovereignty changes, the whole country changes color and label at once. Press play, or drag the slider.

Norway has spent almost exactly half of the last eleven centuries under someone else's crown. Roughly 420 of the ~910 years since unification (c. 995) were spent in a union where Norway did not control its own foreign policy — 1397–1814 under Denmark (with a Kalmar interlude shared with Sweden too), then 1814–1905 under Sweden. The 1940–45 occupation is shorter but harsher: the only stretch since the Viking Age when Norway was ruled by neither its own crown nor a fellow Scandinavian one.

1945 – present

Kingdom of Norway (restored)

Sovereign kingdomHaakon VIILutheranism (state church until 2012)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Vietnamese, Balkan & Syrian refugees (1975–)
Ruling house:House of Glücksburg

Who & why: liberated from German occupation in May 1945, King Haakon VII returns from five years of exile in London to a hero's welcome. Norway becomes a founding NATO member in 1949, discovers North Sea oil in the late 1960s, and twice votes narrowly against joining the European Union (1972, 1994) while remaining in the European Economic Area.

What it represents: the same cross flag Norway has flown since 1821 — the occupation changed nothing about the design itself; Norwegians simply resumed flying, openly, the flag the exiled government and resistance had kept flying the whole time.

1940 – 1945

German occupation (Reichskommissariat Norwegen)

Occupied territoryJosef TerbovenLutheranism (state church, nominal)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Nazi Germany (1940–45)Emigration: wartime refugees & deportation of Jews

Who & why: Germany invades on 9 April 1940; unlike Denmark's hours-long capitulation, Norwegian and Allied forces resist for two months before King Haakon VII and the government escape to London, where they continue functioning as the legal government-in-exile throughout. Reichskommissar Josef Terboven administers the occupied country directly, while the Norwegian fascist Vidkun Quisling — whose name became an international byword for "collaborator" — is installed as puppet minister-president from February 1942.

What it represents: no distinct Norwegian occupation flag existed; German administration flew the Reich's own colors, while Quisling's Nasjonal Samling party used its own separate gold sun-cross emblem for party purposes. The legitimate Norwegian cross flag was banned from official use domestically but never stopped flying abroad, on Allied ships, and in secret at home.

1905 – 1940

Kingdom of Norway (independent)

Sovereign kingdomHaakon VIILutheranism (state church)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the United States (tapering by 1920s)
Ruling house:House of Glücksburg

Who & why: the Storting unilaterally dissolves the union with Sweden on 7 June 1905; an August referendum returns a near-unanimous vote in favor, and King Oscar II accepts the outcome after negotiations at Karlstad rather than risk war — a rare peaceful union dissolution in European history. Prince Carl of Denmark is invited to take the throne, choosing the name Haakon VII to deliberately revive Norway's last fully independent medieval king.

What it represents: the same flag flown since 1821, now finally the flag of a fully sovereign state rather than one partner in a personal union — the union badge that had marked the previous era's state flag is simply gone, nothing added in its place.

1814 – 1905

Union of Sweden and Norway

Union kingdomKarl XIV JohanLutheranism (state church)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the United States (19th c.)
Ruling house:House of Bernadotte

Who & why: the January 1814 Treaty of Kiel forces Denmark — on the losing side of the Napoleonic Wars — to cede Norway to Sweden's king. Norwegians did not simply accept the transfer: they convened at Eidsvoll and adopted their own constitution on 17 May 1814, still Norway's constitution today, and briefly declared full independence under a Danish prince. A short war that autumn forced them instead into a narrower personal union with Sweden, one that left Norway its own constitution, laws, and (from 1821) its own flag — a far looser arrangement than the old bond with Denmark had become. No distinct flag was ever created for that seven-month interlude of self-declared independence between Kiel and the actual union with Sweden; Norway simply kept using its existing (still Dannebrog-derived) colors until the matter of a proper national flag was taken up by the Storting the following years.

What it represents: Norway designs its own flag in 1821 (Fredrik Meltzer's cross design, still in use today) rather than sharing Sweden's; from 1844 the state and war ensign additionally carries a small combined red-white-blue "union badge," nicknamed sildesalaten ("herring salad") by Norwegians who largely disliked it as a mark of subordination — the plain civil flag never carried it at all.

1523 – 1814

Denmark–Norway (personal union)

Union kingdomFrederick ILutheranism (state church, from 1537)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: German miners at Kongsberg (from 1623)
Ruling house:House of Oldenburg

Who & why: when Gustav Vasa breaks Sweden out of the Kalmar Union in 1523, Denmark and Norway stay bound together under the same crown — a narrower union that will run almost three centuries, far outlasting Kalmar itself. Real power sits increasingly in Copenhagen: the 1536–37 Reformation formally abolishes Norway's separate royal council and Catholic hierarchy, after which Norway is administered, in practice, much like a Danish province, even though it is never legally annexed and keeps a separate law code and the nominal title of a kingdom throughout.

What it represents: Norway has no flag of its own for this entire span — the Dannebrog, Denmark's ancient red-and-white cross, serves as the flag over Norwegian territory too, a visible marker of just how thoroughly Copenhagen came to dominate the union's institutions.

1397 – 1523

Kalmar Union (Denmark, Norway & Sweden)

Union kingdomMargaret IRoman Catholicism (state religion)
Ruling house:House of Oldenburg

Who & why: Margaret I, having spent the 1380s and 1390s assembling personal control over all three Scandinavian crowns, has her great-nephew Eric of Pomerania crowned king of a single union at Kalmar Castle in June 1397, binding Denmark, Norway (with its dependency Iceland), and Sweden (with Finland) under one monarch for the only time in this atlas's history. Each kingdom keeps its own laws and nobility; Swedish resentment of Danish-favored rule builds for over a century until Gustav Vasa's 1523 rebellion breaks Sweden out for good.

What it represents: the Kalmar Union banner — not a formally codified flag so much as a widely used device — is remembered as a gold field bearing a red cross with three crowns, one for each united kingdom; Norway, already the weakest of the three partners by this point, has no separate flag of its own during the union.

c. 995 – 1397

Kingdom of Norway (medieval, independent)

Sovereign kingdomOlav TryggvasonRoman Catholicism (Christianization from 995)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Hanseatic German merchants at Bergen (from c. 1360)
Ruling house:House of Fairhair

Who & why: unification, built on Harald Fairhair's contested 9th-century legacy, is cemented by kings like Olav Tryggvason (r. 995–1000) and Olaf II Haraldsson, whose death at the 1030 Battle of Stiklestad — fighting rebellious chieftains — gives the new kingdom a unifying royal saint cult that outlasts him by centuries. Norway reaches the height of its medieval power under Haakon IV and Magnus the Lawmender in the mid-13th century, ruling Iceland (from 1262), Greenland, and briefly the Hebrides, before the crown passes by marriage into the Kalmar Union in 1397.

What it represents: the crowned gold lion rampant, bearing St. Olav's silver axe, on a red field — Norway's royal arms, attested from the 13th century and still the national coat of arms today, shown here as a reconstructed banner rather than a strictly documented vexillological standard, since medieval Norway (like most of its era) worked primarily through heraldry rather than fixed national flags.

Fragmented petty-kingdoms & chieftaincies

Fragmented chieftainciesHarald FairhairNorse paganism
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Viking settlement of Iceland, Faroes & beyond

Who & why: the traditional Battle of Hafrsfjord (c. 872), where Harald Fairhair is said to have first claimed authority over a fragmented coastline, is likely more legend than settled fact this early — real, durable unification is still over a century away. Regional petty-kings and jarls contest the western fjords and southern lowlands throughout this period even as Norse raiding and settlement fan out across the North Atlantic, including the 874 settlement of Iceland.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic, pre-Christian era; any authority was personal and local rather than symbolized by a fixed territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 500 BCE – 793 CE

Nordic Iron Age Scandinavia

Fragmented chieftainciesNo single ruler documentedNorse paganism
Migration & conquest:Contact: Roman-era trade goods reach Norway (c. 1st–4th c. CE)

Who & why: for well over a millennium before the traditional 872 date for Harald Fairhair's unification, Norway's coast is home only to scattered farming and fishing communities under local chieftains — the Pre-Roman, Roman, and Migration-period Iron Age. The earliest known runic inscription in the Elder Futhark alphabet anywhere, carved into an iron spearhead found at Øvre Stabu, dates to around 180 CE, direct physical evidence that Norwegian society had already developed a distinct writing system centuries before the Viking Age most associate with runes. The conventional start of that Viking Age itself — the 793 raid on Lindisfarne monastery in Northumbria, carried out by raiders including Norwegians — marks this entry's own end point.

What it represents: no flag or comparable territorial banner is attested from this era; the only surviving symbolic record is burial goods, votive deposits, and the earliest runic carvings, none of which constitute anything resembling a flag. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the classical Greek and Hellenistic worlds, the Roman Republic's rise and its transformation into an empire under Augustus, and, much closer to its end, Charlemagne's Frankish empire and the 793 Lindisfarne raid that conventionally opens the Viking Age across Western Europe — a reminder that Iron Age Norway, though undocumented in its own words, was never disconnected from the trade networks and, eventually, the raiding that tied it to the wider ancient and early medieval world.
