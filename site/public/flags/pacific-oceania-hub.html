Flags of the Pacific

No region in this atlas is more geographically dispersed. Long before any European ship entered the Pacific, Austronesian-speaking peoples had already settled it — a millennia-long expansion out of island Southeast Asia that carried Melanesian, Micronesian, and finally Polynesian voyagers across open ocean using wayfinding: reading stars, swells, currents, and bird flight without instruments. Polynesian navigators reached New Zealand, Hawai'i, and Rapa Nui (Easter Island), the three points of the "Polynesian Triangle," centuries before Columbus crossed the Atlantic — one of the great, underrecognized feats of human exploration.

European contact came late and slowly: Magellan crossed the Pacific in 1521, Abel Tasman charted parts of Australia, Tasmania, and New Zealand in the 1640s, and James Cook's three voyages (1768–1779) mapped much of the region in detail. Britain founded a penal colony at Sydney in 1788 that became Australia, and in 1840 signed the Treaty of Waitangi with Māori chiefs, still New Zealand's founding constitutional document. Germany, France, and the United States carved up most of the remaining islands as coaling stations, plantations, and strategic outposts. The great wave of independence came late too, mostly the 1960s–1980s, and several islands remain constitutionally tied to the powers that once colonized them.

Drag the slider or press play; each snapshot compresses a messier reality into whoever actually held power at that moment.

Colors here are shared rule, not decoration. When two or more territories — however far apart across the ocean — answered to the very same flag, they take the very same color, and the caption below the map merges them into one named power. Tonga keeps one persistent red from the moment it first appears here, because it is the one Pacific nation that never took another color at all — it was never formally colonized.

About this hub

The map above uses one shared Pacific-wide projection: real, simplified traced coastlines for the three large landmasses, and honest position-markers — not forced coastline polygons — for the eleven small and scattered island nations, since coastline-tracing atolls thousands of miles apart at this scale would either be illegible or would force the three large countries off the map entirely. Regions here don't share physical borders the way adjacent countries do in the atlas's other hubs, so instead of a border dissolving between neighbors, this map dissolves labels: any two or more territories under the identical flag in a given era merge into one named caption beneath the map, no matter how far apart they actually sit.

Colors on the country cards above are drawn from each country's current national flag, not a historical palette, since no per-country flag lineage exists yet for most of these fourteen. Once a country's own page publishes, its card here will link out.
