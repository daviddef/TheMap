Flags of Papua New Guinea

Highland communities in what is now Papua New Guinea were cultivating crops independently, and very early — among the first places on Earth to develop agriculture on its own, going back millennia at sites like Kuk Swamp — while organizing into thousands of distinct clans and villages rather than any single overarching polity. That fragmentation, and the extraordinary linguistic diversity it produced, is still visible today: over 800 living languages are spoken here, more than in any other country in the world.

The documented flag history proper begins in 1884, when Germany and Britain carve the territory into two separately administered colonies on the same island. Australia inherits and eventually unifies that administration across the 20th century, and Papua New Guinea becomes fully independent on 16 September 1975 — the black, red, and gold flag chosen that year, carrying the raggiana bird-of-paradise and the Southern Cross, is the one still flown today.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Papua New Guinea, pre-1884–present, four political entries. The 1884–1920 entry covers a genuinely divided colonial period — German rule in the northeast, British/Australian rule in the southeast — shown here as a split flag and, below, a real internal-split map. 1975 – present

Independent Papua New Guinea

Independent stateMichael SomareNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: West Papuan refugees (1960s–)

Who & why: Papua New Guinea gains full independence from Australian administration on 16 September 1975, with Michael Somare as its first prime minister, closing out a path to self-government that had been building through the 1960s–70s. It joins the United Nations and Commonwealth of Nations shortly after, retaining the British monarch as head of state under a parliamentary system.

What it represents: the flag was chosen from a 1971 national design competition won by Susan Karike, then a 15-year-old student; the raggiana bird-of-paradise, a bird found nowhere else, sits on black, while five white stars forming the Southern Cross — visible only from the southern hemisphere — sit on red, the country's own black-red-gold palette rather than any inherited colonial scheme.

1920 – 1975

Australian-administered Territory of Papua and New Guinea

Mandate / trust territoryEvan WisdomNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Japan (1942–45, northern territory)

Who & why: after Australian forces occupy German New Guinea in the opening weeks of WWI, the League of Nations formally hands it to Australia as a Class C mandate in 1920, administered alongside the already-Australian Territory of Papua (itself transferred from Britain in 1906) but kept as two separate administrations until a 1949 act unifies them into the single Territory of Papua and New Guinea. After WWII the former mandate continues as a United Nations trust territory under Australia, with the combined territory moving through self-government in the early 1970s toward full independence in 1975.

What it represents: neither territory nor the later combined administration had its own distinct flag; the Australian Blue Ensign, the flag of the administering power itself, was what flew over government buildings throughout this period.

1884 – 1920

Divided colonial rule

Divided colonial ruleNo single ruler documentedNo single state religion
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Germany & Britain (1884 partition)Immigration: Chinese & Pacific Islander plantation laborers

Who & why: in 1884, Germany annexes the northeastern mainland and nearby islands as German New Guinea (initially run by the chartered New Guinea Company, then directly by the German government from 1899), while Britain declares a protectorate over the southeastern mainland the same year, transferring its administration to Australia in 1906 as the Territory of Papua. The two territories run as entirely separate colonial administrations, with different flags, different capitals, and no shared government, until Australian forces occupy German New Guinea within weeks of the outbreak of WWI in 1914 and formally take it over as a mandate in 1920, unifying administration of the island for the first time.

What it represents: this entry is shown as a split flag rather than picking one side: the left half stands in for the German colonial flag — a white field carrying Germany's own black-white-red imperial colors and colonial eagle motif — and the right half stands in for the British/Australian Blue Ensign flown over Papua, honestly showing the dual rule rather than erasing either half of it.

Indigenous Papua New Guinea

Indigenous nations, pre-contactNo single ruler documentedIndigenous animist traditions
Migration & conquest:Immigration: earliest human settlement (c. 50,000 years ago)Immigration: Austronesian/Lapita coastal settlement (c. 3,000 years ago)

Who & why: the highlands of what is now Papua New Guinea are among the earliest sites of independently developed agriculture anywhere in the world, with farming at places like Kuk Swamp reaching back several thousand years. The territory before 1884 is home to thousands of distinct clan and village communities, organized locally rather than under any single overarching kingdom or state, and to the most linguistically diverse population on Earth — over 800 living languages are still spoken here today.

What it represents: no single flag, in the European heraldic sense, ever governed this territory before colonial claims arrived in 1884; with no unifying polity, there is no banner to reconstruct. This entry is shown honestly blank rather than inventing one, matching the same approach taken for other pre-contact eras across this atlas.
