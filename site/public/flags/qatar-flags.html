Flags of Qatar

From a pre-Islamic peninsula on the fringe of the Sasanian Persian Empire, home to an attested Christian ecclesiastical province, through centuries spent inside Bahrain's own orbit under the Al Khalifa's loose claim, an Ottoman garrison era at Doha, an 1868 British-brokered settlement that first recognized a separate Qatari sheikhdom under the Al Thani family, a 1916 protectorate treaty, independence in 1971, and a transformation from a poor pearling coast into one of the world's wealthiest states on the back of natural gas.

Qatar's maroon-and-white serrated flag, one of the most distinctive in the world, traces back to the same plain red field flown across this whole coast for centuries — the maroon shade and nine-point serration were fixed only in 1949, a late refinement of a much older regional tradition rather than a wholly new design.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Qatar, c. 1st century CE–present, seven political entries, opening with the pre-Islamic peninsula's place within the Sasanian Persian sphere and the Church of the East's Beth Qatraye province. State of Qatar (independent)

Emirate, independentAhmad bin Ali Al ThaniSunni Islam
Migration & conquest:Immigration: South Asian & Southeast Asian migrant labor
Ruling house:House of Al Thani

Who & why: Qatar declares independence on 3 September 1971 as Britain withdraws from the Gulf, having briefly explored and then abandoned joining the wider federation that becomes the UAE. The 1995 discovery-era buildout of the North Field, the world's largest natural gas field (shared with Iran), transforms Qatar within a generation from one of the poorer Gulf sheikhdoms into one of the wealthiest countries per capita on Earth, later hosting the 2022 FIFA World Cup.

What it represents: the maroon-and-white serrated flag, its design fixed in 1949 and its exact maroon shade and nine points settled by then, continues unchanged as the flag of the independent state — the serrations are said to distinguish Qatar from Bahrain's own similar historical banner.

Ottoman garrison era & Al Thani local rule

Ottoman suzerainty / local rule
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Ottoman Empire (Doha garrison, 1871–1915)
Ruling house:House of Al Thani

Who & why: an Ottoman garrison is stationed at Doha from 1871, asserting nominal Ottoman suzerainty over Qatar as part of a wider push into eastern Arabia, though the Al Thani sheikhs continue governing day-to-day local affairs throughout. The arrangement ends abruptly with the Ottoman garrison's withdrawal at the outbreak of World War I in 1915, clearing the way for the 1916 British treaty.

What it represents: Ottoman provincial banners fly over the garrison itself, while the Al Thani continue using their own plain red field locally — genuinely overlapping authority rather than one flag, so this entry is an honest bridging placeholder.

Separate Al Thani sheikhdom (post-1868 treaty)

SheikhdomMuhammad bin ThaniSunni Islam
Ruling house:House of Al Thani

Who & why: after a Bahraini naval attack on Doha in 1867 sparks a British intervention to restore order, Britain brokers a settlement in 1868 that recognizes Muhammad bin Thani and his son Jassim as Qatar's own separate rulers for the first time, formally distinct from Bahrain's Al Khalifa — the founding moment of the Al Thani dynasty's own recognized sheikhdom.

What it represents: the plain red flag, shared across this whole coast, continues locally, now flown for a sheikhdom recognized as separate from Bahrain rather than as part of its orbit.

Qatar within Bahrain's Al Khalifa orbit

Loosely-claimed dependencyAhmed al Fateh Al KhalifaSunni Islam
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Al Thani & other Najdi tribes (18th c.)

Who & why: after the Al Khalifa tribe conquers Bahrain in 1783, it also claims loose overlordship over Qatar's own coastal towns, including Zubarah, without ever governing the peninsula directly or continuously — a real but thin claim that Qatar's own Al Thani family gradually chips away at over the following decades before Britain formally ends it in 1868.

What it represents: Bahrain's own plain red flag stands in for this loosely-claimed period, since no distinctly Qatari banner is attested separate from Bahrain's own during these decades.

c. 700 – 1783

Fragmented tribal coast

Fragmented tribal settlementsNo single ruler documentedSunni Islam (from 7th c.)

Who & why: for most of this long span, the Qatar peninsula is home to small fishing and pearling settlements under scattered local tribal leadership, without a unified ruling family of its own. Islam arrives in the 7th century, and the peninsula passes through the outer orbit of various regional powers, including periods of Persian and, later, Bahraini Al Khalifa influence, without any single dynasty governing it continuously before 1783.

What it represents: no flag in the modern territorial sense survives from this era; local tribal groups used banners and insignia rather than fixed flags, and none can be attributed to Qatar specifically with confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 1st – 7th century CE

Pre-Islamic Qatar (Beth Qatraye)

Fragmented / regional peripheryNo single ruler documentedChurch of the East & Arabian paganism
Migration & conquest:Within the orbit of: Sasanian Persian Empire

Who & why: centuries before Islam's arrival, the Qatar peninsula — recorded by the Roman geographer Pliny the Elder in the 1st century CE as home to the "Catharrei" — sits within the outer trading and political orbit of Mesopotamian and Persian powers, culminating in the Sasanian Empire's control of the Gulf's Arab coast from the 3rd century CE onward. The region, together with neighboring Bahrain, forms part of Beth Qatraye ("region of the Qataris"), a notable ecclesiastical province of the Church of the East, whose several attested bishops appear in synodal records between the 5th and 7th centuries alongside a majority population that otherwise practiced pre-Islamic Arabian paganism. Islam reaches the peninsula in the 7th century, ending Beth Qatraye's Christian institutional presence and beginning the era covered in the next entry.

What it represents: no flag in the modern sense survives from this era; shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span overlaps the height of the Roman and then Byzantine Empire's Mediterranean dominance, the flourishing of the Sasanian Persian Empire as Byzantium's great rival — including the building of the Taq Kasra palace at Ctesiphon, one of the largest brick vaults ever constructed — and the rapid rise of Islam itself from the 610s onward under Muhammad, a set of far larger neighboring civilizations whose religious and political currents each washed successively over this small, thinly populated Gulf peninsula.
