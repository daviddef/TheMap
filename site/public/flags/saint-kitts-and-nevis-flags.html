Flags of Saint Kitts and Nevis

Two small volcanic islands separated by a narrow strait called The Narrows carry an outsized weight of firsts: Saint Kitts hosted the first permanent English colony anywhere in the Caribbean in 1623, and the first permanent French colony in the region followed just two years later, in 1625 — the beachhead from which both empires fanned out across the rest of the West Indies. Today the federation the two islands share is the smallest sovereign state in the Americas by both area and population, and it was the last former British Caribbean territory to become independent, in 1983.

Six flags trace that arc: an Igneri farming settlement centuries before European contact, a pre-contact Kalinago era with nothing securely documented, a genuinely messy century of divided Anglo-French rule on Kitts while Nevis stayed quietly English throughout, a united British colony built on sugar and slavery, a late-colonial and associated-state period complicated by an attached (and eventually estranged) Anguilla, and finally the independent federation's own diagonal flag of green, black, and red.

Scope of this page: the two-island federation of Saint Kitts and Nevis, c. 800–present, six entries. Anguilla was administratively attached to this colony from 1882, rebelled against rule from Basseterre in 1967, and formally separated as its own British dependency in 1980 — a change to the colony's composition, not to Saint Kitts-Nevis's own flag, so it's noted in prose below rather than given its own card. Two islands, one that was really split

Saint Kitts and Nevis's own real two-island border, traced whole — The Narrows, the strait between them, is real water, not a schematic line. Press play, or drag the slider, to watch the one genuine internal partition in this federation's history: Saint Kitts itself, divided between English and French settlers for ninety years, while Nevis stayed continuously English throughout.

Nevis was never divided; Saint Kitts was. From 1623/1625 to 1713, Saint Kitts was a real, repeatedly re-fought Anglo-French condominium — the two powers partitioned and re-partitioned the same island across the century's colonial wars. Nevis, settled separately and purely English from 1628, took no part in that division. The border between the two islands below only shows a color difference where that was genuinely true: from 1713 onward, one crown held both, and the line dissolves for good.

Independent Federation of Saint Kitts and Nevis

Independent stateKennedy SimmondsNo established church
Migration & conquest:Immigration: citizenship-by-investment program (1984–)
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: independence from Britain arrives on 19 September 1983 — the last former British Caribbean colony to become independent, and the smallest sovereign country in the Americas by both area and population. Anguilla, attached to the colony since 1882, is not part of the new federation: it broke away in the 1967 Anguillan Revolution and was formally separated as its own British dependency in 1980, three years before Saint Kitts and Nevis itself became independent without it.

What it represents: a diagonal band split green (upper hoist) and red (lower fly), divided by a yellow-bordered black diagonal band running from the lower hoist to the upper fly. Two white five-pointed stars on the black band stand for hope and liberty, one for each island.

British Saint Kitts-Nevis(-Anguilla), late colony and associated state

Associated statehood / late colonyRobert BradshawNo established church
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to Britain (Windrush era, 1950s–60s)
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: Anguilla is administratively attached to the Saint Kitts-Nevis colony in 1882. On 27 February 1967 the grouping becomes a West Indies Associated State, with full internal self-government while Britain keeps defense and foreign affairs. Anguillans, resentful of rule from Basseterre, revolt within months in the 1967 "Anguillan Revolution" and govern themselves outside the union from then on; a brief British paratrooper landing in 1969 and prolonged negotiation lead to Anguilla's formal separation as its own British dependency in 1980, three years before Saint Kitts and Nevis becomes independent without it.

What it represents: the colonial and associated-state Blue Ensign — a dark blue field with the Union Jack in the canton and the territory's badge on the fly — flown throughout this span.

British Saint Kitts and Nevis (united British rule)

British colonyNo single ruler documentedAnglican (established)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: enslaved Africans for sugar plantations (18th c.)Emigration: to the Panama Canal (1904–14)
Ruling house:House of Hanover

Who & why: the 1713 Treaty of Utrecht, ending the War of the Spanish Succession, cedes the French-held portion of Saint Kitts to Britain, giving Britain sole control of the whole island for the first time; Nevis had already been continuously English/British since its own 1628 settlement, administered alongside Kitts throughout. A major sugar-plantation slave economy dominates the colony for well over a century, with emancipation coming in 1834 (full freedom in 1838); membership in the short-lived West Indies Federation (1958–62) falls late in this span, just before the shift into associated statehood.

What it represents: the British colonial Blue Ensign with a Saint Kitts-Nevis badge, the standard pattern flown across Britain's Caribbean colonies through this period.

Divided Anglo-French Saint Kitts, with English Nevis

Anglo-French division (Kitts) / English NevisSir Thomas WarnerAnglican & Roman Catholic (divided)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: English & French colonists (1623–28)Immigration: enslaved Africans (from mid-1600s)
Ruling house:House of Stuart

Who & why: Sir Thomas Warner establishes the first permanent English colony anywhere in the Caribbean at Saint Kitts in 1623. French settlers under Pierre Belain d'Esnambuc arrive just two years later, in 1625, and the island is divided into English and French zones — a genuine, distinctive "condominium" arrangement, with a brief joint English-French massacre of the indigenous Kalinago population at Bloody Point in 1626 uniting the two colonial rivals against a common target. The two powers then repeatedly fought over and re-partitioned Kitts across the 17th century's colonial wars, right up until the 1713 Treaty of Utrecht finally gives the whole island to Britain. Nevis, settled separately by the English from Saint Kitts in 1628, stays continuously English/British throughout this entire span and is never divided.

What it represents: because Nevis alone was unambiguously English the whole time, the English/British colonial ensign is shown here as the flag of record for this bridging span, rather than inventing false precision about Kitts's repeatedly re-partitioned and re-fought Anglo-French boundary.

c. 1493 – 1623

Kalinago Saint Kitts and Nevis

Kalinago / pre-colonialNo single ruler documentedKalinago spiritual tradition

Who & why: Columbus sights both islands in 1493, naming Saint Kitts "San Cristóbal" (later reinterpreted in English and French use as "Saint Christopher"/"Saint-Christophe," shortened to "Kitts") and Nevis for "Nuestra Señora de las Nieves" (Our Lady of the Snows, for the cloud that habitually wreathes its central peak). Neither name brings settlement: both islands remain under Kalinago control for well over a century, serving as a significant Kalinago population center in the Lesser Antilles before the first English and French colonists arrive in 1623–25.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-contact era; the Kalinago did not use fixed territorial banners in the European heraldic sense. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Igneri (Arawak) Saint Kitts and Nevis, before the Kalinago

Igneri / pre-KalinagoNo single ruler documentedIgneri spiritual tradition
Migration & conquest:Displaced by: Kalinago migration (c. 1300)

Who & why: centuries before Columbus's 1493 sighting, Saint Kitts and Nevis are already settled by the Igneri, an Arawak-speaking farming and pottery-making people whose ancestors, carrying the Saladoid ceramic tradition, migrated up the Lesser Antilles from the Orinoco basin starting around 500 BCE and reached the Leeward Islands by roughly the 8th century CE. Around 1300, Kalinago peoples moving up the same island chain displace or absorb the Igneri population, establishing the Kalinago presence that greets, and later resists, the first English and French colonists in 1623–25.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-contact era; the Igneri, like the Kalinago after them, did not use fixed territorial banners in the European heraldic sense. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the Viking Age's own overseas voyages, including brief Norse settlement in Newfoundland around 1000 CE, the height of Classic Maya city-states in the Yucatan before their 9th-century collapse, the completion of Angkor Wat in Cambodia (early 12th century), and the Islamic Golden Age's House of Wisdom in Baghdad — a reminder that Caribbean, European, Mesoamerican, and Southeast Asian societies were each developing on entirely separate, unconnected tracks at the same moment.
