Flags of Singapore

From the 14th-century Malay port-kingdom of Singapura, documented in both the Malay Annals and Yuan-dynasty Chinese trade accounts, through its fall and half a millennium of nominal Malacca and Johor sultanate overlordship, to a trading post founded by Stamford Raffles for the British East India Company in 1819, over a century of colonial growth into one of the world's great ports, brutal Japanese wartime occupation, a brief and uneasy merger into Malaysia, and full independence in 1965 — six entries spanning more than seven centuries.

Singapore's own crescent-and-five-stars flag, adopted in 1959 for internal self-government, has flown continuously since — through the Malaysia merger, the 1965 separation, and to the present day — making it, unusually for the region, a flag whose design outlasted the very political union it was created to join.

Scope of this page: the island and territory of present-day Singapore, c. 1299–present, six political entries. Singapura's 14th-century existence is genuinely documented by both the Malay Annals and independent Chinese Yuan-dynasty trade accounts, and by archaeological finds at Fort Canning, so it is included as a real starting point rather than treated as unattested; by the time of Raffles's 1819 treaty the island had long been a sparsely settled part of the wider Johor Sultanate's nominal territory rather than a distinct polity in its own right. Republic of Singapore

Republic, independentLee Kuan YewSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: foreign work-permit labor (1970s–)

Who & why: Singapore separates from Malaysia on 9 August 1965, after two years of tension over economic policy and communal politics, becoming fully independent, an outcome its first Prime Minister Lee Kuan Yew famously described as unwanted at the time. The city-state goes on to build one of the world's most rapid economic transformations, from a low-income port economy to a global financial and trade hub within a single generation.

What it represents: the same 1959 flag — a white crescent moon for a young nation on the rise and five stars for democracy, peace, progress, justice, and equality — simply carries over unchanged into full sovereignty.

State of Singapore within Malaysia

State within federationLee Kuan YewIslam (Malaysian federal religion; Singapore secular internally)

Who & why: Singapore joins the new Federation of Malaysia on 16 September 1963 alongside Sabah and Sarawak, seeking economic security and an end to colonial rule through union with the peninsula. Political friction between Singapore's largely Chinese, PAP-led government and the federal government in Kuala Lumpur escalates quickly, including race riots in 1964, and the Malaysian Parliament votes to expel Singapore in August 1965.

What it represents: Singapore retains its own 1959 flag as a constituent state's flag within the federation, flown alongside the new Malaysian federal flag.

British Crown Colony of Singapore

Colonial (Britain)Franklin GimsonChurch of England (colonial administration)
Ruling house:House of Windsor

Who & why: Britain separates Singapore from the Straits Settlements to govern it as its own crown colony after reoccupation in 1946, and gradually extends self-government — a fully elected legislature by 1955, full internal self-rule under Lee Kuan Yew's People's Action Party from 1959 — while Britain retains control of defense and foreign affairs until the 1963 merger into Malaysia.

What it represents: the British colonial Union flag, flown as the territory's own ensign through most of this period, before Singapore's own 1959 flag is adopted for self-government.

Japanese occupation (Syonan-to)

Occupation (Japan)Tomoyuki YamashitaState Shinto (Japan's religion; not imposed locally)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Japan (1942–45)

Who & why: Japan takes Singapore on 15 February 1942, one of Britain's most significant military defeats of the war, and renames it Syonan-to ("Light of the South"). Occupation is marked by harsh treatment of the population, including the Sook Ching massacre targeting the ethnic Chinese community, before British forces retake the island following Japan's August 1945 surrender.

What it represents: the Japanese naval and military ensign, flown over the occupied island; no distinct Singaporean flag flew during this period.

British trading post and Straits Settlements

Colonial (Britain)Stamford RafflesChurch of England (colonial administration)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Chinese, Indian & Malay settlers (19th–early 20th c.)
Ruling house:House of HanoverHouse of Windsor

Who & why: Stamford Raffles, acting for the British East India Company, signs a treaty with the local Johor-aligned rulers in January 1819 to establish a trading post on the island, quickly attracting merchants and migrants from across the region and beyond. Singapore joins Penang and Malacca as the Straits Settlements in 1826, becomes a British crown colony directly in 1867, and grows over the following century into one of the world's busiest ports.

What it represents: the British colonial Union flag, flown as the settlement's own ensign throughout, with no distinct Singaporean symbol yet in use.

c. 1299 – 1819

Kingdom of Singapura & Malacca/Johor Sultanate Rule

Kingdom, then vassal/tributarySang Nila Utama (traditional founder)Hindu-Buddhist, then Islam (from early 15th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquest by: Majapahit & Ayutthaya-aligned forces (14th c.)Conquest of Malacca by: Portugal (1511)

Who & why: the Malay Annals credit Sang Nila Utama with founding Singapura around 1299, and Chinese trade accounts from the 1330s (referring to the settlement as Temasek, or by the strait name Long Ya Men) independently confirm a real, functioning port-city on the island by the 14th century, a finding since supported by archaeological digs at Fort Canning. Majapahit and Siamese-aligned attacks weaken the kingdom across the 14th century; its last king, Parameswara, flees around 1396–98 to found the Sultanate of Malacca, which absorbs Singapura's remaining population and territory. After Portugal conquers Malacca in 1511, the deposed royal line re-establishes itself as the Johor Sultanate, under whose loose, largely nominal authority the sparsely settled island remains until Stamford Raffles signs his 1819 treaty with the Temenggong and Sultan Hussein Shah of Johor.

What it represents: no flag survives, or was ever plausibly created in a modern sense, for Singapura, Malacca's brief hold on the island, or the Johor Sultanate's centuries of nominal overlordship; contemporary Malay states used royal regalia and court banners rather than fixed national flags. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Singapura's rise and fall runs alongside the height of the Hindu-Buddhist Majapahit Empire across the Indonesian archipelago, Yuan- and early Ming-dynasty Chinese maritime trade through the Strait of Malacca (including Zheng He's treasure-fleet voyages in the early 15th century), and, by the time of the Johor Sultanate, the 1453 fall of Constantinople and Portugal's own arrival in Southeast Asia — the same Portuguese expansion that ends Malaccan rule over the island in 1511.
