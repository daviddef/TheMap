Flags of Somalia

The Horn's documented history stretches back to the Land of Punt, a trading partner named in Egyptian inscriptions from around 2500 BCE and vividly recorded in Queen Hatshepsut's own 15th-century-BCE expedition accounts, though no single Somali state existed before colonization: the intervening centuries and the medieval era are one of clan-based sultanates and trading city-states, from the Sultanate of Adal's wars with Christian Ethiopia to the later Ajuran and clan sultanates further south. Colonization split the Somali-speaking coast between Britain and Italy, yet independence reunited it almost instantly — British and Italian Somaliland became sovereign within days of each other in 1960 and merged immediately into one Somali Republic, one of the fastest unifications in this whole atlas.

That unity did not last in practice: a 1969 coup installed a socialist dictatorship, and the central government's 1991 collapse left Somalia without a single, fully effective authority for decades. Its sky-blue flag, adopted in 1954, has flown through all of it.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Somalia, c. 2500 BCE–present, six political entries. The Land of Punt is included as a genuinely documented ancient trading partner of Egypt, though its exact borders are debated and the record goes silent for centuries afterward before the medieval Somali sultanates emerge. British and Italian Somaliland flew genuinely different flags under genuinely different colonial administrations, so the map below traces that real 1884–1960 split; the union moment that erased it gets its own entry. Two colonial territories, briefly one country, then a de facto split again

Somalia's own real border, split into the two pieces that were, for over seven decades, governed by entirely different colonial powers: British Somaliland in the north, and Italian-administered Somalia (later a UN Trust Territory) in the south. The same line reappears after 1991, when the north's territory declares itself the unrecognized Republic of Somaliland while the south continues under Mogadishu's various governments. The line below only solidifies where the two pieces were actually governed apart. Before 1884 there was no such border — no unified Somali state to split. It dissolves the moment the two territories unite, on 1 July 1960, and only resolidifies once more in 1991: the former British Somaliland Protectorate's territory declares itself the independent Republic of Somaliland that year and has functioned as a de facto separate administration within those same 1960 borders ever since. Drawing that line here is a statement about who exercises actual control on the ground, not a statement on international recognition — Somaliland has never been recognized by any UN member state, and Somalia's own federal government continues to claim the whole country, north and south alike.

Real traced borders for both former territories (Natural Earth 10m); the internal line is schematic where it runs through unmarked desert, not a surveyed frontier. From 1991 onward it marks Somaliland's self-declared, unrecognized de facto boundary, not an international frontier.

Somali Democratic Republic → state collapse & federal republic

Civil war / state collapseMohamed Siad BarreSunni Islam (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: refugees fleeing civil war (1991–)

Who & why: General Mohamed Siad Barre seizes power in a 1969 coup and declares the Somali Democratic Republic, ruling as a socialist one-party dictatorship for two decades while pursuing (and losing, against Ethiopia in 1977-78) the pan-Somali dream the flag's own star alludes to. Barre's government collapses in January 1991 amid civil war and clan-based armed opposition, and Somalia has had no single fully effective central government since, cycling through transitional administrations, the breakaway (unrecognized) Somaliland republic in the north, and today's Federal Government of Somalia, established in 2012 and still working to extend real authority nationwide.

What it represents: the same 1954 flag continues in use throughout, regardless of who holds power — light blue for the sky, echoing the blue of the United Nations flag, and a white five-pointed star whose five points represent the five historically Somali-inhabited territories that pan-Somali nationalism hoped to one day unite: British and Italian Somaliland, French Somaliland (Djibouti), the Ogaden in Ethiopia, and Kenya's Northern Frontier District.

Somali Republic (union)

Union / FederationAden Abdullah Osman DaarSunni Islam (state religion)

Who & why: the British Somaliland Protectorate becomes independent on 26 June 1960, and the Italian-administered UN Trust Territory of Somalia follows just five days later on 1 July 1960 — the two unite immediately as the Somali Republic, a genuinely fast and distinctive unification among this atlas's country pages, achieved within days rather than years. Aden Abdullah Osman Daar becomes the new republic's first president. The union is not without friction — the two territories carry different colonial legal, administrative, and linguistic legacies (English/Italian, common law/civil law) that persist for years — but the merger itself holds until Barre's 1969 coup changes the state's character entirely.

What it represents: the same sky-blue flag with its white star, first adopted by the UN Trust Territory of Somalia in 1954 and carried forward as the flag of the whole united republic from the moment of union in 1960.

British Somaliland Protectorate & Italian Somaliland

Colonial ruleNo single ruler documentedNo official state religion (colonial administrations)
Ruling house:House of HanoverHouse of WindsorHouse of Savoy

Who & why: Britain declares a protectorate over the northern Somali coast from 1884-87, mainly to secure a provisioning route for its colony at Aden, while Italy establishes colonial control over the southern and central coast from the late 1880s-90s, formalized as Italian Somaliland by the early 1900s. Italian Somaliland becomes a UN Trust Territory under continued Italian administration from 1950, with independence promised within a decade — a distinctive postwar arrangement. Nationalist movements in both territories press for independence through the 1950s, timed so that both territories can become sovereign within days of each other in 1960 and unite immediately.

What it represents: the standard British colonial ensign pattern in the north; the Italian tricolor pattern (with a colonial badge, simplified here) in the south — two separate colonial administrations with no shared flag, reflecting the genuinely divided rule that independence and union would soon erase.

Sultanate of Adal, Ajuran Sultanate & successor clan sultanates

Kingdom / Sultanate, independentAhmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ("Gurey")Sunni Islam (state religion)
Ruling house:House of Walashma (Adal)Garen dynasty (Ajuran)

Who & why: no single unified Somali state exists in this period; instead, real, substantial Muslim sultanates rise and compete across the Horn. The Sultanate of Adal, based around the port of Zeila, reaches its height in the 16th century under Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ("Gurey"), who leads a prolonged and nearly successful war against the Christian Ethiopian empire. Further south and inland, the Ajuran Sultanate dominates much of south-central Somalia from roughly the 13th to 17th centuries, known for extensive irrigation and fortification works. Both eventually fragment into smaller successor sultanates — Geledi, Majeerteen, Hobyo, and others — that persist into the colonial era of the late 19th century.

What it represents: no flag survives with confidence for Adal, Ajuran, or their successor sultanates; this entry is shown honestly without an invented banner, labeled instead with the era's dominant polities.

Land of Punt

Trading partner, location debatedAti, Queen of Punt (fl. c. 1470 BCE)Unknown (no indigenous record survives)
Migration & conquest:Trade contact: Egypt (Fifth Dynasty–New Kingdom)

Who & why: Punt is one of ancient Egypt's longest-running trading partners, first mentioned in inscriptions from the Fifth Dynasty around 2500 BCE and recurring across Egyptian records for nearly two millennia, most vividly in Queen Hatshepsut's own account of her expedition around 1470 BCE, whose temple reliefs at Deir el-Bahri depict Puntite houses on stilts, its king Parahu, and its queen Ati by name. No Puntite text or artifact independently confirms these accounts, and Punt's precise location is still debated among historians, but most place it on or near the Somali coast and the wider Horn of Africa, making it the earliest documented trading partner associated with this territory. Egyptian references to Punt continue sporadically into the first millennium BCE before fading from the record; what happens in the intervening centuries before the rise of the early Islamic-era coastal trading towns is undocumented.

What it represents: no flag or banner survives, or is described in any Egyptian account, for Punt; what is known comes entirely from Egyptian reliefs and inscriptions describing goods, tribute, and named rulers, not any Puntite emblem or standard. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Punt's earliest documented contact with Egypt falls within the era of Old Kingdom pyramid-building (the Great Pyramid of Giza is completed c. 2560 BCE), and Hatshepsut's own famous expedition runs alongside the height of the Egyptian New Kingdom and the wider Bronze Age world of Babylon, Minoan Crete, and Mycenaean Greece — all trading civilizations whose own goods moved through networks touching the same Red Sea and Indian Ocean routes that carried Puntite frankincense to the pharaohs.
