Flags of South Asia

Eight countries across a subcontinent that holds some of the world's oldest continuous civilizations. The Indus Valley Civilization arose here around 3300 BCE, one of the earliest urban cultures anywhere, and over the millennia that followed the region produced major, well-documented empires: the Maurya Empire, which under Ashoka in the third century BCE controlled most of the subcontinent before he embraced Buddhism and non-violence; the classical Gupta Empire; the Delhi Sultanate; and the Mughal Empire, whose emperors built the Taj Mahal and ruled from the 1500s into the 1800s.

The British East India Company gradually displaced Mughal authority from the mid-1700s, and after the 1857 rebellion the British Crown took direct control, formalizing the British Raj in 1858 over most of the subcontinent. Nepal and Bhutan, the Himalayan kingdoms, were never fully colonized and remained independent through this period. Sri Lanka (as Ceylon) and the Maldives followed separate British colonial tracks. Afghanistan, further west, spent the nineteenth century repelling British invasions during the "Great Game" rivalry with Russia and retained its independence.

In August 1947, British India was partitioned into two independent states, India and Pakistan, divided largely along religious lines. Partition triggered one of the largest mass migrations in human history, as some fourteen million people crossed the new borders, and it was accompanied by widespread communal violence with an estimated death toll ranging from several hundred thousand to over a million. Pakistan itself was founded as two wings separated by a thousand miles of Indian territory; in 1971, after a brutal crackdown and a nine-month war, East Pakistan won its independence as Bangladesh. Eight eras, one map

A real, traced map — actual coastlines and borders, not schematic shapes, built from the same public boundary data used across the atlas. China, Myanmar, Central Asia, and Iran are shown faint for orientation only; the Maldives, a chain of coral atolls far to the southwest, is shown as an inset, not to scale. Drag the slider or press play; each snapshot simplifies a messier reality into the dominant power of the moment.

Borders here are data, not decoration. A line between two regions only draws solid when they were actually governed separately. When they weren't — Pakistan and India both under the Mughals, say — the line dissolves and their names merge into whoever actually ruled them both. Sri Lanka and the Maldives never share a border line with anyone on this map: both were islands administered on their own track throughout, including through the colonial period, when Ceylon was a separate Crown Colony rather than part of British India.
A note on Kashmir. The 1947 Partition left the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir's accession disputed between India and Pakistan; a 1947–48 war ended in a ceasefire line that still divides the region today. This map traces the current de facto line of control for the "Partition," "1971," and "Present" snapshots only, as background geography — it is not a statement about who the territory should belong to, and the dispute itself is described here only as a well-documented, still-unresolved fact.

About this hub

The map above traces eight snapshots across roughly 2,250 years using real, publicly sourced coastline and border data, simplified for legibility. China, Myanmar, Central Asia (Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan), and Iran are shown only as faint outlines for geographic orientation — they aren't tracked as regions in their own right here. The Maldives, a chain of low coral atolls roughly 700 km southwest of Sri Lanka, is shown as a small inset rather than at its true, far-off position, since the map's frame is built around the mainland subcontinent.

Kashmir, the Himalayan region at the northern edge of the former British Raj, remains a territorial dispute between India and Pakistan, administered in parts by each since a 1947–48 war shortly after independence; it is unresolved to this day. The map traces the current de facto line for orientation only, as described in the note above the map, and does not take a position on the dispute's resolution.

Colors on the country cards above are drawn from each country's current national flag, not a historical palette, since no per-country flag lineage exists yet on this site. Once a country's own page publishes, its card here will link out to a full flag timeline, matching the depth of the map above.
