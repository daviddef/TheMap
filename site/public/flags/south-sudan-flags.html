Flags of South Sudan

South Sudan is the world's newest widely-recognized sovereign state, born in 2011 after more than fifty years of near-continuous conflict with the government in Khartoum — two full civil wars, from 1955 to 1972 and again from 1983 to 2005, fought over the south's place within a unified Sudan its Nilotic peoples had never really chosen. A 2011 referendum settled the question about as decisively as a vote can: roughly 98 percent in favor of independence.

Its flag, adopted at that independence, is built directly from the flag of the SPLM, the movement that led the south's fight for self-determination for over two decades.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day South Sudan, Ottoman-Egyptian era–present, five political entries. The country's own internal civil war since 2013 is noted briefly in the final entry rather than given a separate one, since the page's core scope is the flag lineage through the 2011 independence moment. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Republic of South Sudan

Republic, independentSalva KiirSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: southern returnees from SudanEmigration: refugees fleeing the 2013– civil war

Who & why: the 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement, ending the Second Sudanese Civil War, grants the south six years of autonomy and a promised independence referendum; that vote, held in January 2011, returns an overwhelming result — roughly 98 percent in favor — and South Sudan becomes independent on 9 July 2011 under President Salva Kiir, the SPLM's leader since John Garang's death in 2005. It is the world's newest widely-recognized sovereign state. Independence does not bring lasting internal peace: a political split within the SPLM erupts into a further civil war from December 2013, formally ended by a 2018 peace agreement whose implementation has continued unevenly since.

What it represents: black for the people, red for the blood shed in the long struggle, green for the land, separated by white fimbriation for peace, with a blue hoist triangle for the Nile and a gold five-pointed star for the unity of South Sudan's peoples — adapted directly from the flag flown by the SPLM/SPLA throughout the second civil war.

1956 – 2011

South within independent Sudan (two civil wars)

Civil war / state collapseJohn GarangIslam (Sharia imposed by Khartoum)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: refugees from decades of civil warEmigration: "Lost Boys" resettled to the United States

Who & why: Sudan becomes independent from Britain and Egypt on 1 January 1956, incorporating the south into a single unified state, but southern fears of northern political and cultural domination trigger the First Sudanese Civil War, which actually begins in 1955, just before independence itself, and runs until 1972, when the Addis Ababa Agreement grants the south regional autonomy. That autonomy is revoked in 1983 when President Nimeiry imposes Sharia law nationwide, triggering the Second Sudanese Civil War, fought largely by the SPLA/SPLM under John Garang against Khartoum's government for the next twenty-two years — one of Africa's longest and deadliest conflicts — ended by the 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement, which sets the terms for the 2011 independence referendum.

What it represents: independent Sudan's own national flag (red-white-black horizontal bands with a green hoist triangle, adopted 1970) is the flag under which the south nominally sits throughout this period, though real effective control of much of the south passes back and forth with the fighting; the SPLM's own separate flag, adopted from the 1980s, is the direct ancestor of independent South Sudan's flag.

Anglo-Egyptian Sudan

Colonial ruleLord Kitchener (founding Governor-General)No official state religion (Christian missions encouraged)
Ruling house:House of Muhammad Ali (Egypt)House of Windsor

Who & why: after defeating the Mahdist State, Britain and Egypt establish the Anglo-Egyptian Condominium over Sudan in 1899 — nominally joint rule, in practice administered mainly by Britain. Colonial policy treats the south differently from the Arabic-speaking, Muslim-majority north for much of this period (the "Southern Policy," restricting northern administrative and missionary influence in southern provinces until the early 1940s), a distinction that later feeds directly into southern anxieties about union with the north at independence.

What it represents: the Union Flag, flown as the dominant administering power's own flag over the Condominium, alongside the Egyptian flag in its nominal co-sovereign role; no distinct southern Sudanese flag existed in this period.

c. 1821 – 1899

Ottoman-Egyptian expansion & the Mahdist State

Colonial ruleMuhammad Ahmad (the Mahdi)Sunni Islam (Mahdist / Ottoman-Egyptian)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: peoples trafficked north by the slave tradeConquered by: Ottoman Egypt, then the Mahdist State
Ruling house:House of Muhammad Ali (Egypt)

Who & why: Ottoman Egypt pushes military expeditions up the Nile from the 1820s, gradually extending nominal control (and a brutal ivory-and-slave trading frontier) into the southern regions, including the era of Anglo-Egyptian officials like Charles Gordon serving as governor in the 1870s-80s. A large-scale religious and political uprising under Muhammad Ahmad, who declares himself the Mahdi, overthrows Egyptian control across most of Sudan from 1881, establishing the Mahdist State, which holds power (including over much of the south) until an Anglo-Egyptian army defeats it in 1898, leading to the Condominium the following year.

What it represents: no single flag with confirmed authority over the southern regions specifically survives from either the Ottoman-Egyptian administration or the Mahdist State; this entry is shown honestly without an invented banner.

Stateless Nilotic peoples of the upper Nile

Fragmented / pre-stateNo single ruler documentedTraditional Nilotic religion (no state establishment)
Ruling house:House of Chollo (Shilluk reth)

Who & why: before Ottoman-Egyptian expansion up the Nile, the region that becomes South Sudan is home to Nilotic peoples — the Dinka, Nuer, Shilluk, and others — organized through cattle-based kinship, age-set, and chiefdom-level structures rather than centralized states. No single authority governs the region as a whole in this period.

What it represents: no securely documented unified flag survives for this era. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
