Flags of Suriname

Suriname's colonial history turns on a single, oddly specific real-estate swap: the 1667 Treaty of Breda, in which the Dutch acquired the colony that would become Dutch Guiana from England — and, in the same treaty, ceded New Amsterdam on the other side of the Atlantic to the English, who renamed it New York. The Netherlands held Suriname as a plantation colony and then an autonomous territory for over three centuries, until full independence arrived on 25 November 1975.

Few countries in South America carry a more genuinely mixed population than Suriname. Dutch plantation slavery brought enslaved Africans until abolition in 1863; the decades after abolition then brought waves of indentured laborers from British India, from Java in the Dutch East Indies, and from China, layering Hindustani, Javanese, and Chinese communities onto the existing Afro-Surinamese, Maroon, and Dutch population — a demographic mix the country's flag itself, with its single unifying star, was explicitly designed to represent.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Suriname, c. 1500s (indigenous and early colonial era) – present, four political entries. No internal governing split exists within Suriname's own modern borders at any point in its history, so unlike some other pages in this atlas, this one carries no internal-partition map. 1975 – present

Republic of Suriname (independent, modern)

RepublicHenck ArronSecular (no official state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the Netherlands around independence (1970s)

Who & why: Suriname becomes independent from the Netherlands on 25 November 1975, and this flag — adopted that same year — has flown unchanged ever since. The country's population, shaped by Dutch, African-Maroon, Hindustani (Indian-descended), Javanese/Indonesian, and Chinese migration and labor history, is genuinely one of the most ethnically diverse in South America.

What it represents: the yellow five-pointed star centered on the red band stands for national unity and the sacrifice of all of Suriname's ethnic groups; the colors represent agriculture (green), justice and the road to independence (red), and the country's progressive future (yellow, via the star).

1954 – 1975

Suriname, autonomous country within the Kingdom of the Netherlands

Autonomous territoryJohan Adolf PengelNo official state religion (multi-faith)
Ruling house:House of Orange-Nassau

Who & why: the 1954 Charter for the Kingdom of the Netherlands grants Suriname, alongside the Netherlands Antilles, autonomous self-governing status while remaining part of the Kingdom under the Dutch crown. In the first years of this period Suriname flies the plain Dutch tricolor, its own distinct flag not yet in use; a dedicated Surinamese flag is adopted in 1959 and flies until full independence two decades later, in 1975.

What it represents: the 1959 flag's white field carries a red oval band enclosing five small stars, each a different color, representing Suriname's main population groups — African, Indian (Hindustani), European (Dutch), Chinese, and Javanese/indigenous — joined by the band as a single nation.

1667 – 1954

Dutch Guiana (Netherlands colony)

ColonyAbraham CrijnssenDutch Reformed Christianity (established)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: enslaved Africans (colonial era)Immigration: Indian, Javanese & Chinese indentured laborers
Ruling house:House of Orange-Nassau

Who & why: the Dutch acquire the colony from England by the 1667 Treaty of Breda — the same treaty in which the Dutch cede New Amsterdam, on the other side of the Atlantic, to England, which renames it New York, a frequently noted historical trade. The Dutch develop Suriname as a plantation colony reliant on enslaved African labor. After the Netherlands abolishes slavery in 1863 (with a mandatory decade-long transition period before full emancipation), indentured laborers are brought from British India, the Dutch East Indies (Java), and China to work the plantations, producing the genuinely diverse population Suriname has today.

What it represents: the plain Dutch tricolor — red, white, and blue horizontal bands — serves as the colonial flag and ensign for the whole of this nearly three-century span; no distinct Surinamese colonial flag is adopted until the transition to autonomy in the 1950s.

c. 1500s – 1667

Indigenous peoples and earlier settlement attempts

No unified polityNo single ruler documentedTraditional Arawak/Carib beliefs (no state establishment)

Who & why: Arawak and Carib peoples inhabit the coastal and interior regions of present-day Suriname for centuries before European colonization, living in autonomous communities with no single overarching authority. English settlers make the first lasting European colonial foothold in the region in the 1650s, before the Dutch acquire the territory outright from England in 1667 by the Treaty of Breda.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from either the indigenous communities of this era or the brief, contested early English and other European settlement attempts; none can be attributed to this territory with confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.
