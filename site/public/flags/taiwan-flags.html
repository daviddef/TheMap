Flags of Taiwan

Taiwan's flag history is short and unusually direct compared to its neighbors: seventeenth-century Dutch and Spanish trading outposts, Qing Chinese annexation, a half-century as a Japanese colony, and then, since 1945, first the returning Republic of China's normal provincial rule and then, from 1949, the ROC central government's own relocation to the island after losing the Chinese Civil War on the mainland — bringing with it the same Blue Sky, White Sun, and a Wholly Red Earth flag it had flown since 1928, still flying today.

Taiwan's political status is a live, disputed question: Taiwan has governed itself since 1949 under the Republic of China's own constitution, with its own elected government, military, and currency, while the People's Republic of China claims Taiwan as its own territory. This page states that dispute as an ongoing, unresolved fact without characterizing either government's claim as correct — see this atlas's China page for the mainland's own separate lineage since 1949.

Scope of this page: the island of Taiwan (and its offshore territories), c. 1624–present, seven political periods. This page covers the Republic of China's flag lineage as it continues on Taiwan after 1949; the People's Republic of China's mainland lineage, and China's own pre-1949 imperial and republican history, are covered on this atlas's separate China page. 1949 – present

Republic of China (on Taiwan)

RepublicChiang Kai-shekNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: mainland Chinese relocation (1949)

Who & why: after losing the Chinese Civil War to the Communist Party on the mainland, Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalist government relocates to Taiwan in 1949, bringing roughly two million mainland refugees and the ROC's full constitutional government with it. Chiang rules under martial law (lifted only in 1987) until his death in 1975; Taiwan then democratizes through the late 1980s and 1990s, holding its first direct presidential election in 1996 and its first peaceful transfer of power between rival parties in 2000. The same flag, unchanged, flies throughout — Taiwan has governed itself continuously since 1949, while the People's Republic of China maintains its own claim to the island.

What it represents: the identical flag adopted by the Nationalist government in 1928 (see China's own page for its origin under Sun Yat-sen's revolutionary movement); no design change accompanies the 1949 relocation, only a change in the territory the flag actually governs.

1945 – 1949

Retrocession to the Republic of China

Republic (province)Chen YiNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Japanese repatriation (1946)Immigration: mainland Chinese administrators & troops (1945–)

Who & why: Japan's defeat in World War II ends its 50-year colonial rule over Taiwan; Republic of China forces accept the Japanese surrender on the island on 25 October 1945, an event Taiwan still marks as Retrocession Day. The following years bring friction between mainland-appointed administrators and the local population, culminating in the February 1947 uprising and crackdown (the 228 Incident) — a traumatic episode in Taiwan's own history, stated here factually. The ROC's full national government then relocates to the island in 1949 as the mainland falls to the Communists.

What it represents: the same Blue Sky, White Sun, and a Wholly Red Earth flag already flown by the Republic of China since 1928, now raised over Taiwan as an ordinary ROC province for the first time.

1895 – 1945

Japanese colonial Taiwan

Colonial territoryKabayama SukenoriState Shinto
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Japan (1895)Immigration: Japanese settlers (1895–1945)
Ruling house:Imperial House of Japan

Who & why: defeated in the First Sino-Japanese War, Qing China cedes Taiwan to Japan under the April 1895 Treaty of Shimonoseki. Local resistance briefly declares the short-lived Republic of Formosa that same year — it lasts about five months before Japanese forces suppress it. Japan then governs Taiwan as a colony for exactly half a century, building railways, schools, and administrative infrastructure alongside suppressing Taiwanese and Indigenous resistance and pursuing wartime assimilation policy, until its surrender ends colonial rule in 1945.

What it represents: Japan's own Hinomaru sun-disc flag (see this atlas's Japan page for its own history), flown as the flag of the colonizing power rather than any flag of Taiwan's own.

1683 – 1895

Qing Dynasty Taiwan

Imperial territoryKangxi EmperorConfucianism (state orthodoxy), with imperial patronage of Tibetan Buddhism
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Qing Dynasty (1683)Immigration: Han Chinese settlers from Fujian & Guangdong
Ruling house:Qing dynasty (Aisin Gioro)

Who & why: the Qing conquer the Zheng family's Kingdom of Tungning in 1683, formally annexing Taiwan as a prefecture (later, from 1885, a full province) of the Qing Empire. Qing administration remains relatively light for much of this period, concentrated on the western coastal plain, before the island is ceded to Japan in 1895 at the close of the First Sino-Japanese War.

What it represents: the same Qing Yellow Dragon Flag flown across the wider empire (see China's own page for its full history and 1889 formal adoption), extended to Taiwan as ordinary Qing territory rather than any flag distinct to the island.

1661 – 1683

Kingdom of Tungning

Independent kingdomKoxinga (Zheng Chenggong)Confucianism and Chinese folk religion (no distinct establishment)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Ming-loyalist refugees & troops (1661–62)
Ruling house:House of Zheng

Who & why: Ming-loyalist military leader Koxinga (Zheng Chenggong) expels the Dutch from Taiwan in 1662 and establishes a base on the island for continued resistance against the newly established Qing Dynasty on the mainland, styling it a Ming-loyalist regime. His descendants rule Taiwan as a functionally independent kingdom until the Qing conquest of 1683 ends it.

What it represents: no state flag is documented for this brief, militarized regime; shown here with the Ming's own dynastic red-gold color reference, since Tungning styled itself the Ming's loyalist continuation.

1624 – 1662

Dutch (and briefly Spanish) colonial outposts

Colonial trading outpostMartinus SonckDutch Reformed Christianity (VOC-established); Roman Catholic (Spanish outpost)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Han Chinese laborers recruited by the VOC

Who & why: the Dutch East India Company establishes a fortified trading base at Fort Zeelandia in southwestern Taiwan in 1624, the first sustained European colonial presence on the island, used mainly to support trade with China and Japan. Spain separately holds a smaller, shorter-lived northern outpost around Keelung and Tamsui from 1626 until the Dutch expel it in 1642. Both are aimed at coastal trade rather than governing the island's Indigenous Austronesian population, which the outposts never fully control. Koxinga's 1662 conquest ends Dutch rule.

What it represents: the Dutch Prince's Flag (orange-white-blue, the forerunner of today's Dutch tricolor, shown here in its later red-white-blue civil form since orange pigment historically faded and darkened toward red) flown over the VOC's fort; before this era, Taiwan's Indigenous Austronesian communities had no single unified flag or polity of their own to record.

Indigenous Austronesian Taiwan

Fragmented, no single polityNo single ruler documentedTraditional Austronesian beliefs (no state establishment)

Who & why: before sustained European contact, Taiwan is home to numerous distinct Indigenous Austronesian peoples — the island is now understood as one likely origin point for the broader Austronesian language family that spread across the Pacific and Indian Oceans — organized into separate villages and tribal groups rather than any single island-wide government.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or unified territorial symbol survives from this era; shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Reading the tags

Fragmented / colonial outpost — no single sovereign island-wide government
Kingdom / imperial territory — ruled under a monarch or emperor
Republic — sovereign or self-governing under a republican constitution
Leader — the named ruler most associated with the era's start
State religion — the official/established faith, not the population's
