Flags of Tannu Tuva

A real state in the Sayan Mountains, on the modern border of south Siberia and Mongolia, nominally independent from 1921 as the Tuvan People's Republic — though under heavy Soviet influence for its entire existence. Recognized only by the USSR and Mongolia, and by almost no one else, it survived just over two decades before direct Soviet annexation in 1944 folded it into the USSR as the Tuvan Autonomous Oblast. Today it is the Tuva Republic, a federal subject of Russia, with no path back to separate statehood. The same upper Yenisei basin carried two millennia of earlier steppe history before any of that — Xiongnu, Turkic, and Mongol-era rule, and its own Yenisei Kirghiz Khaganate attested by inscriptions still standing in Tuva today.

Tuva briefly entered American pop culture in the 1970s–80s as physicist Richard Feynman's obsession — he spent years trying, and ultimately failing, to visit before his death — a reminder of just how obscure and cut-off this vanished republic became during the Cold War.

Scope of this page: the Sayan Mountains region straddling today's Tuva Republic (Russia) and northwestern Mongolia, c. 200 BCE–present, five political stages. Documentation for Tuvan state flags is thinner and less consistent than for better-recorded European states; specifics below are informed reconstructions drawing on the fullest available descriptions, not verified official specifications. Who & why: in August 1944, the Tuvan People's Republic's own Little Khural (parliament) petitions to join the USSR — a request widely understood to have been engineered by Moscow rather than freely initiated — and the Soviet Union formally absorbs Tuva as an autonomous oblast within the Russian SFSR, later upgraded to an autonomous republic in 1961. It becomes the Tuva Republic on the USSR's 1991 collapse, remaining a federal subject of the Russian Federation, one of the country's poorest and most remote regions.

What it represents: the Soviet tricolor flown through the oblast and republic period, succeeded by modern Russia's own white-blue-red tricolor after 1991 — shown here as the flag Tuva has flown continuously since ceasing to be its own state.

Who & why: in the republic's last full year, its government under Salchak Toka — by now thoroughly a Soviet client, having collectivized herds and suppressed Buddhist lamaseries and traditional Tuvan script on Moscow's model through the 1930s — readopts a flag closer to Soviet republic design conventions, shortly before petitioning for outright absorption. Tuvan People's Republic (early flag)

Nominally independent republicMongush Buyan-BadyrgyTibetan Buddhism (traditional, later suppressed)

Who & why: local revolutionaries, backed by the Red Army, declare the Tannu Tuva People's Republic on 14 August 1921, following the collapse of the region's earlier status under Qing Chinese suzerainty and a brief 1914–19 Russian imperial protectorate (the Uryankhaisky Krai). The USSR recognizes the republic in 1924, and Mongolia in 1926, but almost no other state ever does, leaving Tuva in a genuine diplomatic limbo for its entire existence — nominally sovereign, but entirely dependent on Soviet backing, advisors, and eventually direct political control through the ruling Tuvan People's Revolutionary Party.

What it represents: a blue field with a gold canton carrying old Mongolian-script lettering, adopted around 1926 and flown through the republic's middle decades — shown here as an informed reconstruction using a simplified Latin-letter stand-in for the original script, since a precise, source-verified rendering isn't available.

Who & why: the Uryankhai region (the historical name for what becomes Tuva) is loosely governed as part of Qing China's Outer Mongolian administration for over two centuries before Qing collapse in 1911–12 opens a window for local Tuvan nobles to seek a new protector; Tsarist Russia establishes a formal protectorate over the territory in 1914 as the Uryankhaisky Krai, administered from Russian Siberia until revolution and civil war upend that arrangement by 1918–19, setting the stage for the 1921 republic.

What it represents: no distinct Tuvan territorial flag is documented for this era; the region flew Qing and then Russian imperial banners as a dependency rather than maintaining a flag of its own. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 200 BCE – 1758

Xiongnu, Turkic, and Yenisei Kirghiz-era steppe rule

Fragmented / successive steppe politiesNo single ruler documentedTengrism, with Buddhism spreading from the 13th c.
Migration & conquest:Documented locally by: Old Turkic Yenisei inscriptions (9th–10th c.)Conquered by: Mongol Empire (1210s–20s)

Who & why: for roughly two thousand years before the Qing Empire's mid-18th-century conquest of the region, the upper Yenisei river basin that includes present-day Tuva passes through the orbit of successive Inner Asian steppe powers with no single lasting government of its own: the Xiongnu confederation's sphere of influence, the First and Second Turkic Khaganates, the Uyghur Khaganate, and then the Yenisei Kirghiz Khaganate, whose own runic stone inscriptions survive within Tuva itself. The Mongol Empire absorbs the region in the 1210s–20s, and it passes afterward through Mongol successor states, including the Oirat/Zunghar Khanate, until the Qing dynasty's defeat of the Zunghars brings the region under Qing suzerainty by 1758.

What it represents: no flag or comparable territorial banner survives, or was likely ever created in any modern sense, from any of these successive steppe polities as they relate specifically to the Tuva region; shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the rise and fall of the Han and Tang dynasties in China, the Turkic Khaganates' own rivalry and diplomacy with Byzantium and Sasanian Persia, the rapid 7th-century Arab conquests and spread of Islam, and Genghis Khan's early-13th-century unification of the Mongol steppe that would go on to build the largest contiguous land empire in history.
