Flags of Timor-Leste

From the pre-colonial Wehale kingdom and a centuries-old sandalwood trade documented since the 14th century, through over two and a half centuries as a remote Portuguese colonial outpost, a nine-day declared independence immediately overrun by invasion, twenty-four years of Indonesian occupation marked by significant, well-documented violence, a UN transitional administration, and finally full independence in 2002 — five entries tracing Southeast Asia's youngest nation.

Timor-Leste's flag, first raised for those nine days in 1975, was kept unchanged when independence was finally achieved in 2002 — a deliberate act of continuity, restoring the very same design that had been interrupted rather than replaced.

Scope of this page: the territory of present-day Timor-Leste, documented from the 14th century–present, five political entries. No flag is attested for the island's pre-colonial polities, or for organized Portuguese colonial administration's own earliest decades, so those spans are shown honestly blank rather than invented. The 1975–1999 Indonesian occupation entry opens with the brief 28 November–7 December 1975 proclaimed republic rather than treating it as a separate near-zero-day entry, since the invasion followed within nine days. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

One country, four pieces of land

Timor-Leste's own real border, traced whole — the mainland, the Oecussi enclave (a real exclave of the same country, surrounded by Indonesian West Timor), Atauro island, and tiny Jaco island off the eastern tip. Press play, or drag the slider.

Oecussi is not a rival territory — it's the same country, twice. The 1859 Dutch–Portuguese treaty gave Portugal the eastern two-thirds of the island plus this separate western enclave, and independent Timor-Leste inherited both pieces intact. All four polygons shown here always belong to the same government; there is no internal split to draw.

2002 – present

Democratic Republic of Timor-Leste

Republic, independentXanana GusmãoSecular (no official state religion)

Who & why: Timor-Leste becomes fully independent on 20 May 2002, following the UN transitional administration, with independence hero Xanana Gusmão as its first president. The young country continues to rebuild institutions and infrastructure largely destroyed in the violence surrounding the 1999 referendum, while developing offshore oil and gas revenue as its central economic resource.

What it represents: the same flag first raised on 28 November 1975 is deliberately readopted unchanged at full independence — a black triangle (the dark years of colonization) inside a yellow arrow (traces of colonialism) on red (the struggle for freedom), with a white star for peace and guiding light.

1999 – 2002

UN Transitional Administration (UNTAET)

UN transitional administrationSérgio Vieira de MelloNone (UN administration, secular)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: forced deportation to West Timor (1999)

Who & why: after nearly 80% of East Timorese vote for independence in the UN-supervised 30 August 1999 referendum, pro-Indonesia militias, with the involvement of elements of the Indonesian military, carry out a scorched-earth campaign of violence and destruction; an Australian-led international peacekeeping force restores order within weeks, and the UN Transitional Administration in East Timor assumes full governing authority in October 1999 to rebuild institutions and prepare the territory for independence.

What it represents: the United Nations flag, flown over a territory placed under direct UN administration, a nearly unprecedented step reflecting the scale of the preceding destruction.

1975 – 1999

Proclaimed independence and Indonesian occupation

Occupation (Indonesia)SuhartoNo official state religion (Indonesian Pancasila)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Indonesia (1975 invasion)Immigration: Indonesian transmigrasi settlers

Who & why: as Portugal's colonial administration collapses amid its own 1974 revolution, the pro-independence Fretilin movement unilaterally declares the Democratic Republic of East Timor on 28 November 1975; Indonesia invades just nine days later, on 7 December, and formally annexes the territory as its 27th province in 1976, a claim never recognized by the United Nations. The occupation that follows, lasting until 1999, is marked by significant, well-documented violence, including mass displacement and an estimated death toll in the tens of thousands to over a hundred thousand from conflict, famine, and repression.

What it represents: Indonesia's own national flag, adapted with a small provincial emblem for East Timor as one of its provinces, imposed over a population that had never voted to join it.

c. 1702 – 1975

Portuguese Timor

Colonial (Portugal)António Coelho GuerreiroRoman Catholic (Portuguese state religion)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Japan (1942–45)
Ruling house:House of Braganza

Who & why: Portuguese traders and missionaries establish influence on Timor from the early 1500s, but organized colonial administration only formalizes with the first resident governor's appointment around 1702, developing gradually as a remote and lightly administered outpost centered on sandalwood, and later coffee, trade. An 1859 treaty with the Dutch formally divides the island, fixing Portugal's territory to the eastern half plus the Oecussi enclave. Japan occupies Portuguese Timor from 1942 to 1945 despite Portugal's neutrality in World War II, before Portuguese administration resumes; the colony remains in place until the Carnation Revolution in Lisbon in 1974 prompts Portugal's rapid, disorderly decolonization.

What it represents: the Portuguese colonial ensign, based on the national flag's green-and-red field with the armillary sphere and shield, flown as the territory's own colonial banner.

Wehale and the liurai kingdoms; the sandalwood trade

No flag documentedNo single ruler documentedIndigenous animist belief systems (pre-Catholic)
Migration & conquest:Trade contact: Chinese, Javanese, and Malay sandalwood traders (from 14th c.)

Who & why: centuries before organized Portuguese colonial administration formalizes around 1702, Timor is documented as a source of fragrant sandalwood traded across a wider Indian Ocean and maritime Southeast Asian network — Chinese records note the trade as early as 1349. The island itself has no single government: numerous local liurai chieftaincies rule their own domains, while the Wehale kingdom, centered inland, holds a wide-reaching ritual paramountcy recognized by many of them, a structure described in the earliest Portuguese and Dutch accounts of the island from the 16th century onward. Portuguese traders and missionaries begin visiting from around the 1510s–20s, well before the entry that follows.

What it represents: no flag or comparable unifying territorial symbol is documented for Wehale, the liurai kingdoms, or the island's pre-colonial trade network; shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the sandalwood trade that first brings Timor into written record runs alongside the Majapahit Empire's peak as the dominant maritime power of the Indonesian archipelago and, in the following century, the Ming dynasty's massive Zheng He treasure-fleet voyages across the Indian Ocean — part of the same broad trading world, though no direct Zheng He visit to Timor itself is attested.
