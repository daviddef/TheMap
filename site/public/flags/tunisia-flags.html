Flags of Tunisia

Tunisia's modern flag is a rare case in this atlas: adopted in 1831 under an Ottoman-appointed bey, it survived a half-century as an autonomous regency, seventy-five years as a French protectorate, and independence completely unchanged — the same red field, white disc, and red crescent-and-star flying continuously since. From ancient Carthage and Roman Africa, through early Islamic Kairouan and the Hafsid dynasty, three centuries as the Ottoman Beylik of Tunis, a French protectorate, and finally the birthplace of the 2010–11 Arab Spring, this page traces six eras.

The Tunisian Revolution that began in December 2010, sparked by the self-immolation of street vendor Mohamed Bouazizi and culminating in President Ben Ali's flight in January 2011, is widely credited as the spark that set off the broader Arab Spring across the region.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Tunisia, c. 814 BCE–present, six political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Republic of Tunisia

Republic, independentHabib BourguibaIslam (constitutionally established)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Libyan war refugees (2011)Emigration: Jewish community to Israel & FranceEmigration: irregular migration to Italy (2011–)

Who & why: Tunisia gains independence from France in March 1956, and the monarchy is abolished a year later in favor of a republic under Habib Bourguiba. More than five decades later, Tunisia becomes the birthplace of the Arab Spring: the December 2010–January 2011 Tunisian Revolution, triggered by Mohamed Bouazizi's self-immolation in protest of police harassment, forces President Zine El Abidine Ben Ali to flee and sets off a wave of uprisings across the wider Arab world.

What it represents: the same flag adopted in 1831 — red field, white disc, red crescent and star — continues through independence and the 2011 revolution without any design change, formally codified again in the 2014 post-revolution constitution.

French Protectorate of Tunisia

ProtectorateMuhammad III as-SadiqIslam (bey's establishment, French administration)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: French & Italian settlersOccupied by: Nazi Germany & Fascist Italy (1942–43)
Ruling house:House of Husainid

Who & why: France invades in 1881 and imposes the Treaty of Bardo, establishing a protectorate that leaves the Husainid bey nominally in place as sovereign while French officials hold real authority. Nationalist opposition, led from the 1930s by Bourguiba's Neo Destour party, builds steadily until France grants full independence in March 1956.

What it represents: the bey's own flag, unchanged since 1831, continues to fly as Tunisia's national banner throughout the protectorate, alongside the French Tricolor — the bey remained nominal head of state the entire period.

Beylik of Tunis (autonomous)

Ottoman regencyHusain II BeySunni Islam (Maliki)
Ruling house:House of Husainid

Who & why: Husain II Bey adopts a distinct Tunisian flag in 1831, a mark of the beylik's growing autonomy from Istanbul even as it remains under nominal Ottoman suzerainty. Tunisia is also the first Arab and Muslim state to formally abolish slavery, in 1846, and later issues one of the region's earliest constitutions, in 1861, before mounting debt and European pressure open the door to the 1881 French invasion.

What it represents: the red field, white disc, and red crescent-and-star, said to echo the Ottoman naval ensign while asserting a distinct Tunisian identity — the same design carries forward, unaltered, to the present day.

Beylik of Tunis (early Ottoman regency)

Ottoman regencySinan PashaSunni Islam (Maliki)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Andalusian Muslim refugees (from 1609)Immigration: Livornese Jewish merchants (17th c.–)
Ruling house:House of Husainid

Who & why: Ottoman forces finally secure Tunis in 1574 after decades of contest with Habsburg Spain, ending the Hafsid dynasty and installing an Ottoman-appointed bey. Real power gradually concentrates in the hereditary beylik, especially from the Husainid dynasty's founding in 1705, though the flag remains the plain Ottoman ensign until the 1831 reform.

What it represents: the standard Ottoman red field with white crescent and star, flown across the empire's North African regencies before Tunisia adopted its own distinct design.

Early Islamic dynasties of Ifriqiya

Successive dynastiesIbrahim I ibn al-AghlabSunni Islam (Maliki), with a Shia Ismaili interlude under the Fatimids
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Banu Hilal Arab tribes (from 1050s)

Who & why: the Aghlabid dynasty, ruling from Kairouan from 800, is followed by Fatimid rule (before the Fatimids relocate their capital to Cairo), the Zirids, brief Almohad unification, and finally the Hafsid dynasty from 1229, which presides over a prosperous and largely independent Tunisia until Ottoman conquest in 1574.

What it represents: no single flag can honestly represent nearly eight centuries and several distinct ruling dynasties; heraldic banners of this era are poorly attested. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Ancient Carthage & Roman Africa

Successive civilizationsHannibal BarcaPunic religion, then Roman polytheism, then Christianity (from 2nd c. CE)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Rome (146 BCE)Conquered by: Vandals (439 CE), then Byzantine Empire (533 CE)

Who & why: Phoenician settlers from Tyre traditionally found Carthage around 814 BCE; it grows into the dominant trading power of the western Mediterranean, ruling much of the North African coast, Sicily, and Sardinia until three wars with an expanding Rome — the Punic Wars (264–146 BCE) — end in the city's total destruction in 146 BCE. Rome rebuilds Carthage as the capital of the province of Africa, one of the empire's most valuable grain suppliers, until Vandal invaders seize it in 439 CE; Byzantine forces under Belisarius retake it for Justinian in 533–34 CE, and Byzantine rule persists until the Arab conquest completes in 698 CE, opening the roughly century that precedes the Aghlabid dynasty below.

What it represents: no flag in any modern sense existed anywhere in this era; Carthaginian ships and Roman legions carried standards and devices (Rome's eagle-topped aquila among them) rather than woven national banners. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: Carthage's traditional 814 BCE founding falls in the same broad age as the legendary founding of Rome (753 BCE) and the earliest recorded Greek Olympic Games (776 BCE); the Punic Wars run alongside the Qin dynasty's unification of China and the start of the Great Wall's construction in the 3rd century BCE; and the Vandal-to-Byzantine transition in Carthage overlaps with Emperor Justinian's construction of Constantinople's Hagia Sophia (532–37 CE), completed just a few years after Belisarius's reconquest.
