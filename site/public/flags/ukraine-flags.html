Flags of the Dnieper

From Scythian steppe nomads and ancient Greek Black Sea colonies, through a Varangian-and-Slavic trade-route state that gave three modern nations their common ancestor, a medieval successor kingdom crowned by a papal legate, four centuries pulled between Lithuania, Poland, the Cossack Hetmanate and the Tsars, a chaotic five years of competing governments after empire collapsed in 1918, seventy years inside the Soviet Union broken by a genocidal famine and a brutal foreign occupation — thirteen flags, and a blue-and-yellow bicolor that has now flown, on and off, since 1918.

Ukraine's own flag colors are read two ways, both attested: sky blue over golden wheat fields, or simply the historic arms of Galicia-Volhynia (a blue field, gold devices) recovered as the modern national banner in the last years of the Russian Empire.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Ukraine, c. 700 BCE–present, thirteen political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

Ukraine

Sovereign stateLeonid KravchukSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Russia (Crimea 2014; parts of the east & south from 2022)Emigration: refugees from the 2022 invasion

Who & why: the Verkhovna Rada declares independence on 24 August 1991 as the USSR disintegrates; a referendum on 1 December 1991 returns a decisive 92% in favor, with majorities in every region including Crimea and the industrial east, sealing the Soviet Union's fate three weeks before its formal dissolution. Ukraine's internationally recognized 1991 borders included Crimea, transferred from the Russian SFSR to the Ukrainian SSR in 1954.

What it represents: the blue-and-yellow bicolor, readopted essentially unchanged from the design first flown by the 1917–21 republic — sky over wheat, or, on another common reading, the historic colors of the Galicia-Volhynia arms. For the historical record: Russia's 2014 annexation of Crimea and its 2022 invasion of the rest of the country are widely documented, ongoing disputes over this same territory, well outside this page's historical scope.

Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic (1949 flag)

Communist stateNikita KhrushchevState atheism
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Jewish emigration to Israel (1970s–91)

Who & why: postwar reconstruction under one-party rule continues through the Khrushchev and Brezhnev decades; the Ukrainian SSR is a founding UN member in its own right in 1945 (a Soviet arrangement giving Moscow extra General Assembly seats) despite having no independent foreign policy of its own. The 1986 Chernobyl nuclear disaster and the late-1980s Rukh national movement erode confidence in Soviet rule through the republic's final years.

What it represents: a 1949 redesign adds a blue horizontal stripe along the bottom of the plain red Soviet-republic field — widely read, then and since, as a coded echo of the same blue-over-gold associated with the outlawed independence movement, alongside the standard gold hammer-and-sickle-and-star of every Soviet republic flag.

1944 – 1949

Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic (restored)

Communist stateNikita KhrushchevState atheism
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Poles resettled to Poland (1944–46)Immigration: Ukrainians repatriated from Poland (1944–46)

Who & why: the Red Army clears the last German forces from Ukrainian territory by October 1944 after brutal fighting that leaves the republic devastated. For the first time, all of Ukraine — including Galicia and Volhynia in the west, annexed by the USSR from Poland in 1939 under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact's secret protocols and confirmed by the postwar settlement — is governed as one unified Ukrainian state, ending seven centuries in which the west and east had been ruled separately almost without interruption since the Mongol invasion.

What it represents: the same plain red field and gold hammer-and-sickle-and-star flown before the war, unchanged, asserting continuity of Soviet rule across the occupation's interruption.

1941 – 1944

Reichskommissariat Ukraine (Nazi occupation)

Occupying powerErich KochNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Occupied by: Nazi Germany (1941–44)Emigration: Ostarbeiter forced laborers to Germany

Who & why: Germany invades the USSR on 22 June 1941; most of Ukraine is organized under civilian administration as the Reichskommissariat Ukraine, led by the notoriously brutal Erich Koch, while the far west (Galicia) is attached instead to the German-run General Government based in occupied Poland. The occupation brings mass killing on an enormous scale, including the September 1941 massacre at Babyn Yar outside Kyiv, one of the largest single mass shootings of the Holocaust, and the deportation of hundreds of thousands of Ukrainians for forced labor.

What it represents: no Ukrainian symbol of any kind is issued or tolerated; Nazi Germany's own state flag — red field, white disc, black swastika — is flown throughout, exactly as it is over every other occupied territory in the same period.

1921 – 1941

Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic

Communist stateChristian RakovskyState atheism
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Russian workers into the Donbas (1920s–30s)Immigration: Russian & Belarusian peasants into famine-emptied villages (1933–)

Who & why: the Red Army defeats the last Ukrainian and allied forces by 1921; on 30 December 1922 Soviet Ukraine becomes one of the four founding republics of the USSR, alongside Russia, Belarus, and the Transcaucasian SFSR. The worst of Moscow's rule falls here: forced collectivization and grain requisitioning trigger the Holodomor, the engineered famine of 1932–33 that killed an estimated 3.5 to 5 million people in Soviet Ukraine, a catastrophe increasingly recognized internationally as a genocide targeting the Ukrainian peasantry specifically. Stalin's purges through the 1930s fall especially hard on Ukraine's own cultural and political elite.

What it represents: a plain red banner, first marked simply with gold Cyrillic lettering, later replaced by the standard gold hammer-and-sickle-and-star used across the Soviet republics — the exact year of that particular redesign varies by source, so it's shown here in its later, more familiar form.

1917 – 1921

Ukrainian People's Republic (and rival governments)

Contested republicSymon PetliuraEastern Orthodoxy (autocephaly sought)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Jewish flight from civil-war pogroms (1918–20)Emigration: political émigrés fleeing west (1920–21)

Who & why: as the Russian Empire collapses, the Central Rada in Kyiv declares Ukrainian autonomy in June 1917 and full independence on 22 January 1918. The next three years are genuinely chaotic: a German-backed Hetmanate under Pavlo Skoropadskyi briefly replaces the Rada in 1918, a restored Directorate government fights simultaneously against Bolshevik Russia, Poland, White Russian forces under Denikin, and Nestor Makhno's anarchist movement, while a separate West Ukrainian People's Republic declares itself in Lviv in November 1918 and formally unites with the Kyiv-based republic on 22 January 1919 — a union that, in practice, neither side controls for long. By 1920–21 the Bolsheviks prevail across most of the territory and the republic's government goes into exile.

What it represents: the blue-and-yellow bicolor, formally adopted by the Central Rada in 1918 and understood even then two ways — sky over wheat field, or the historic arms of medieval Galicia-Volhynia recovered as a national symbol — the same design that becomes Ukraine's flag again in 1991.

1795 – 1917

Russian Empire rule

Foreign-ruled territoryCatherine II "the Great"Russian Orthodoxy (imperial state church)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: German Mennonite & colonist settlers (from 1780s)Emigration: peasant emigration to Canada & the US (1891–1914)Emigration: Jewish flight from pogroms (1881–1914)
Ruling house:House of Romanov

Who & why: the 1795 Third Partition of Poland-Lithuania delivers most of Ukraine into the Russian Empire outright, ending the Hetmanate's already-hollowed-out autonomy for good. Russian rule brings the harshest cultural suppression of Ukrainian identity anywhere in the empire, including the 1876 Ems Ukaz banning Ukrainian-language publishing outright. One large exception: Galicia, in the far west, is not part of this story at all — seized by Austria back in the First Partition of 1772, it remains under Habsburg rule throughout this entire period, giving western Ukraine a markedly different 19th-century experience, with considerably more religious and political freedom and a tolerated Ukrainian national revival, from the Russian-ruled east.

What it represents: the Russian Empire's own white-blue-red civil flag, flown over Ukraine simply as imperial territory — no separate Ukrainian flag of any kind is permitted here in this period.

1654 – 1795

Cossack Hetmanate (Russian protectorate)

Autonomous protectorateBohdan KhmelnytskyEastern Orthodoxy (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Mazepa's followers exiled after Poltava (1709)Emigration: Cossacks fleeing to Ottoman territory (1775)

Who & why: Bohdan Khmelnytsky's 1648 uprising against Polish rule shatters Commonwealth control over the Ukrainian steppe; unable to secure lasting independence alone, he turns to Muscovy, and the January 1654 Treaty (Council) of Pereyaslav places the Cossack Hetmanate under the Tsar's protection — historians still debate whether this meant full incorporation, a personal union, or a temporary alliance. In practice it's only the Hetmanate's core, east of the Dnieper plus Kyiv, that comes under Russian protection at first; western Ukraine, including Galicia, remains Polish-Lithuanian for over a century more. Hetman Ivan Mazepa's failed 1708 break with Peter I, crushed at the Battle of Poltava in 1709, triggers decades of tightening Russian control; Catherine II abolishes the Hetmanate's title in 1764 and destroys the Zaporozhian Sich outright in 1775.

What it represents: the Zaporozhian Host's own crimson banner with a white cross, one of several attested Cossack Hetmanate flag patterns — distinct from both the Polish and Russian colors on either side of it, asserting a genuine if fragile autonomy.

1569 – 1654

Kingdom of Poland (Crown lands)

Kingdom (Polish Crown)Sigismund II AugustusRoman Catholicism (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Polish nobility & settlers (steppe colonization)Emigration: Jewish flight from the Khmelnytsky Uprising (1648–54)
Ruling house:House of Jagiellon

Who & why: the 1569 Union of Lublin merges the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania into a single Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth — but not as an equal merger of territory. As part of the union's terms, the Lithuanian-ruled lands of Ukraine (Volhynia, Podolia, and Kyiv itself) transfer directly to the Polish Crown's own administration, while Belarus remains with the Grand Duchy, now the Commonwealth's junior partner. Polish colonization and the spread of serfdom into Ukraine's steppe frontier over the following decades helps drive the rise of the Cossacks — free warrior-settlers organizing around the Zaporozhian Sich — who upend this arrangement within a century.

What it represents: the Polish Crown's own white eagle on red, flown over Ukraine simply as an administrative extension of Poland proper — no distinct Ukrainian flag exists under this direct rule.

Grand Duchy of Lithuania

Grand duchyAlgirdasEastern Orthodoxy (tolerated)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: captives taken in Crimean Tatar slave raids
Ruling house:House of Gediminas

Who & why: Galicia-Volhynia's own line of rulers dies out by 1340, and its territory is fought over for years; by 1349 King Casimir III has secured Galicia for the Polish crown, while Volhynia and the rest of the old Rus' lands are absorbed piece by piece into the fast-expanding Grand Duchy of Lithuania — Kyiv itself falls under Lithuanian control after Grand Duke Algirdas's victory at the Battle of Blue Waters in 1362. By 1392, under Vytautas the Great, Lithuania rules nearly all of what is now Ukraine as one realm, ruling largely through existing Rus' law and the local nobility rather than displacing it.

What it represents: the Vytis (Pahonia) — a white armored horseman on a red field — the Grand Duchy's own dynastic arms, in use across all its territories including the absorbed Rus' lands that would become Ukraine and Belarus alike.

1240 – 1349

Galicia-Volhynia (Principality)

Rus' successor principalityDaniel of GaliciaEastern Orthodoxy (state religion)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Mongol Empire / Golden Horde (1240)
Ruling house:House of Romanovich

Who & why: Batu Khan's forces storm and burn Kiev in December 1240, a catastrophe chroniclers describe in apocalyptic terms, ending the city's centuries-long primacy over the Rus' lands. In the southwest, the principality of Galicia-Volhynia — under Danylo of Galicia, crowned "King of Rus'" by a papal legate in 1253 — survives as the most substantial successor state on the territory of modern Ukraine, though even it pays tribute to the Golden Horde for decades. It remains a genuine medieval power in its own right until its own princely line dies out around 1340.

What it represents: a gold lion rampant on blue, attested on Galicia-Volhynian seals and later 14th-century coinage — commonly called the "Leo of Galicia" — one of the oldest heraldic devices from any part of what is now Ukraine.

c. 900 – 1240

Kievan Rus'

Medieval stateVolodymyr the GreatEastern Orthodoxy (from 988)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Varangian (Norse) founders of the Rurikid dynasty
Ruling house:House of Rurik

Who & why: Kievan Rus' — centered on Kyiv, founded as a Varangian-and-Slavic trade-route state along the Dnieper — is the common ancestor claimed by three modern nations: Ukraine, Belarus, and Russia alike trace their origins to this one polity. Volodymyr the Great converts the realm to Christianity in 988; Yaroslav the Wise (r. 1019–54) presides over its cultural and legal high point. Sviatoslav I (r. 945–72) is its most aggressively expansionist ruler, campaigning as far as the Volga Bulgars and the Khazar Khaganate before dying in an ambush by Pecheneg raiders in 972. Rus' had already been fragmenting into competing principalities for over a century — formalized by the 1097 Council of Liubech — before the Mongols arrived and ended Kyiv's primacy for good.

What it represents: no securely documented state flag survives from this pre-heraldic era. Rulers used a dynastic seal-mark instead — the trident (tryzub), attested on Volodymyr the Great's own coins and seals from the 980s — the same emblem, revived a thousand years later, that forms the center of Ukraine's modern coat of arms. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 700 BCE – c. 900 CE

Scythians, Greek Colonies & the Steppe Successor Peoples

Successive civilizationsIdanthyrsusScythian & Greek polytheism, later Slavic paganism and Khazar Judaism (elite)
Migration & conquest:Attempted conquest by: Persia under Darius I (failed, 513 BCE)Invasion by: Huns (370s CE)

Who & why: Scythian nomadic confederations dominate the steppe north of the Black Sea from around 700 BCE, famously repelling Darius I's Persian invasion in 513 BCE, while Greek settlers found trading colonies on the coast — Olbia (c. 600 BCE) and Chersonesus (c. 528 BCE) among them — that endure for centuries under their own city-state governments. Sarmatians succeed the Scythians as the dominant steppe power, Gothic peoples arrive from the north in the 3rd century CE, and Hunnic invasions from the 370s CE scatter the Goths and impose new steppe overlordship. Slavic tribes settle the forest-steppe zone through this period, later falling under Khazar Khaganate tribute-rule from the 8th century, until Varangian traders establish Kyivan Rus' around 900.

What it represents: no flag in any modern sense existed across this span; Scythian elites are known instead through elaborate gold kurgan burial goods, and Greek colonists used city seals and coinage rather than national banners. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the Greek colonization of the Black Sea coast runs alongside the flowering of Classical Greece itself; Darius I's failed 513 BCE Scythian campaign comes a generation before the better-known Persian invasions of mainland Greece at Marathon (490 BCE) and Salamis (480 BCE); and the Hunnic invasions of the 370s CE that scatter the Goths are directly tied to the wider "Age of Migrations" that ends with the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476 CE.

Reading the tags

Principality / kingdom — medieval or early polity
Foreign-ruled territory — governed from an outside capital
Republic — democratic, sovereign or contested
Communist state — one-party, Soviet-aligned
Occupying power — wartime military occupation
Leader — the named ruler most associated with the era's start
State religion — the official/established faith, not the population's
