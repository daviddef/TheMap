Flags of the United Arab Emirates

From the Bronze Age copper-trading civilization Mesopotamian records call Magan, through a long pre-Islamic and fragmented tribal coast, the 18th-century rise of the Qawasim maritime confederation that the British came to call the "Pirate Coast," a series of 19th-century maritime truces that gave the region its lasting name — the Trucial States — and finally the sudden, rapid transformation from a British-protected patchwork of pearling sheikhdoms into a single federation on 2 December 1971.

Nearly every sheikhdom on this coast flew some version of a plain red flag for generations before 1971; the modern UAE tricolor-plus-red-band, designed in a matter of weeks around federation, is a genuinely new symbol built to represent seven emirates at once rather than an evolution of any single one of their own older banners.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day United Arab Emirates (the historical Trucial Coast), c. 3000 BCE–present, six political entries. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

Seven sheikhdoms, one federation

The UAE's own real internal borders — the seven emirates that eventually federate, traced from real administrative boundaries. Press play, or drag the slider, to watch the coast go from an undivided tribal shoreline to seven separately governed Trucial States to one federal union.

The seven emirates shown here are the seven of 1971, projected back for reference — not seven fixed borders that existed all along. For most of this timeline no internal division existed at all: the coast was either undocumented, or shared a single common maritime identity. Only from 1853 were these seven genuinely separate, individually treatied polities — and even then, Fujairah wasn't independently recognized by Britain until 1952, governed as part of Sharjah before that. The border lines only solidify where a real, separate government existed; they dissolve the moment one government held the whole coast, which happens for good in 1971–72.

United Arab Emirates (federation)

FederationSheikh Zayed bin Sultan Al NahyanIslam (Sunni, official)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: South Asian & Filipino labor migrants (1970s–)
Ruling house:House of Al NahyanHouse of Al MaktoumHouse of Al Qasimi

Who & why: as Britain withdraws from its remaining Gulf commitments, six of the Trucial States federate as the United Arab Emirates on 2 December 1971, with Ras al-Khaimah joining as the seventh emirate weeks later in February 1972. A Bedouin, pearling, and fishing economy transforms within a generation into one of the world's major oil-and-gas economies and a global trade and finance hub, centered on Abu Dhabi and Dubai.

What it represents: a new flag, designed by a 19-year-old, Abdullah Mohammed Al Maainah, in a nationwide competition held just before federation, uses pan-Arab colors — a vertical red band at the hoist against horizontal green, white, and black stripes — to represent the seven emirates as a single new whole rather than favor any one of their older individual banners.

Trucial States (Perpetual Maritime Truce)

British-protected sheikhdomsSaeed bin Tahnun Al Nahyan (Abu Dhabi)Sunni Islam
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Indian (Gujarati) merchant communitiesImmigration: enslaved East Africans (until 1963 abolition)
Ruling house:House of Al QasimiHouse of Al NahyanHouse of Al Maktoum

Who & why: the individual sheikhdoms — Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Sharjah, Ajman, Umm al-Quwain, Ras al-Khaimah, and Fujairah (recognized 1952) — sign a "Perpetual Maritime Truce" with Britain in 1853, formalizing decades of anti-piracy treaties into the lasting arrangement that gives this coast its name: the Trucial Coast, its rulers the Trucial Sheikhs. Britain handles each sheikhdom's foreign affairs and defense while leaving internal rule to its own sheikh.

What it represents: most sheikhdoms continue flying plain red, the region's long-standing maritime color; Britain requires some to add distinguishing white borders or other marks under earlier 1820s treaty terms, so that British ships could tell truce-observing vessels apart at a glance.

General Maritime Treaty era

British-protected sheikhdomsSultan bin Saqr Al QasimiSunni Islam
Ruling house:House of Al QasimiHouse of Al NahyanHouse of Al Maktoum

Who & why: after a British punitive expedition against Qawasim ports in 1819, the coast's sheikhs sign the General Maritime Treaty of 1820, the first in a series of agreements curbing raids on shipping in the Gulf, laying the legal groundwork for the fuller "perpetual" truce that follows in 1853.

What it represents: the plain red flags already flown by the coast's ports and ships continue through this transitional treaty era, the same maritime tradition shared with neighboring Bahrain, Qatar, and Oman.

Qawasim maritime confederation

Maritime confederationRahma bin Matar Al QasimiSunni Islam
Ruling house:House of Al Qasimi

Who & why: the Al Qasimi (Qawasim) tribal confederation rises to dominate the coast from Ras al-Khaimah and Sharjah through the 18th century, building one of the region's largest fleets and contesting British and Omani shipping alike — a rivalry that leads the British to brand the coast the "Pirate Coast," a characterization the Qawasim's own descendants and many historians dispute as one-sided.

What it represents: no single specific Qawasim banner is well attested; plain red, the shared maritime color of this whole coast for generations, is shown here as an informed reconstruction rather than a documented period specimen.

Fragmented tribal coast

Fragmented tribal settlementsNo single ruler documentedSunni Islam (from the 7th century)

Who & why: for most of this long span, the coast that becomes the UAE is home to scattered pearling, fishing, and trading settlements under local tribal leadership, with no unified polity of any real size. Islam arrives in the 7th century, and the coast passes through the outer orbit of various regional powers without ever being governed as a single entity until the Qawasim confederation's rise in the early 18th century.

What it represents: no flag in the modern territorial sense survives from this era; local tribal groups used banners and insignia rather than fixed flags, and none can be attributed to this coast specifically with confidence. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

Bronze Age Magan & the pre-Islamic coast

Successive civilizationsNo single ruler documentedPre-Islamic Arabian polytheism, with a Nestorian Christian presence (6th–7th c.)

Who & why: archaeological sites at Hili and along this coast document the Umm an-Nar Bronze Age culture (c. 2600–2000 BCE), the civilization Sumerian and later Akkadian cuneiform records call "Magan," prized as a source of copper shipped to Mesopotamia. Iron Age settlement follows, sustained by early falaj irrigation, and by late antiquity the coast sits within the pre-Islamic Arabian tribal order, with a small Nestorian Christian monastery documented on Sir Bani Yas island as late as the 6th–7th centuries. Islam arrives in the 630s CE, opening the roughly seven centuries of scattered tribal settlement described in the fragmented tribal coast entry above.

What it represents: no flag in any modern sense existed across this span; Magan is known through copper ingots, ceramics, and Mesopotamian trade tablets rather than any surviving banner or device. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the Umm an-Nar culture's copper trade with Mesopotamia runs alongside the construction of Egypt's Great Pyramid of Giza (c. 2560 BCE) and the height of Sumerian city-states such as Ur; and the small Nestorian Christian monastery on Sir Bani Yas island, active in the 6th–7th centuries, was built as the Byzantine–Sasanian wars raged to the north, only decades before the rise of Islam reshaped the entire region.
