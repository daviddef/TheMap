Flags of Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe takes its name from Great Zimbabwe, the largest stone-walled complex in pre-colonial sub-Saharan Africa, capital of a real and monumental African kingdom centuries before any European set foot in the region — itself the successor to Iron Age farming communities and the gold-trading Leopard's Kopje/Mapungubwe culture that came a thousand years earlier still. Its path since then runs through the Ndebele Kingdom that resettled here during the Mfecane, Cecil Rhodes's chartered company conquest, decades as the self-governing colony of Southern Rhodesia, a white-minority regime's unilateral break from Britain, and a hard-fought transition to majority rule in 1980.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Zimbabwe, c. 200 CE–present, eight political entities, from early Iron Age farming communities and the Kingdom of Zimbabwe and Ndebele Kingdom through British and settler rule to the modern republic. The Kingdom of Zimbabwe/Mutapa/Rozvi succession came first, but the Ndebele Kingdom that followed coexisted side by side with the Shona-speaking successor states rather than replacing them outright — the map below traces that one genuine internal partition alongside the whole country's own real border. Part of a larger atlas — see the linked pages at the bottom.

One border, mostly one region — except for half a century

Zimbabwe's own real traced border, split for one genuine stretch of overlap: from the late 1830s–40s until the 1893–94 British South Africa Company conquest, the Ndebele Kingdom in the southwest (Matabeleland) and the Shona-speaking successor states to its north and east (Mashonaland) coexisted side by side, not one after another. Two regions overlap for roughly fifty years; the rest of this page is one piece. Great Zimbabwe's Shona-speaking successor states (the Mutapa and Rozvi empires) held the whole territory alone until Mzilikazi's Ndebele Kingdom carved out Matabeleland in the southwest around 1840 — the two coexisted and often clashed for the better part of a century, not in sequence. BSAC rule, the self-governing colony, Rhodesia's UDI, and independent Zimbabwe each reunited and governed the whole territory as one piece afterward. The border line below only solidifies for that one overlapping era; it dissolves everywhere else.

1980 – present

Republic of Zimbabwe (majority rule)

Republic, independentRobert MugabeSecular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: economic & political exodus (2000s–)

Who & why: following the December 1979 Lancaster House Agreement, internationally supervised elections bring Robert Mugabe's ZANU-PF to power, and Zimbabwe becomes internationally recognized independent on 18 April 1980 under majority rule — ending nearly 90 years of settler-minority governance. Mugabe governs until a 2017 military-assisted resignation; the country continues under ZANU-PF rule today.

What it represents: the seven stripes (green-yellow-red-black-red-yellow-green) evoke the land, mineral wealth, liberation-war bloodshed, and the country's Black majority; the white triangle carries a red star for socialism/hope and the Zimbabwe Bird, a soapstone carving recovered from Great Zimbabwe's ruins, directly connecting the modern flag to the pre-colonial kingdom the country is named for.

1979 – 1980

Transition to majority rule (Lancaster House to independence)

Transitional administrationLord Soames (British governor)No official state religion

Who & why: this is a genuinely messy, contested few months rather than a clean chapter: a brief 1979 internal settlement called "Zimbabwe Rhodesia" (with limited Black participation but still settler-dominated) fails to win international recognition, and the December 1979 Lancaster House Agreement brings the territory briefly back under direct British colonial authority as "Southern Rhodesia" one final time to supervise a ceasefire in the Bush War and organize the February 1980 elections that bring Mugabe to power.

What it represents: no single settled flag reflects this rapid, multi-stage transition; Britain's own Union Jack flew during the brief resumed colonial administration, treated here as one honest bridging entry rather than invented sub-period flags.

1965 – 1979

Rhodesia (Unilateral Declaration of Independence)

White-minority rule (unrecognized)Ian SmithNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Emigration: white Rhodesians to South Africa (late 1970s)

Who & why: on 11 November 1965, Prime Minister Ian Smith's white-minority government unilaterally declares independence from Britain rather than accept British demands for a path to majority rule — a breakaway, apartheid-adjacent regime that no country ever formally recognizes as legitimate. UN sanctions follow, and the Rhodesian Bush War, an escalating guerrilla conflict between the government and Black nationalist movements (ZANU and ZAPU), runs through the 1970s until the regime is finally forced to the negotiating table.

What it represents: adopted in 1968 as Rhodesia moved to formally declare itself a republic, the green-white-green flag carries the same Zimbabwe Bird emblem later inherited by independent Zimbabwe's own flag, on a plain shield rather than in gold relief.

1923 – 1965

Southern Rhodesia (self-governing colony)

Colony (settler self-government)Charles CoghlanNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Immigration: British settlers & regional farm labor (1945–)

Who & why: after white settlers vote against joining the Union of South Africa, Southern Rhodesia becomes a self-governing British colony in 1923, with political power concentrated almost entirely in the hands of the small white settler minority throughout. From 1953 to 1963 it is joined with Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland in the unpopular, settler-dominated Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland before reverting to separate colonial status ahead of Ian Smith's 1965 UDI.

What it represents: the standard British colonial Blue Ensign pattern, defaced with Southern Rhodesia's own badge, in continuous use (apart from the Federation's shared flag, 1953–63) until the 1965 unilateral break from Britain.

1890 – 1923

British South Africa Company rule (Rhodesia)

Chartered company ruleCecil RhodesNo official state religion
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: British South Africa Company (1893–94)

Who & why: Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company occupies Mashonaland in 1890 and, after fierce resistance from King Lobengula's Ndebele Kingdom, conquers Matabeleland in the 1893–94 Matabele War, naming the combined territory Rhodesia after Rhodes himself. Company administration runs until 1923, when settlers vote for self-government rather than incorporation into South Africa.

What it represents: a British colonial-pattern Blue Ensign defaced with the Company's own badge, the administrative flag of BSAC-governed Rhodesia until direct settler self-government begins.

c. 1840 – 1890

Ndebele Kingdom (Matabeleland)

Kingdom, independentMzilikaziTraditional Ndebele religion (ancestor veneration)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Ndebele migration from Zululand (Mfecane)
Ruling house:House of Khumalo (Ndebele)

Who & why: Mzilikazi, a former Zulu general who broke from Shaka during the Mfecane's upheavals, leads his Ndebele (Matabele) people on a long migration that ends in southwestern Zimbabwe by the late 1830s–40s, founding a powerful kingdom that coexists and often clashes with the region's Shona-speaking states. His son Lobengula succeeds him in 1870 and rules until the kingdom's conquest by the British South Africa Company in 1893–94.

What it represents: no securely documented national flag survives for the Ndebele Kingdom; royal authority was expressed through regalia and military regiments in the Zulu-derived tradition rather than a fixed territorial banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 1220 – 1840

Kingdom of Zimbabwe, Mutapa, and Rozvi Empire

Kingdom, independentNo single ruler documentedTraditional Shona religion (Mwari veneration)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Nguni incursions (Mfecane, early 19th c.)
Ruling house:Mutapa dynastyChangamire dynasty (Rozvi)

Who & why: the Kingdom of Zimbabwe, centered on the monumental stone city of Great Zimbabwe (built roughly 1220–1450, the largest such complex in pre-colonial sub-Saharan Africa and the direct namesake of the modern country), controls a Shona-speaking trading state at the height of its power in the 13th–15th centuries, linked by trade routes to the Indian Ocean and Swahili coast. Its successor states, the Mutapa (Munhumutapa) Empire and later the Rozvi Empire, carry Shona statehood forward into the 18th and 19th centuries, until Rozvi power declines under pressure from Nguni groups migrating north during the Mfecane.

What it represents: no securely documented flag or banner survives from any of these states; their authority is attested instead through monumental architecture, royal regalia, and oral tradition. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 200 – c. 1220 CE

Early Iron Age farming states & the Leopard's Kopje culture

Fragmented / Iron Age chiefdomsNo single ruler documentedTraditional/ancestral religion (Bantu)
Migration & conquest:Migration: Bantu-speaking farmers & ironworkers (from c. 200 CE)Trade & cultural influence: Mapungubwe kingdom (c. 1075–1220)

Who & why: long before Great Zimbabwe's famous stone walls rose, Bantu-speaking farmers and ironworkers of the Gokomere/Ziwa archaeological culture settled the Zimbabwe plateau from around 200 CE, part of the broader Bantu expansion reshaping sub-Saharan Africa. From roughly 900 CE, the cattle-wealthy Leopard's Kopje culture develops, its Mapungubwe center just south of the modern border becoming, by the 11th and 12th centuries, a major gold- and ivory-trading kingdom linked to the Indian Ocean and the Swahili coast — generally regarded by archaeologists as Great Zimbabwe's direct forerunner in wealth, scale, and trade connections. Mapungubwe's decline after about 1220 coincides closely with Great Zimbabwe's own rise on the plateau to its north, covered in the entry that follows.

What it represents: no flag or comparable state banner survives from any of these Iron Age societies, and none is invented here — shown honestly blank. Pottery sequences, cattle kraals, and the gold and glass-bead grave goods of Mapungubwe are the record that exists instead.

World context: this millennium runs alongside the decline and fall of the Western Roman Empire, the rise and spread of Islam across North Africa and the Swahili coast that fed Mapungubwe's own Indian Ocean gold trade, and, in Europe, the height of the medieval period — closing just as Great Zimbabwe's monumental stone city itself begins to rise.
