Flags of the Adriatic

Every flag that has flown over the land your family comes from — going back past Ragusa, Venetian Dalmatia, and the Habsburg kingdom to Illyrian tribes resisting Rome and the Slavic Croat duchies that came before the medieval kingdom, through two Yugoslavias to the modern republic — laid out in order, with the colors and proportions needed to paint each one accurately.

Borders in this corner of the Adriatic rarely moved because a nation voted for it. A republic was dissolved by Napoleon in an afternoon; a checkerboard shield was granted by a Habsburg chancery; a red star was sewn onto a partisan banner in the mountains before it ever flew over a capital. The flag usually tells you who held the pen.

Scope of this first pass: the lands of present-day Croatia, under whichever crown, republic, or federation governed them, c. 400 BCE–present. Eighteen political entities, newest first — working backward from today's republic to the Illyrian tribes and Roman Dalmatia that preceded the medieval kingdom. This is the pilot for a much larger project — see the roadmap at the bottom for where to point it next (the rest of former Yugoslavia, then outward).

Four regions, one country — eventually

The same real coastline as the regional hub, zoomed in on Croatia alone and split into the four pieces that were, for significant stretches of history, governed separately: continental Croatia-Slavonia, the Dalmatian coast, the Istrian peninsula, and — for 328 years — the militarized Croatian Military Frontier, carved out of continental Croatia's own southern and eastern borderlands (including Lika-Senj) and answering directly to Vienna's War Council rather than the Ban in Zagreb. Press play, or drag the slider.

Croatia wasn't governed as one place until surprisingly recently. Continental Croatia answered to Budapest or Vienna; Dalmatia answered to Venice, then Vienna directly; Istria was ceded to Italy twice in the 20th century alone; and from 1553 to 1881 the Military Frontier was carved out of continental Croatia itself, a standing army district answering to Vienna's War Council rather than Zagreb's own civil government. The border lines below only solidify where two pieces were actually ruled apart — they dissolve the moment a single government held them together.

1990 – present

Republic of Croatia

Sovereign state Franjo Tuđman Secular (no state religion)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Croat & Bosnian war refugees (1991–95)Emigration: EU-era labor emigration (2013–)

Who & why: as communist rule crumbled across Eastern Europe, Croatia held its first multi-party elections in 1990, adopted a new constitution, and declared independence from Yugoslavia on 25 June 1991 — triggering the Croatian War of Independence (1991–95) against the Yugoslav army and Krajina Serb forces before its borders were secured.

What it represents: the flag and coat of arms were fixed by law on 21 December 1990, months before the formal declaration. The star of the socialist era is gone; in its place, the historic five-shield crown — the oldest Croatian arms, Dubrovnik, Dalmatia, Istria, Slavonia — over the šahovnica, asserting a continuous Croatian identity reaching back past Yugoslavia to the medieval kingdom this atlas opened with.

1991 – 1995

Republic of Serbian Krajina

Breakaway state Milan Babić Serbian Orthodox (identity marker, not formally established)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: Croats expelled from Krajina (1991)Emigration: Serb exodus after Operation Storm (1995)

Who & why: as Croatia moved toward independence in 1990–91, ethnic Serb-majority areas of the Croatian interior declared their own state, backed by the Yugoslav army, rather than remain in a non-Yugoslav Croatia. Its constitution was adopted 19 December 1991. Croatian forces retook most of its territory in the 1995 Operation Storm; the last sliver, Eastern Slavonia, was peacefully reintegrated under UN administration by 1998.

What it represents: visually almost identical to Serbia's own flag, red-blue-white rather than Croatia's red-white-blue, asserting Serb rather than Croatian national identity on contested ground — a small ordering difference carrying the entire claim.

1947 – 1990

Socialist Republic of Croatia

Socialist republic Josip Broz Tito State atheism

Who & why: one of the six constituent republics of SFR Yugoslavia, with its own capital (Zagreb), constitution, and Communist Party branch, though real power stayed centralized under the federal party and Tito personally.

What it represents: the Croatian tricolor and checkerboard shield returned to official use, but topped with a red star where a crown had once sat — the old national symbol permitted again, but only under the new regime's mark. That gold-bordered star was the first thing removed when Croatia adopted its post-independence flag in 1990.

1946 – 1992

Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia

Socialist republic Josip Broz Tito State atheism

Who & why: Tito formalized six constituent republics — Croatia, Serbia, Slovenia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Macedonia, Montenegro — under a one-party federal constitution adopted 31 January 1946. Yugoslavia broke with Stalin in 1948 and charted its own non-aligned path through the Cold War, holding together a genuinely diverse federation for over four decades.

What it represents: The flag set by Article 4 of the 1946 constitution was blue-white-red with a red five-pointed star with a golden-yellow edge in the centre, its upper point reaching halfway into the blue band. FOTW credits the painter Đorđe Andrejević-Kun with the design (Wikipedia marks this citation needed). The sources read give the end of the flag as spring 1991 (FOTW) or April 1992 (Wikipedia); they do not cover a Croatian republic-level variant.

1943 – 1946

Democratic Federal Yugoslavia (Partisan flag)

Socialist republic Josip Broz Tito State atheism (Communist-led movement)

Who & why: Josip Broz Tito's communist-led Partisans fought both the Axis occupiers and rival royalist Chetniks through the war, building the only resistance movement the Allies came to fully back — formal recognition came at the November 1943 Tehran Conference. Their Anti-Fascist Council (AVNOJ) proclaimed a new federal Yugoslavia even before the war ended.

What it represents: the same blue-white-red tricolor, now carrying a plain red star in the center — communism laid directly over the old kingdom's flag, signaling continuity of the state but a total change of who ruled it.

1941 – 1945

Independent State of Croatia (NDH)

Occupation / puppet state Ante Pavelić Roman Catholic (traditionally favored, not formally established)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Nazi Germany & Fascist ItalyEmigration: Serbs, Jews & Roma fleeing Ustaše genocide

Who & why: after the Axis invasion of Yugoslavia on 6 April 1941, Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy installed the Ustaše, a Croatian fascist movement led by Ante Pavelić, as rulers of a nominally independent state covering Croatia and Bosnia-Herzegovina. It was a client regime, ceding the Adriatic coast to Italy and enforcing genocidal policies against Serbs, Jews, and Roma. It collapsed with the Axis defeat on 8 May 1945.

What it represents: the flag — decreed 28 April 1941 — looks like a Croatian tricolor at a glance, but the checkerboard's first square was set white instead of the traditional red, and the state flag added the Ustaše "U" monogram in the canton. A separate civil flag omitted both.

1939 – 1941

Banovina of Croatia

Autonomous province Vladko Maček Roman Catholic (majority faith, not separately established)

Who & why: facing mounting unrest and the threat of European war, the Yugoslav government and Croatian Peasant Party leader Vladko Maček signed the Sporazum (Agreement) on 26 August 1939, merging the Sava and Littoral banovinas (plus slices of others) into a single self-governing Croatian banovina — Belgrade kept only defense and foreign affairs.

What it represents: the Croatian tricolor with the plain šahovnica shield, used "by custom" rather than formal decree. It was the closest thing to Croatian self-rule in the interwar period — and it lasted barely eighteen months before the German-Italian invasion of April 1941 erased it.

1929 – 1941

Kingdom of Yugoslavia

Kingdom Alexander I No single state religion (Serbian Orthodox privileged)

Who & why: in 1929, King Alexander I suspended the constitution and parliament, renamed the country Yugoslavia ("land of the South Slavs"), and redrew internal borders into nine banovinas that deliberately ignored historic Croatian, Serbian and Slovenian lines — an attempt to dissolve ethnic identity into a single Yugoslav one. Alexander was assassinated in Marseille in 1934.

What it represents: the flag itself didn't change from the 1918 tricolor; what changed was the claim behind it — a single Yugoslav nation rather than three united peoples.

1918 – 1929

Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes

Kingdom Peter I of Serbia No single state religion (Serbian Orthodox privileged)

Who & why: as Austria-Hungary collapsed at the end of World War I, the short-lived State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs merged with the Kingdom of Serbia and Kingdom of Montenegro on 1 December 1918, uniting the South Slavs under the Serbian Karađorđević dynasty. The new government fixed the state tricolor on 9/22 December 1918, confirmed by the 1921 St Vitus Day Constitution.

What it represents: The Kingdom's flag was a plain blue-white-red horizontal tricolour. Wikipedia describes it as a variation of the Pan-Slavic flag adopted at the Prague congress of 1848, and says the Corfu Declaration allowed the Serbian, Croatian and Slovenian national flags to be displayed freely beside it; the 1922 law fixes the blue-white-red ensigns. The sources read do not say the ordering was chosen to follow any one constituent's flag.

1868 – 1918

Kingdom of Croatia-Slavonia-Dalmatia

Kingdom (in union) Franz Joseph I Roman Catholic (traditional)
Migration & conquest:Emigration: to the Americas (1880s–1914)
Ruling house:House of Habsburg

Who & why: the 1868 Croatian-Hungarian Settlement (Nagodba) gave Croatia-Slavonia internal autonomy within the Hungarian half of Austria-Hungary — its own Sabor, Ban, and official language — while foreign policy, defense, and finance stayed with Budapest and Vienna. Dalmatia, still separately administered from the Austrian side, was claimed on paper but not actually governed together with it.

What it represents: the civil tricolor is the 1848 Croatian flag continued; the state version adds the combined arms of Croatia, Slavonia and Dalmatia beneath the Crown of St. Stephen, asserting a unity the empire never actually granted on the ground.

1848

The Illyrian & National Revival Flag

Kingdom (civic use) Josip Jelačić Roman Catholic (traditional)

Who & why: in the 1830s, writer Ljudevit Gaj's Illyrian Movement pushed a shared South Slavic language and identity against Hungarian pressure inside the Habsburg lands. When revolution swept Europe in 1848, the Croatian Sabor (parliament) and Ban Josip Jelačić adopted a Croatian tricolor and pressed for autonomy from Budapest.

What it represents: red, white and blue — the "pan-Slavic colors" formally adopted that same year at the Prague Slavic Congress, echoing Russia's flag as a sign of Slavic solidarity. Croatia stacked them red-white-blue; Serbia used red-blue-white; Slovenia white-blue-red — the same three colors, reordered by each nation, a pattern that recurs through every flag below.

1809 – 1813

Illyrian Provinces (Napoleonic France)

Colonial rule Napoleon Bonaparte Roman Catholic (majority faith, not exclusively established)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Napoleonic France

Who & why: after defeating Austria, Napoleon carved Carniola, western Carinthia, Istria, Croatia south of the Sava, Dalmatia, and the just-annexed Ragusa into a single province of the French Empire, cutting Austria off from the Adriatic and securing his own supply routes.

What it represents: no distinct local flag is recorded — Wikipedia's article shows the French flag for the province, which was administered in French, with Napoleonic law and Illyrian-language schooling introduced. Short-lived (Austria recovered the area in 1813–14, formalized at the 1815 Congress of Vienna), but the name "Illyria" it popularized would be picked up a generation later by Croatian nationalists.

1527 – 1868

Kingdom of Croatia under the Habsburgs

Kingdom Ferdinand I of Habsburg Roman Catholic (Habsburg)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Serb refugee-soldiers on the Military Frontier (16th–18th c.)Conquered by: Ottoman Empire (parts of Slavonia, 16th c.–1699)
Ruling house:House of HabsburgHouse of Frankopan

Who & why: facing Ottoman advance, the Croatian nobility elected the Habsburg Ferdinand I as king at the 1527 Cetin assembly in exchange for military protection — the start of nearly four centuries under Vienna. Much of the kingdom's territory became the Habsburg Military Frontier, a defensive buffer zone garrisoned against the Ottomans.

What it represents: the red-and-white checkerboard, šahovnica, is first attested as a decorative symbol of the Kingdom of Croatia in 1495 and appears officially on a seal of 1527, when Ferdinand I was elected king. Historians think the Habsburg dynasty probably influenced its creation, and there is no historical evidence for the older story that its colours stood for Red and White Croatia. It had no fixed form until the 19th century. It is the single symbol every later Croatian flag on this page still carries.

1102 – 1527

Kingdom of Croatia (in union with Hungary)

Kingdom Coloman of Hungary Roman Catholic
Ruling house:House of Anjou (Angevin)House of Frankopan

Who & why: after the death of the last native Trpimirović king, Croatian nobles reached the 1102 Pacta Conventa with King Coloman of Hungary — traditionally recorded as an agreement, though no surviving contemporary document proves the exact terms. Croatia entered a personal union under the Hungarian crown rather than outright annexation: it kept its own nobility, its ban (viceroy) governing in the king's name, and its Sabor (assembly), while the same monarch wore both crowns. That union outlasted the Árpád dynasty and continued under the Angevin and Jagiellonian kings of Hungary for four centuries, ending only when Louis II died at Mohács in 1526 and Croatian nobles, meeting separately at Cetin in 1527, elected Ferdinand of Habsburg as their own king.

What it represents: no single fixed flag or coat of arms is reliably documented for Croatia across this whole 425-year span — the kingdom used royal Hungarian banners and, locally, the ban's and various noble houses' own heraldry rather than one settled national device. The checkerboard šahovnica that later became fixed to Croatia is first clearly attested only around 1527, at the close of this period, not before it — shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

1420 – 1797

Venetian Dalmatia

Colonial rule Doge Tommaso Mocenigo Roman Catholic (Venetian)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Republic of Venice

Who & why: the Republic of Venice conquered the Dalmatian coastal cities (Zadar, Split, Šibenik, and the islands, though never Ragusa) to secure its Adriatic trade routes and naval supply lines. Venice ran Dalmatia as a maritime colony, the Stato da Màr, for nearly four centuries.

What it represents: the winged lion of the evangelist Mark, patron of Venice, holding an open book — peacetime rule — or a closed book and drawn sword in wartime. It was carved into fortress walls up and down the coast and remains visible on Dalmatian towers today.

1358 – 1808

Republic of Ragusa (Dubrovnik)

City-republic No single ruler documented (rotating Rector) Roman Catholic (state religion, patron St. Blaise)

Who & why: a Dalmatian merchant city that bought its way out of Venetian and then Hungarian overlordship, governing itself under an aristocratic Senate. It survived for 450 years on diplomacy and trade between the Ottoman and Christian worlds.

What it represents: its 1272 statute-book named the "VIXILLUM S. BLASIJ," the flag of Saint Blaise, the city's patron. A second flag simply read LIBERTAS — liberty — the Republic's own motto and, alongside Venice, one of the earliest civic mottoes flown as a flag anywhere in Europe. Napoleon dissolved the Republic in 1808.

c. 925 – 1102

Medieval Kingdom of Croatia & Dalmatia

Kingdom Tomislav Roman Catholic

Who & why: Tomislav was crowned the first king around 925, uniting Pannonian and Dalmatian Croat duchies under one crown recognized by Rome. There was no "national flag" in the modern sense — rulers fought under dynastic and religious banners, often bearing a cross or the ruler's own device.

What survives: the star-over-crescent device shown here is the arms later remembered as "the oldest shield of Croatia" — it still sits at the top of the modern coat of arms' crown of five shields. It was retroactively fixed in heraldry centuries after the fact, not flown as a battle standard at the time. The sources read date the device later than this card: Wikipedia's Coat of arms of Croatia finds the star over a crescent on coins of Andrew II and calls it a misconception that it is the oldest Croatian arms, and Flags of the World quotes Velde, Stancic and Heimer for coins of the late 12th or early 13th century (1196). Neither describes a banner for 925 to 1102, so the device is shown here as a later heraldic reconstruction, not a documented medieval standard.

c. 400 BCE – 925 CE

Illyrian Tribes, Roman Dalmatia & the Early Croat Duchies

Fragmented / successive polities Queen Teuta (Illyrian Wars) Illyrian/Slavic paganism, then Christianity (from 9th c.)
Migration & conquest:Conquest by: Rome (Illyrian Wars, 229–168 BCE)Settlement: Slavic & Croat migration (from c. 7th c. CE)

Who & why: Illyrian tribes — the Delmatae (who give Dalmatia its name), the Liburni, and others — hold the eastern Adriatic coast when Rome, provoked by piracy and the resistance of Queen Teuta and later King Genthius, annexes the region through three Illyrian Wars between 229 and 168 BCE. Roman Dalmatia becomes thoroughly Romanized; the emperor Diocletian, born near Salona (modern Split), retires there in 305 CE to the fortified palace whose walls still form the core of Split's old town, a genuine surviving Roman monument rather than an invented one. Slavic peoples, including the Croats, migrate into the weakening Byzantine and former Roman provinces from around the early 7th century, and by the 9th century Frankish-recognized Croat dukes — Borna, Mislav, Trpimir, and Branimir, who wins Pope John VIII's explicit recognition in 879 — rule separate Pannonian and Dalmatian duchies. Duke Tomislav unites these duchies and is crowned king around 925, exactly where the neighboring entry on this page begins.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from any part of this thirteen-century span — centuries before heraldry develops anywhere in Europe. Illyrian tribes and the early Croat dukes used their own devices and seals rather than fixed territorial flags. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: the Illyrian Wars run alongside Rome's own Punic Wars against Carthage; Diocletian's Split palace is built while the Roman Empire is still constructing monuments across the Mediterranean; and the Croat duchies' Christianization and Frankish overlordship in the 9th century are contemporary with Charlemagne's Carolingian Empire and the Byzantine mission of Cyril and Methodius to neighboring Great Moravia in 863.

Reading the tags

City-republic — self-governing maritime city-state
Colonial rule — governed from a foreign capital
Kingdom — crowned realm, own or in union
Autonomous province — self-rule inside a larger state
Occupation / puppet state — installed by an outside power
Socialist republic — constituent or federal, one-party state
Breakaway state — unrecognized or partially recognized
Sovereign state — internationally recognized nation
Leader — the named ruler most associated with the era's start
State religion — the official/established faith, not the population's
