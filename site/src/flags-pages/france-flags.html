Flags of France

Before 1789 the royal colour of France was white and the royal banner was blue with golden fleurs-de-lis; the tricolour began as a cockade in July 1789, became the navy’s flag in 1790 and took its present order, blue at the hoist, by a decree of 15 February 1794. It has flown over two empires and several republics, was replaced by the white flag from 1814 or 1815 to 1830, and was kept by both Vichy and Free France in 1940–1944.

Unlike most pages in this atlas, every era below was written from the sources named under it, and the panel beneath each one says what they establish and what they leave open. Where the two main sources, Wikipedia and Flags of the World, disagree, both are given.

Scope of this page: metropolitan France, from Roman Gaul to the present, with the eras that changed the flag over the mainland. The overseas empire, Algeria and the colonies are not covered, and there is no interactive map yet on this page.

1958 – present

Fifth Republic

RepublicSecular (laïcité)

Who & why: the constitution of the Fifth Republic names the flag in its Article 2: the national emblem is the tricolour flag, blue, white, red (Wikipedia, quoting the article; Flags of the World, citing the Société française de vexillologie, gives the same wording for 1958). The shade of the blue has moved: President Giscard d'Estaing had it lightened in the 1970s (1974 in Wikipedia and the SFV, 1976 in a Flags of the World contributor), and on 13 July 2020 the Élysée Palace went back to the darker navy, without an announcement and without orders for other institutions.

What it represents: the same blue-white-red tricolour whose order the Convention fixed on 15 February 1794 — blue at the hoist, white in the middle, red at the fly. The 1958 text does not set a shade, and the swatches below are approximations of the commonly shown colours, not a specification.

1944 – 1958

Provisional Government & Fourth Republic

Republic

Who & why: a tricolour that had been the last to fly over Paris in June 1940, from the PFG building on boulevard Richard-Lenoir after a German flag went up over the Arc de Triomphe on 14 June, was raised again on 19 August 1944, according to Flags of the World. Wikipedia says the constitutions of 1946 and 1958 both named the blue, white and red flag as the national emblem.

What it represents: the same tricolour continuing through the change of regime; the sources read describe no change to its design in these years.

1940 – 1944

The French State (Vichy) & Free France

Occupation & divisionPétain

Who & why: in June 1940, after the defeat, most deputies gave Marshal Pétain full powers, and the French State was legally the successor to the Third Republic (Flags of the World). It kept the tricolour as the national flag and replaced the republican motto with Travail, Famille, Patrie; the only new flag was the head of state's standard, prescribed by a decree of 19 March 1942 as a tricolour with seven gold stars on the white stripe below a francisque. Free France had to add a charge to the tricolour because Vichy kept it: Wikipedia says Vice-Admiral Muselier created a naval jack with a red Cross of Lorraine by general order of 3 July 1940, and Flags of the World says a regulation of 5 June 1941 prescribed the cross as the emblem of Free France.

What it represents: the one tricolour claimed by two governments. The image shows the plain tricolour that both started from; neither source read describes the cross of Lorraine flag in enough detail to draw it here.

1870 – 1940

Third Republic

Republic

Who & why: when the Comte de Chambord, the Bourbon claimant, was offered the throne he made a white flag a condition and refused the tricolour, so the monarchist plan fell through and the Republic kept the tricolour. Wikipedia dates his refusal to 1870 in a caption; Flags of the World dates the flag dispute to 1873 and records compromise ideas, a tricolour with fleurs-de-lis or a white flag with a tricolour cravat, that he rejected.

What it represents: the same tricolour, now kept by default when the one alternative on offer was the Bourbon white flag.

1852 – 1870

Second Empire

EmpireNapoléon III

Who & why: Prince Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte was proclaimed Emperor of the French as Napoléon III on 2 December 1852, and the national flag remained the blue-white-red tricolour (Flags of the World, Ivan Sache). A regulation of 17 May 1853 fixed the ensign's “optical” proportions of 30:33:37, and the dark navy blue was firmly established in practice at least from this period.

What it represents: the tricolour continuing under the second Bonaparte; Flags of the World treats the imperial bee, used again under this empire, as an emblem rather than a flag.

1848 – 1852

Second Republic

Republic

Who & why: Wikipedia says the Second Republic adopted a tricolour variant for a few days, from 24 February to 5 March 1848, and its caption to Philippoteaux's painting says it depicts Lamartine rejecting the red flag in front of the city hall. A decree of 7 March 1848 confirmed the tricolour ensign (Flags of the World, citing Heimer, du Payrat and Gay).

What it represents: the tricolour again, kept at the moment a red flag was on offer.

1830 – 1848

July Monarchy

Constitutional monarchyLouis-Philippe

Who & why: the tricolour had been banned since 1815 and reappeared during Les Trois Glorieuses of 27–29 July 1830. On 30 July Lafayette gave Louis-Philippe a tricolour on the balcony of the city hall, and on 1 August Louis-Philippe, as Lieutenant-General, ordered that France resume its national colours (Flags of the World). He briefly used a fleur-de-lis coat of arms from 13 August 1830 to 16 February 1831 before dropping it under pressure. Wikipedia says the tricolour has been used since 1830 except for 1848.

What it represents: the tricolour restored as the flag of a monarchy: the same flag the Restoration had banned.

1814 – 1830

Bourbon Restoration (with the Hundred Days)

MonarchyLouis XVIII

Who & why: Flags of the World says the white flag was restored as the national flag in 1814, which it had not been under the Ancien Régime, and calls this a major political mistake because people had accepted the tricolour. Wikipedia says the tricolour was replaced by the pre-revolutionary white naval flag in 1815–1830. In the Hundred Days Napoléon landed on 1 March 1815, had the Elba ensign lowered and the tricolour hoisted, and a decree of 9 March 1815 ordered the tricolour displayed on town halls; he abdicated for the second time on 22 June 1815, and the tricolour was banned again until the revolution of July 1830.

What it represents: a plain white flag standing for the Bourbon monarchy, with the tricolour carried by its opponents.

1804 – 1814

First Empire

EmpireNapoléon I

Who & why: Napoléon Bonaparte was proclaimed Emperor of the French on 2 December 1804, and Flags of the World says the national flag during the First Empire was the blue-white-red tricolour. The eagle and the bee were imperial emblems rather than the national flag; the same page says Napoléon had no personal standard before Elba, and that the eagle-on-thunderbolt standard sometimes shown is alleged and rests on a cigarette card.

What it represents: the tricolour carried over from the Revolution into the empire, with the imperial emblems beside it.

1789 – 1804

Revolution & First Republic

Revolution & Republic

Who & why: in July 1789 the Paris militia, formed on 13 July, wore blue and red and white was added to make the tricolour cockade, which was part of the National Guard uniform from 27 July (Wikipedia); Flags of the World says the cockade existed by 12 July and that Lafayette formalised it without inventing it. In 1790 the navy's jack and ensign were the first vertical tricolours, with red at the hoist; a decree of 24 October 1790, made law on 31 October, set three vertical bands in red-white-blue order. A decree of 26 June 1793 ordered tricolour flags on Paris public buildings, and the decree of 15 February 1794 (27 Pluviôse, Year II) reversed the order to blue at the hoist, the design in use today.

What it represents: the flag the Revolution made from a cockade, in its present order from 1794; the earlier red-hoist form of 1790 is not the one drawn.

1589 – 1789

Bourbon Kingdom

Kingdom

Who & why: Wikipedia says the House of Bourbon ruled France in 1589–1792 and 1815–1848 and that the white Bourbon banner was used. Flags of the World says white was the royal and military colour before 1789, that the King's vessels carried plain white flags (Pierre Gay), and that a decree of 3 March 1781 prescribed the white flag on French consulates in the Levant, the first reported use of a white flag on land.

What it represents: a white flag for the king and his ships, not yet a national flag: Flags of the World says the white flag was made the national flag only in 1814.

987 – 1589

Capetian & Valois Kingdom

Kingdom

Who & why: Wikipedia says the red oriflamme, the banner of Saint Denis, was the royal banner under the Capetians; that the Valois arms of 1328 were blue with gold fleurs-de-lis bordered in red; that Charles V changed the scattering of lilies to three about 1376; that the blue field became the common standard of French armies in the Hundred Years' War; and that Joan of Arc's standard led to the prominent use of white. Flags of the World says the lilies first appear on Louis VIII's seal and on Philip II's banners, that Charles V reduced them to three in 1365, and that in 1422 Charles VII adopted a white cross as his emblem and a white flag as his banner.

What it represents: the blue banner of gold lilies that stands for the kings of this long period; the oriflamme before it and the white flag after it are not drawn. The dates for the reduction to three lilies differ (1365 or about 1376).
