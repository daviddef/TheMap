Flags of Saudi Arabia

From more than a millennium of Thamudic tribes, the Lihyanite kingdom, and Nabataean traders carving the monumental tombs of Hegra, through the birthplace of Islam in 7th-century Mecca and Medina, centuries of fragmented emirates and Ottoman suzerainty over parts of the peninsula, the 18th-century religious-political alliance that produced the First Saudi State, its destruction and a Second Saudi State's rise and fall, to Abdulaziz ibn Saud's reconquest and the 1932 unification — one family's green banner, carrying the shahada and later a sword, running through the more recent half of it.

The shahada (the Islamic declaration of faith) has marked Saudi banners since the 18th-century Wahhabi movement; the sword beneath it, a later addition marking Ibn Saud's own reconquest, has stayed distinctive and unchanged in spirit even as the kingdom itself was built, lost, and rebuilt twice over.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Saudi Arabia, c. 1000 BCE–present, eight political entities. Part of a larger atlas — see the map and linked pages at the bottom.

One interior, eight flags

Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (standardized)

Kingdom, independentFaisalSunni Islam (Wahhabi, established)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: foreign labor migration (1970s–)
Ruling house:House of Saud

Who & why: a 1973 royal decree fixes the flag's precise proportions, calligraphy, and sword design in law, formalizing a banner the family had already flown for decades. The kingdom becomes a founding OPEC member, the world's largest oil exporter through the later 20th century, and host to Islam's two holiest cities, Mecca and Medina — a role its rulers style themselves "Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques" to reflect from 1986 onward.

What it represents: the same green field, shahada, and sword carried since the reconquest era, now specified precisely enough that the flag is never lowered to half-mast and never printed on disposable items, out of respect for the religious text it bears.

1932 – 1973

Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (unification)

Kingdom, independentAbdulaziz ibn SaudSunni Islam (Wahhabi, established)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: American Aramco oil workers (from 1938)
Ruling house:House of Saud

Who & why: Abdulaziz ibn Saud, having reconquered Riyadh in 1902 and steadily absorbed Nejd, Hejaz, and Asir, proclaims the unified Kingdom of Saudi Arabia on 23 September 1932 — still celebrated as the kingdom's national day. Oil is discovered at Dammam in 1938, transforming a poor desert kingdom into one of the world's most consequential energy producers within a generation.

What it represents: the green shahada-and-sword banner is the flag of the unified state. Wikipedia says the flag was not standardized before 15 March 1973, and that by 1938 it had essentially its present form, with a more curved sword and larger sword and shahada; Flags of the World says the basic design was in use by 1932 and the current version was adopted on 15 March 1973 (Cabinet Decision 101), with 1946 given by one author for the older sword pattern.

1926 – 1932

Kingdom of Hejaz and Nejd

Kingdom, independentAbdulaziz ibn SaudSunni Islam (Wahhabi, established)
Ruling house:House of Saud

Who & why: Ibn Saud captures Mecca and Medina from the Hashemite Kingdom of Hejaz in 1924–25, and is proclaimed King of Hejaz in January 1926 while keeping his older title as Sultan of Nejd — the two realms administered under one crown but not yet formally merged into a single kingdom, a transitional arrangement that lasts six years.

What it represents: Saudi sources describe the flag of the Kingdom of Hejaz and Nejd (1926-1932) as green with the Shahada in white in the middle and no sword (Al Majalla adds a white border); a sword was added beneath the Shahada from 1932. Details of the border and script are not established, and Flags of the World calls the flag alterations after 1925 unknown.

Emirate / Sultanate of Nejd (Al Saud reconquest)

Emirate / SultanateAbdulaziz ibn SaudSunni Islam (Wahhabi, established)
Ruling house:House of Saud

Who & why: the young Abdulaziz ibn Saud, exiled in Kuwait since the Rashidis drove his family from Riyadh, retakes the city in a night raid in January 1902, beginning the third and final Al Saud restoration. He formally upgrades his title from Emir to Sultan of Nejd in 1921 as his territory expands across central Arabia.

What it represents: Wikipedia says an Arabic inscription different from the shahada was used in 1902-1921 (text not given), and that the sword was added in 1921 by Abdulaziz Al-Saud, not at the 1902 reconquest; the Al Rashid emirate of Jabal Shammar opposed the Saudis until its defeat in 1921. The sources read do not link any flag to the retaking of Riyadh.

Fragmented Najd: Ottoman-Egyptian occupation, Second Saudi State, Rashidi Ha'il

Fragmented / contestedTurki bin AbdullahSunni Islam (Wahhabi, per the Second Saudi State)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Ottoman-Egyptian forces (1818)
Ruling house:House of SaudHouse of Rashid

Who & why: an Ottoman-Egyptian army destroys Diriyah in 1818, ending the First Saudi State. The Al Saud rebuild a smaller Second Saudi State around Riyadh from 1824, but it fractures under a succession dispute and finally falls to the rival Al Rashid emirate of Ha'il in 1891, which dominates Najd until Ibn Saud's 1902 reconquest. One honest bridging entry rather than three invented sub-flags, since no single banner can be attributed with confidence across this fractured, contested 84-year span.

What it represents: the sources read do not cover the Second Saudi State or the Ottoman-Egyptian occupation of Najd. Wikipedia says the Al Rashid emirate of Jabal Shammar opposed the Saudis until 1921 and that followers of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab used the shahada on flags from the 18th century; it does not say a Saudi green shahada banner persisted in this span. 1744 – 1818

First Saudi State (Emirate of Diriyah)

EmirateMuhammad ibn SaudSunni Islam (Wahhabi, founded here)
Ruling house:House of Saud

Who & why: in 1744 the ruler of the small oasis town of Diriyah, Muhammad ibn Saud, allies with the religious reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, combining political and religious authority in a pact that founds the Al Saud dynasty's first state. It expands across most of the Arabian interior by the early 1800s, including a 1803 capture of Mecca, before an Ottoman-Egyptian invasion crushes it in 1818.

What it represents: a plain green banner with the shahada, no sword yet: Saudipedia (citing the Saudi Press Agency) says the First Saudi State's flag was green, made of silk and wool, with the declaration of faith in the centre, and dates the state to 1727; Wikipedia and Flags of the World both tie the shahada flag to the followers of Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab in the 18th century.

Caliphal and emirate-era Arabia (Hejaz and Najd)

Fragmented / caliphal suzeraintyAbu BakrIslam (Sunni predominant)

Who & why: the Hejaz, home to Mecca and Medina, is the birthplace of Islam in the early 7th century and the seat of the earliest caliphates before authority shifts to Damascus and then Baghdad; the wider Arabian interior, including Najd, remains a patchwork of local emirates and tribal rule for most of this span, with the Ottomans asserting suzerainty over parts of the peninsula's edges from the 16th century onward without ever governing the deep interior directly.

What it represents: no flag in the modern sense exists across this millennium-long span; various caliphates and local dynasties used banners and seals rather than fixed territorial flags, and none can be attributed to this specific territory with confidence. Ancient Thamudic, Lihyanite & Nabataean Arabia

Fragmented / tribal kingdoms & caravan citiesNo single ruler documentedPre-Islamic Arabian polytheism

Who & why: more than a millennium before Islam, the northwestern Arabian Peninsula is already home to literate, city-building societies documented in inscriptions and monumental architecture: Thamudic tribal groups, attested from the early 1st millennium BCE in rock inscriptions across the Hejaz; the Lihyanite kingdom, centered on the oasis of Dedan (modern al-'Ula) from roughly the 6th century BCE; and the Nabataean kingdom, whose traders controlled the incense and spice routes linking Arabia to the Mediterranean and who, from around the 1st century BCE, carved the monumental tombs of Hegra (Mada'in Salih) — the largest Nabataean site south of Petra itself, and Saudi Arabia's first UNESCO World Heritage-listed site. Rome annexes the Nabataean kingdom outright in 106 CE, and the wider Hejaz and Najd remain a patchwork of tribes and caravan cities until the rise of Islam in the early 7th century.

What it represents: no flag in the modern territorial sense survives from any of these societies; Nabataean and Lihyanite authority was expressed through royal inscriptions, coinage, and monumental tomb facades rather than a woven banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs alongside the height of the Assyrian and Babylonian empires and the building of Babylon's own famed walls, the classical Greek and Hellenistic worlds from Athens to Alexander the Great, the Roman Republic's rise into empire and its 106 CE annexation of the Nabataean kingdom itself, and, at this era's close, the birth of Islam in Mecca in the early 7th century — a span long enough to run from the Iron Age Near East to the eve of the Islamic conquests.
