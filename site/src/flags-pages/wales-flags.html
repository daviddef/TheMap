Flags of Wales

Long before any dragon banner, Iron Age Celtic tribes — the Ordovices, Silures, Demetae, and Deceangli — held these hills, and Rome spent decades subduing them before garrisoning the land for over three centuries. Y Ddraig Goch, the Red Dragon, is one of the oldest national symbols in Europe — claimed in legend for the Arthurian era and attested in writing by the 9th century — yet it took until 1959 for Wales to receive an official national flag of its own. In between lie four centuries of independent native princedoms, Edward I's conquest, and four hundred years in which Wales, legally absorbed into England, had no separate flag status at all.

This page tracks both threads: the dragon's long informal and heraldic life, and the hard constitutional facts of conquest, incorporation, and eventual recognition that determined which flag actually had legal standing at any given point.

Scope of this page: the lands of present-day Wales, c. 800 BCE–present, six political entities. Medieval Wales was never a single unified kingdom in the way Scotland was — Gwynedd, Powys, Deheubarth, and others ruled separately and often rivaled one another; the banner shown for the independent era is Gwynedd's own device under Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the strongest and last independent Welsh polity, labeled here as an informed reconstruction representing that whole fractious era rather than a single pan-Welsh flag that never existed. No internal governing split exists within Wales's own borders at any point covered here — and, as a sub-national entity within England/the UK for most of its history rather than a fully independent state, Wales doesn't call for the same kind of internal-partition map as a country like Vietnam or Yemen. Y Ddraig Goch — red dragon on green-and-white
1959 – present

Wales (national flag officially recognized)

Part of the United KingdomElizabeth IINo established church (disestablished 1920)

Who & why: Wales remains constitutionally part of the United Kingdom throughout this era, but on 23 February 1959 a royal warrant formally recognizes Y Ddraig Goch on a green-and-white field as the official national flag of Wales — the first time Wales has had a legally recognized flag of its own since incorporation into England over four centuries earlier. The Government of Wales Acts (1998, 2006) later establish the Welsh Assembly, renamed the Senedd (Welsh Parliament) in 2020, giving Wales its own devolved legislature for the first time in its history.

What it represents: the red dragon, drawn from centuries of Welsh tradition (Historia Brittonum c. 828; Henry VII's standard of 1485; the 1807 Royal Badge), set on the green-and-white field of the Tudor livery, which Wikipedia says was added to the flag later; a 1953 badge-on-white flag was used by government until the red dragon on green and white was granted official status in 1959 (Flags of the World; Wikipedia, citing a 1959 Hansard answer).

Wales (fully incorporated into England / Great Britain / the UK)

Part of the Kingdom of England / UKHenry VIIIChurch of England (until 1920 disestablishment)
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Irish, Italian & Spanish workers (1840s–)Emigration: Patagonia (Y Wladfa, 1865) & the United States

Who & why: Henry VIII's Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 go further than Edward I's conquest ever did: Wales is fully incorporated into the Kingdom of England, given English common law, English-style counties and sheriffs, and its own seats in the Westminster Parliament for the first time — a permanent administrative and legal merger, not a personal union. Wales has no separate legal or flag status for the whole of this span: it simply flies whatever flag England, then Great Britain (from 1707), then the United Kingdom (from 1801) flies. The dragon survives only informally — in heraldry, regimental badges, and popular symbolism — with no official civic standing.

What it represents: the flag of the union Wales belonged to. The Flag Institute dates the first Union Flag to 1606 (reserved to royal ships from 1634), the continuation of that design after the Treaty of Union of 1707 and the present design to 1801, and Store norske leksikon says Wales has no part in it; the 1801 flag drawn here therefore fits only the years from 1801. Wikipedia and FOTW add a royal dragon badge of 1807 and a badge flag of 1953.

1283 – 1536

Wales (English-controlled principality, no separate flag)

Part of the Kingdom of England / UKEdward IRoman Catholic
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Kingdom of England (1282–83)

Who & why: Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, Prince of Wales, is killed near Builth in December 1282, and by 1283 Edward I has completed his conquest, formalized the following year with the 1284 Statute of Rhuddlan, which annexes the remaining independent Welsh territories to the English crown and ringed them with a chain of great castles (Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech, Beaumaris). In 1301 Edward's own son, the future Edward II, is created the first English Prince of Wales, a title every heir to the throne has carried since. Wales is administered directly under England's crown, with no separate flag of its own during this span, though the dragon persists as a potent symbol — Henry Tudor, himself of Welsh descent, marches under a red dragon standard at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485, when he takes the English throne.

What it represents: The sources read show Wales as a principality within England after 1284, with Welsh banners surfacing in revolt (Glyndwr's golden dragon in 1401) and in Henry Tudor's red dragon standard in 1485. They do not say that St George's cross was flown over Wales in these centuries.

c. 850 – 1283

Independent Welsh kingdoms (Gwynedd, Powys, Deheubarth)

Independent native kingdomsRhodri MawrRoman Catholic (Celtic tradition)
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Norman Marcher lords (parts of Wales, from 1070s)
Ruling house:House of Aberffraw (Gwynedd)House of Dinefwr (Deheubarth)House of Mathrafal (Powys)

Who & why: medieval Wales is never a single unified kingdom but a shifting patchwork of native princedoms, chiefly Gwynedd in the north, Powys in mid-Wales, and Deheubarth in the south, each ruling in its own right and often at war with one another as much as with England. Gwynedd becomes the dominant power by the 13th century: Llywelyn ap Gruffudd is recognized by England as Prince of Wales under the 1267 Treaty of Montgomery, ruling most of native Wales until Edward I's conquest ends it in 1282–83.

What it represents: Store norske leksikon says the princes of Gwynedd bore a quartered gold-and-red shield with four lions, which Llywelyn the Great took over from his father Iorwerth, and Adam of Usk's chronicle has Owain Glyndwr's golden dragon on white at Caernarfon in 1401. The sources read give no banner for c. 850-1200 and no dragon banner before 1401, so the lions-and-dragon design drawn here is a reconstruction that none of them describes.

c. 400 – 850

Sub-Roman and early medieval Welsh kingdoms

Fragmented kingdomsCunedda ap EdernCeltic Christianity
Migration & conquest:Immigration: Brittonic refugees from the east

Who & why: after Roman withdrawal from Britain in the early 5th century, the Brittonic peoples of what becomes Wales organize into a number of small, competing kingdoms — Gwynedd, Powys, Dyfed, and others — defending against Anglo-Saxon expansion to the east (Offa's Dyke, built by the Mercian king Offa in the later 8th century, marks the rough frontier that emerges). This is also the era the later Arthurian and dragon legends claim as their setting, including the Historia Brittonum's own account, though that text itself dates only from around 830.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this pre-heraldic era; the dragon symbolism later attached to this period (Arthur, Cadwaladr) is retrospective legend recorded centuries after the fact, not a contemporary attested banner. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

c. 800 BCE – c. 400 CE

Iron Age Britons & Roman Wales

Fragmented / tribal, then Roman provinceGnaeus Julius AgricolaCeltic polytheism, then Roman religion & early Christianity
Migration & conquest:Conquered by: Rome (completed c. 77–78 CE)

Who & why: centuries before any Welsh kingdom existed, Iron Age Celtic Britons organized into distinct tribes — the Ordovices in the north-center, the Silures in the south-east, the Demetae in the south-west, and the Deceangli in the north-east — each defending its own hillforts. Rome's conquest of these tribes took decades of hard fighting, including the destruction of the druids' sanctuary on Anglesey in 60/61 CE, and was only completed under the governor Agricola around 77–78 CE. Roman Wales that followed was garrisoned rather than urbanized in most of the interior, anchored by the legionary fortress at Caerleon (Isca) and the fort at Segontium near modern Caernarfon, with a rare documented Roman gold mine worked at Dolaucothi. Roman withdrawal from Britain in the early 5th century ends this era and opens the sub-Roman kingdoms that follow.

What it represents: no securely documented flag survives from this era — Celtic tribes carried their own war standards, mostly unrecorded, and Rome's own military standards represented the legions and the emperor, not a Welsh polity. Shown here honestly blank rather than invented.

World context: this span runs from roughly the founding of Rome itself (traditionally 753 BCE) through the Roman Republic's rise and fall, Julius Caesar's first landings in Britain (55–54 BCE), and the construction of the Colosseum (70–80 CE) and Hadrian's Wall (begun 122 CE) far to the north — the same imperial system whose legions built the forts still visible at Caerleon and Segontium.
